Backpropagation: Derivation and Layerwise Updates

Backpropagation applies the chain rule to assign loss gradients to every MLP weight and bias.

Big picture

An MLP can make useful predictions through hidden layers, but a hidden unit has no target of its own. Backpropagation solves that credit-assignment problem: first compute the prediction with fixed parameters, then use the chain rule to move loss information from outputs back through the network. The result is a gradient for every connection weight and bias. A separate optimizer or update schedule applies the changes.

For the wider training loop and optimizer choices, see multilayer network optimization. Compare the multilayer chain rule with LMS and perceptron learning.

Forward signals and notation

For training pattern n, let layer 0 be the input and layer L the output. For layer ℓ, unit j, and preceding-layer unit i, the field is a weighted sum plus bias; activation follows it:

vj(ℓ)(n)=∑iwji(ℓ)(n)ai(ℓ−1)(n)+bj(ℓ)(n),aj(ℓ)(n)=φj(ℓ)(vj(ℓ)(n))

The weight indexed by the current layer and unit j, then preceding-layer unit i, connects that previous unit to the current one. The bias is added before activation. A superscript names a layer; subscripts name units. The final activation is the prediction. For output set O, the source objective is:

ek(n)=dk(n)−yk(n),ξ(n)=12∑k∈𝒪ek2(n)

This is a per-pattern half-summed squared error. The sum is over outputs for one pattern, not over training patterns. In the worked example there is one output, so it reduces to one half times one squared residual.

Chain rule and the source delta sign

For one output neuron, differentiate along the path from its incoming weight to its field, activation, residual, and loss. The chain-rule factors are the residual derivative, negative residual-to-output derivative, activation derivative, and incoming activation. Therefore:

∂ξ(n)∂wji(n)=−ej(n)φj′(vj(n))ai(n)

The supplement defines a neuron's local gradient as the negative derivative with respect to its field. With that sign choice, a positive delta gives an additive parameter increment:

δj(n)=−∂ξ(n)∂vj(n),Δwji(n)=−η∂ξ(n)∂wji(n)=ηδj(n)ai(n)

For an output unit the target is arbitrary: use desired output minus prediction, then multiply by the output activation derivative. A shortcut that replaces the residual with one minus the prediction is valid only when the target is specifically one.

Hidden credit assignment

A hidden unit has no desired response, so it cannot form a target-minus-output residual of its own. Its credit is the sum of the downstream deltas, weighted by the connections into each next-layer unit, then scaled by its local activation derivative:

δj(ℓ)(n)=φj′(ℓ)(vj(ℓ)(n))∑k∈next(j)δk(ℓ+1)(n)wkj(ℓ+1)(n)

The sum is over next-layer units connected to j. Use the old downstream weights from the same forward-pass snapshot: changing an output weight before calculating a hidden delta changes the derivative being propagated. In matrix notation the next-layer weights are transposed in this backward dependency.

Weight and bias gradients

Each incoming connection uses its own source activation. A bias acts like a connection from a constant-one input:

∂ξ(n)∂wji(ℓ)(n)=−δj(ℓ)(n)ai(ℓ−1)(n),∂ξ(n)∂bj(ℓ)(n)=−δj(ℓ)(n)
Δwji(ℓ)(n)=ηδj(ℓ)(n)ai(ℓ−1)(n),Δbj(ℓ)(n)=ηδj(ℓ)(n)

Compute every layer's deltas and gradients from one unchanged parameter snapshot, then apply the chosen update. A zero input gives zero gradient for that incoming weight on that pattern, but it does not freeze the weight on other patterns.

Activation derivatives

Backpropagation needs a derivative at each neuron's current field. The supplement gives logistic and scaled hyperbolic-tangent examples. For the logistic, α is a positive slope parameter and y is its output:

φ(v)=11+exp(−αv),φ′(v)=αexp(−αv)[1+exp(−αv)]2=αy(1−y),α>0

For the common unit-slope case, α is one. For a scaled tanh with output scale A and input scale B:

φ(v)=Atanh(Bv),φ′(v)=ABsech2(Bv)=AB[1−tanh2(Bv)]=BA(A−y)(A+y)

The final form follows from the scaled-tanh output and assumes A is nonzero. Use the derivative belonging to the chosen activation and evaluate it at that neuron's field. Saturated sigmoid or tanh units have small derivatives, so repeated products can weaken earlier-layer gradients.

One-pattern versus dataset updates

The delta rule computes one pattern's gradient. In an online schedule, apply that example's complete update before processing the next; the viewer uses one observation per epoch, so each epoch contains one online update. In a batch schedule, calculate per-example gradients using the selected batch, aggregate them, and then apply one optimizer step. A sum and a mean differ by the batch count, so name any normalization explicitly. An epoch means one full pass through the dataset; it is not automatically one update. Backpropagation supplies derivatives; the optimizer schedule chooses when and how to apply them.

Stopping and convergence cautions

The lecture does not claim a general convergence proof for backpropagation. It suggests stopping when optimization is sufficient, for example when the absolute change in average squared error per epoch becomes small. That is a practical stopping signal, not proof of a global minimum. Small derivatives through many layers can contribute to vanishing gradients; large products can contribute to exploding gradients. Two improving steps on one constructed example say only that those displayed updates lowered its loss.

Common mistakes

  • Reversing the residual sign without changing the delta and update signs. Here the residual is desired output minus prediction and the local delta is the negative loss derivative, giving an additive learning-rate-times-delta-times-input update.
  • Giving hidden units artificial targets. Hidden deltas come from downstream weighted deltas and each hidden unit's local derivative.
  • Using a just-updated output weight in the hidden-delta calculation. Calculate all deltas from one old-parameter snapshot.
  • Forgetting biases or updating only the output layer. Each trainable layer has weight and bias gradients.
  • Treating the target as a forward input. It enters the loss after the prediction.
  • Treating a gradient formula as the optimizer schedule. Online, mini-batch, or batch describes when per-example gradients are combined and applied.
  • Assuming two lower-loss steps establish convergence or exact target classification.

Exam focus

  • Forward pass: affine field with bias, then activation, layer by layer while parameters stay fixed.
  • Per-pattern objective: half the sum of squared output residuals; residual is desired output minus prediction.
  • Source sign convention: local delta is the negative loss derivative with respect to the field. Output delta is residual times activation derivative.
  • Hidden delta: local activation derivative times the sum of next-layer deltas weighted by old outgoing connections.
  • Every connection gradient is negative delta times its incoming activation; every bias gradient is negative delta. The delta-rule increments add learning-rate times delta times activation, and learning-rate times delta for a bias.
  • Backpropagation computes gradients. The selected optimizer schedule applies them. No general convergence guarantee is claimed.

Practice questions

1. For target 0.2 and prediction 0.7, what residual enters the output delta?

Check your answer

e=d−y=0.2−0.7=−0.5. Do not replace the target by one.

2. Why is there no target-minus-output residual for a hidden unit?

Check your answer

No desired response is assigned to its internal representation. Its delta is computed from the next layer's deltas and outgoing weights, then multiplied by its own activation derivative.

3. If a hidden unit feeds two next-layer units, which downstream weights enter its delta?

Check your answer

Both outgoing weights from that hidden unit, each multiplied by the delta of its corresponding next-layer unit; sum these products and multiply by the hidden unit's local derivative.

4. What is the gradient of a unit's bias under the source delta convention?

Check your answer

∂ξ/∂bj=−δj. Its positive delta-rule bias increment is Δbj=ηδj.

5. When may the output-delta shortcut using one minus the prediction be used?

Check your answer

Only for an output target d=1. In general use δ=(d−y)φ′(v).

6. Do all patterns in a batch have to be updated one at a time?

Check your answer

No. In a batch schedule, compute the selected patterns' gradients, aggregate them using a stated sum or mean, and then apply one update. Online scheduling applies each pattern's update immediately.

7. Does a decreasing loss over two updates prove backpropagation converges?

Check your answer

No. It checks only those updates and that objective. The lecture cautions that general convergence cannot be shown; a small epoch-to-epoch average-error change is one practical stopping criterion.
