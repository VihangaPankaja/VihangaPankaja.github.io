Self-Supervised Learning for Text

Follow how text objectives evolve from static word statistics to contextual prediction, replacement detection, and sequence denoising.

Big picture

Text supplies its own training signal because a sequence already contains the words a model can be asked to predict. The objective determines the information boundary: a fixed word predicts neighbours, a forward language model sees a prefix, a masked encoder sees context on both sides, a replacement detector labels every observed position, and a denoising decoder generates corrupted text back into a target sequence.

The progression here is from static distributional vectors to contextual language models, then to alternative ways of constructing token or sequence targets. The central comparison is always: what is visible, what is selected, what is predicted, and in what direction can information flow? For Transformer architecture details, continue to Machine Translation and Neural Transformers. The sequence-model prerequisites are in Sequence Models; vision SSL is introduced in Self-Supervised Learning for Vision.

Why text provides a training target

Tokens form a discrete sequence, so we can withhold a token or predict the next one and use the original text to create a target automatically. Under the distributional hypothesis, words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. Large text collections provide these prediction examples without first asking people to annotate every word.

The target-generation rule matters more than the model name. Static embeddings use local neighbours or corpus-wide co-occurrences. Language models predict tokens in one or both directions. Denoising models map a corrupted input to a target sequence.

Static representations from context statistics

word2vec: skip-gram and CBOW

With a local context window, skip-gram uses a centre word to predict surrounding words. CBOW reverses those roles and predicts the centre word from its neighbours. Both are trained from naturally occurring word windows; no manually authored word labels are needed. The lecture notes that the resulting vectors can capture analogies such as king−man+woman lying near “queen”.

Representation boundary. Standard word2vec assigns a fixed vector to a word type. The vector for “bank” is the same in “river bank” and “bank account”; the surrounding sentence does not change that stored word vector.

GloVe: global word–word co-occurrence

GloVe first counts how often word pairs co-occur across a corpus, then learns vectors whose dot products match the logarithm of those counts. It draws on global count statistics while learning compact vectors. This is a different learning signal from word2vec’s local skip-gram or CBOW prediction, even though both produce static word vectors.

Information boundary. GloVe’s signal is a corpus-wide word-pair count relationship. It does not predict a token from only the left prefix of one running sentence.

GloVe learns word vectors so their dot products match the logarithms of co-occurrence counts. The training signal is the count relationship across the corpus; the objective is not “predict the next word.”

Context boundary: global word-pair statistics, rather than a one-way token prefix. The resulting vector is still static for each word type.

Context-sensitive language representations

ELMo: separate forward and backward LSTMs

ELMo trains two language models: a forward LSTM predicts the next word, and a backward LSTM predicts the previous word while reading in reverse. A token representation combines learned contributions from the LSTM layers and both directions, so the same spelling can have different representations in different sentences. “Bank” in a river sentence and “bank” in an account sentence are not forced to share one contextual vector.

The Transformer later made self-attention a practical alternative to recurrent processing for much larger pre-trained models. Transformer layer details belong in the linked machine-translation topic.

Check: word2vec/GloVe assign a static type vector; ELMo’s representation for a token depends on its sentence.

Predicting from a gap or a prefix

BERT: masked language modelling and NSP

BERT is a Transformer encoder trained to predict selected original tokens using context from both sides. The lecture’s example is preserved exactly: input [CLS] my dog [MASK] hairy, target is. The selected positions are 15% of the sequence; conditional on a position being selected, the input replacement is [MASK] 80% of the time, random 10%, and unchanged 10%. Thus “80%” is applied to selected positions, not to the whole sequence. Across a 100-token sequence, the expected selected count is 15; the expected [MASK] share is 12 tokens, while the other selected-token cases average 1.5 random and 1.5 unchanged. These are expectations across examples, so an individual sequence uses integer positions.

BERT also has a separate next sentence prediction (NSP) task. The source describes it as supervised. More precisely, the original paper creates binary labels from corpus construction: segment B follows segment A for IsNext, or B is sampled randomly from the corpus for NotNext. This is a supervised-style classification target generated from text, not necessarily an externally human-annotated label. Original BERT paper, §3.1.

Keep NSP as a separate objective

Next sentence prediction (NSP) asks whether a text segment B is the actual next corpus segment after A. In the original BERT procedure, half the pairs use the actual next segment and half use a random corpus segment; this construction supplies the IsNext/NotNext label. It is a supervised-style binary objective, but it does not require an external human annotation. BERT paper, §3.1.

Boundary: MLM predicts selected token identities from both-side context; NSP predicts a relation between two constructed segments.

GPT: causal next-token prediction

GPT uses a Transformer decoder and predicts the next token using only earlier tokens. For the source sequence “The cat sat on the mat”, the target mat is predicted from the prefix The cat sat on the. A causal attention mask blocks every later token from the current prediction. The language-model objective is the sum of next-token log likelihoods:

L=∑tlogP(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)

Because generation repeats the same prefix-to-next-token step, the pre-training objective naturally supports text generation. The original lecture also describes pre-training followed by fine-tuning on labelled tasks.

Improving or changing the prediction signal

RoBERTa: a stronger BERT recipe

RoBERTa keeps masked language modelling and removes NSP. Its recipe uses dynamic masking, larger batches, and substantially more text. Dynamic masking lets the same source text appear with different selected positions on different presentations. The lecture’s point is that data and training choices can matter as much as a newly named objective; RoBERTa remains in the masked-LM family.

Separate recipe changes from objective changes

RoBERTa removes NSP and uses dynamic masking, larger batches, and more training text (about 160 GB in the lecture). The token objective remains MLM; the changed recipe demonstrates that data and training choices matter alongside the objective.

Check: RoBERTa is not the XLNet permutation objective or ELECTRA replacement detection.

XLNet: permute the factorization, not the sentence

XLNet predicts tokens under sampled permutations of the factorization order. For a given target, only tokens earlier in that chosen order may be used. Across permutations a target can receive information from either side in the original sentence, while the objective remains autoregressive and avoids BERT’s artificial [MASK] input token.

The distinction is easy to lose: XLNet does not scramble the observed sentence positions. The words and positional identities stay in original order; the permutation changes the attention/factorization mask. Original XLNet paper, §2.

Apply the permutation mask, not a text shuffle

The input words remain in their original positions; the attention/factorization mask controls which earlier-in-permutation tokens can inform the current target. XLNet maximizes likelihood over sampled factorization orders while retaining an autoregressive objective. XLNet paper, §2.

Check: the factorization order is 1, 4, 3, 2, 5; the observed text is still “the cat sat on mat”. There is no literal [MASK] token in this objective.

ELECTRA: replaced-token detection at every position

A small generator proposes plausible alternatives at selected positions. The discriminator then classifies each token in the resulting sequence as original or replaced, using context from both directions. Unlike MLM, its classification target is a binary identity comparison at every input position, rather than an original token identity at only a selected subset.

The label depends on comparing the discriminator input token with the original. If a generator sample happens to equal the original token, it is labelled original/real, not replaced. The generator is trained with masked-LM prediction; the ELECTRA discriminator learns replaced-token detection. Original ELECTRA paper, §2.

Corrupted input to sequence output

T5 and BART: related encoder–decoder objectives

Both methods use a bidirectional encoder and a left-to-right autoregressive decoder, but they ask the decoder to produce different targets.

T5 span corruption replaces random spans with sentinel tokens in the encoder input. The decoder target contains the missing spans associated with their sentinels. The target is therefore the removed content, not the whole source document.

BART denoising corrupts a document and trains the sequence-to-sequence model to rebuild the original document. The lecture lists token masking and deletion, text infilling, sentence permutation, and document rotation. For text infilling, a span can be replaced by a mask token; unlike T5’s sentinel-marked missing-span target, BART’s target remains the full original text. Its full decoder supports generation tasks such as summarisation. T5 paper; BART paper.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a word2vec or GloVe vector as sentence-dependent. These methods produce a static vector per word type; ELMo contextualizes a token using its sentence.
  • Collapsing word2vec and GloVe into the same objective. Skip-gram/CBOW predict local words; GloVe fits global co-occurrence statistics.
  • Calling BERT’s 80/10/10 split percentages of all tokens. First select 15%; then apply 80/10/10 conditionally to selected positions.
  • Treating BERT NSP as an externally human-labelled relation. The original paper constructs the binary label by pairing actual next segments with random corpus segments.
  • Letting GPT see any token to the right of the target. Its causal prefix is the complete allowed context.
  • Drawing XLNet as a physically shuffled sentence. Only the factorization/attention order is permuted; original token positions remain fixed.
  • Marking every selected ELECTRA position as replaced. A generator sample equal to the original token is labelled original/real.
  • Giving T5 and BART the same target. T5 predicts sentinel-delimited missing spans; BART reconstructs the complete original document.
  • Assuming “bidirectional context” means every part of every objective predicts from both sides. A seq2seq encoder reads both sides, but its decoder still generates left to right.

Exam focus

  • State each objective as input/corruption → selected position or target → allowed context → prediction.
  • Compare skip-gram (centre → context) with CBOW (context → centre), and distinguish both from GloVe’s global co-occurrence fitting.
  • Explain how ELMo combines forward and backward LSTM language models to make token representations context-sensitive.
  • Recall BERT’s exact source example, the selected-position 15% rate, conditional 80/10/10 corruption, and separate corpus-constructed NSP target.
  • Explain why GPT cannot use future target tokens and why that same boundary supports autoregressive generation.
  • Distinguish RoBERTa’s recipe changes, XLNet’s factorization permutation, and ELECTRA’s per-position binary detection.
  • State the target distinction for T5 and BART and the common encoder/decoder directions.
  • Reproduce the five objective-family comparison rows and their typical strengths.

Practice questions

In skip-gram and CBOW, which side supplies the prediction target?

Check your answer

Skip-gram uses a centre word to predict surrounding context words. CBOW uses surrounding words to predict the centre.

Why does the vector for “bank” differ between ELMo’s “river bank” and “bank account” contexts?

Check your answer

ELMo combines forward and backward LSTM context, so the token representation depends on its sentence. A static word2vec/GloVe type vector does not.

For BERT’s source example [CLS] my dog [MASK] hairy, what is the target, and which directions may supply context?

Check your answer

The target is is. The encoder uses context on both sides: “my dog” to the left and “hairy” to the right.

A 100-token BERT sequence selects 15% of positions. What does the 80% replacement rate apply to?

Check your answer

It applies only to the selected prediction positions. The expected selected count is 15 and the expected [MASK] count is 12; the other selected cases are random or unchanged. Individual sequence counts are integers.

How is BERT’s NSP target produced in the original paper?

Check your answer

From pair construction: B is the actual next corpus segment for IsNext or a random corpus segment for NotNext. It is a supervised-style binary task but does not require external human annotation.

Which words are visible when GPT predicts mat in “The cat sat on the mat”?

Check your answer

Only the prefix The cat sat on the. The target and any later tokens are not visible to that prediction.

In the XLNet illustration, what changes when factorization order is 1 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 5?

Check your answer

The attention/factorization mask changes. The observed sentence remains in original position order; the target at position 3 can use positions 1 and 4 because those precede it in this sampled factorization.

An ELECTRA generator replaces “cat” with “dog” but samples “sat” for an original “sat”. What are the two labels?

Check your answer

dog is replaced. The sampled sat matches the original and is labelled original/real.

What is the decoder target for a one-span T5 corruption, and how does it differ from BART?

Check your answer

T5 outputs the missing span with its sentinel markers. BART outputs the complete original document, even when the encoder input was corrupted by a mask or another listed noise operation.

Name the five objective families compared in the lecture.

Check your answer

Causal LM; masked LM; permutation LM; replaced-token detection; denoising seq2seq.
