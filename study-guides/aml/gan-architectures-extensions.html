GAN Architectures, Conditional Generation, and Extensions

How GAN designs change when generation must preserve image structure, encode useful variation, follow a condition, or translate between domains.

Big picture

The first GAN topic establishes the shared adversarial objective and the roles of generator and discriminator. This topic asks what changes when the generated output should have structure, follow a requested condition, or be built across domains or resolutions. Each extension changes a specific part of the system: the image architecture, the discriminator’s score, information supplied to the generator, or the relationship between multiple generators and domains. A CNN overview is a useful prerequisite for the convolutional image architecture; the GAN objective and alternating roles are inherited from the GAN foundations topic.

DCGAN: make image structure part of the generator

Fully connected layers flatten an image’s spatial relationships. DCGAN replaces hidden fully connected layers with convolutional stages and grows a small feature map into an image using fractionally strided (transposed) convolutions. The pictured generator begins with a 100-value latent vector, projects and reshapes it, then doubles spatial width and height at each stride-2 stage. Hidden generator layers use batch normalization and ReLU; the output uses Tanh. The slide shows this generator path, not a full discriminator specification.

The feature depth narrows as spatial detail grows. Read the tensor labels as height × width × channels: 4×4×1024 becomes 8×8×512, then 16×16×256, 32×32×128, and finally a 64×64×3 image. Thus the RGB output contains 12,288 scalar values, while the sampled input has 100. The source’s broad statement elsewhere that noise is “typically higher-dimensional” than the sample conflicts with this example; dimensionality depends on the design. The figure labels 5×5 filters and stride 2 on the shown upsampling blocks.

Check the DCGAN shape expansion

Given: a 100-value z, the source diagram’s initial projection to 4×4×1024, four pictured spatial doublings, and final three channels.

4×4×1024→8×8×512→16×16×256→32×32×128→64×64×3

Intermediate check: each stride-2 upsampling stage doubles both spatial dimensions. Channel counts follow the labels in the source. The final RGB tensor has 64·64·3=12,288 scalar values. The label 64 is the final spatial width/height; it is not an intermediate channel count.

The lecture shows generated bedroom images and anime-face grids at labelled training-update counts. These are historical illustrative panels, not new experiments or quantitative evidence of a universal training trajectory. The source also illustrates latent-vector arithmetic: “man with glasses − man without glasses + woman without glasses” is shown as “woman with glasses.” The companion pixel-space operation visibly fails. This suggests that learned representations can support useful directions in some examples, but it does not establish that every GAN has disentangled semantic coordinates or that all attributes vary linearly.

Interpret the latent direction example

Let each phrase denote the latent representation associated with the pictured face. The slide’s example is:

vman+glasses−vman−glasses+vwoman−glasses≈vwoman+glasses

The arithmetic is illustrated in latent space, where the visual factors appear to combine coherently. Applying the same pixel arithmetic to the three displayed images produces a distorted face in the next slide. The source presents an example of a learned direction, not a guarantee that this relation holds for all images, attributes, or GANs.

Changing the discriminator’s output: probabilities, scores, and energies

The label-flipped slide changes which class is encoded as 1: real is 0 and generated is 1. The loss and desired outputs must flip together. The energy-based alternative changes the output meaning: reconstruction energy measures mismatch, rather than a real/fake probability.

Vflip(D,G)=𝔼x~pdata[log(1−D(x))]+𝔼z~pz[logD(G(z))]

In the probability convention above, D is trained toward 0 for real inputs and toward 1 for generated inputs. For EBGAN, write its discriminator output as energy E instead. Clarification of the margin objective: energy must be lower-bounded; nonnegative reconstruction error supplies that condition. An unconstrained signed score would let the real-data term decrease without limit. The original EBGAN formulation uses a positive margin and two separate losses, both minimized:

LDEB=𝔼x~pdata[E(x)]+𝔼z~pz[max(0,m−E(G(z)))],m>0,E(x)≥0
LGEB=𝔼z~pz[E(G(z))]

The discriminator lowers real energy and raises fake energy toward the margin. Once fake energy reaches the margin, its discriminator hinge term is zero; it need not keep rising. The generator still minimizes fake energy, even above the margin. In the lecture’s autoencoder version, mean-squared reconstruction error provides the nonnegative energy.

E(x)=MSE(Dec(Enc(x)),x)
Reason about EBGAN energy directions

Given: one real example x and one generated example G(z), both scored by the same nonnegative autoencoder energy.

  1. Minimize real reconstruction error.
  2. Raise fake energy while it lies below the positive margin; the discriminator hinge term is zero at or above the margin.
  3. Update G to reduce fake energy. Its separate objective continues to favor lower energy above the margin too.

Supplemental loss check: with margin m=1 and real energy E(x)=0.2, compare two fake energies:

E(G(z))=0.4:LD=0.2+max(0,1−0.4)=0.8,LG=0.4
E(G(z))=1.5:LD=0.2+max(0,1−1.5)=0.2,LG=1.5

These are illustrative loss values, not training measurements. Low energy means more real-like under this model; energy is not a probability.

InfoGAN: encourage information in a chosen latent code

An ordinary GAN gives the generator noise z. InfoGAN splits the input into unstructured noise z and a sampled code c whose prior is selected by the designer. The code is intended to capture some chosen variation; the lecture’s examples include digit identity, angle, and stroke thickness, while its face grid varies azimuth, elevation, lighting, and width. The discriminator’s feature trunk supports a real/fake head, and an auxiliary Q network predicts c from a generated sample. Mutual information rewards outputs that retain information about the sampled code.

I(c;X)=H(c)−H(c∣X)

The true posterior over codes given an image is unavailable, so InfoGAN uses Q to form a variational lower bound. Since the KL divergence between the true posterior and Q is nonnegative, the expected log probability under Q plus the code entropy is no greater than the mutual information. For a fixed code prior, H(c) is constant. Training maximizes the lower bound while the adversarial game still trains the discriminator’s real/fake objective.

LI=𝔼c~p(c),z~pz[logQ(c∣G(z,c))]+H(c)
minG,QmaxDV(D,G)−λLI(G,Q),λ>0

The auxiliary MI objective maximizes the bound with respect to both G and Q. The adversarial objective trains G and D in their usual opposing roles. In the pictured shared-trunk implementation, the common feature layers receive gradients from both objectives. The real/fake head receives adversarial gradients; the Q-specific head receives code-prediction gradients. The sampled code is the target for Q, not an externally supplied class annotation.

Follow one InfoGAN code sample

Given: draw z~pz and c~p(c). The generator receives both, making x~=G(z,c). Q predicts Q(c∣x~) from generated features.

  1. If Q assigns higher conditional likelihood to the sampled c, logQ(c∣x~) increases, improving that sample’s lower-bound contribution. Q is a probability mass for discrete codes or a density for continuous codes.
  2. If Q cannot recover c, the log-likelihood term is smaller, so maximizing LI favors generated samples that preserve information about c.
  3. The discriminator still learns real versus fake. The code prior entropy H(c) is constant only while the chosen prior stays fixed.

Check: Q estimates code from generated x; InfoGAN does not directly observe the true posterior and does not guarantee that codes align with human-interpretable factors.

Conditional generation: provide the condition to both networks

A conditional GAN adds side information c so a sampled output can be steered toward a requested class, text description, or source image. The condition enters both G and D: G receives (z,c), while D judges (image,c). Real training examples use their matching condition. For MNIST, the lecture shows one-hot class vectors and rows of generated examples for each digit. This makes the requested class explicit while leaving variation to the noise input.

x~=G(z,c),D(x,c)

For paired image-to-image translation, c is the source image and the target image supplies the corresponding real pair. The slide examples map semantic labels to street scenes or building facades, aerial views to maps, black-and-white images to color, day to night, and edges to product photos. The discriminator checks both realism and compatibility with the source condition. In text-to-image synthesis, c is a dense text embedding. A positive example is (real image, correct text); negatives include (real image, wrong text) and (fake image, correct text). This teaches the discriminator that a realistic image alone is insufficient when its text condition is wrong.

Classify the text-image pairs

For a description of a small bird with a pink breast and crown:

  • Positive: a real matching bird image paired with its correct description.
  • Negative: a real image paired with a different bird description.
  • Negative: a generated image paired with the intended description.

Each pair reaches D with both image and text embedding. The first negative checks condition matching; the second checks whether G’s output can fool D under that condition.

The face-aging example uses an encoder to estimate an initial latent code z₀ for an input face and age y₀. An identity-preserving optimization refines the code to z, improving reconstruction of the same face at its original age. The generator then receives z with a one-hot target age category, such as “60+,” to produce an aged output. The age rows shown are historical examples; the latent-code optimization is an inference procedure for an input, not a sequence of GAN training epochs.

Trace the face-aging inputs and outputs
  1. Input face x and source age y₀ enter the encoder; it estimates z₀.
  2. Generate an initial reconstruction conditioned on y₀.
  3. Identity-preserving optimization adjusts the latent code from z₀ to z* so the same identity is better reconstructed at the source age.
  4. Feed z* and a one-hot target-age code (for example “60+”) to G. The output is the target-age face.

Check: the target age changes the requested output condition; z* is refined to preserve the input identity. The slide illustrates the method and does not establish a general guarantee of identity preservation.

Multi-domain, multi-scale, and inference extensions

Some extensions alter the relationship between generators rather than only adding a condition. Coupled GAN (CoGAN) uses two domain-specific GAN paths driven by the same latent z. Selected generator layers and discriminator layers share weights; the slide says this encourages learning a joint multi-domain distribution from samples of the marginal distributions, without corresponding supervision. A shared latent code helps align variation across domains in the examples, but it does not provide paired training labels or guarantee identity correspondence.

LAPGAN builds a high-resolution image progressively. A base generator makes a coarse image from noise; each finer generator predicts a difference image conditioned on the current coarse image. The system upsamples the previous image, adds the predicted residual, and repeats. The source says each level is trained independently. Its diagram’s arrow layout can be read from fine to coarse visually, so follow the slide’s written generation order: base at level 3, then residual levels 2, 1, and 0.

LAPGAN generation: coarse base to fine residuals

InputsIndependent noise draws z3,z2,z1,z0Base generator G3 and residual generators G2,G1,G0Each level adds a residual to an upscaled image; models are trained independently.

1. Sample the coarsest base image at level 3

Start from noise z3 and the base generator G3. It produces the coarsest image I~3. No finer image has been generated yet.

I~3=G3(z3)

2. Upscale and add the level 2 residual

Upscale the previous image to the next level and provide it with fresh noise z2 to G2. This generator predicts a residual, not a complete replacement image. Add that residual elementwise to the upscaled image.

I~2=up(I~3)+h~2,h~2=G2(up(I~3),z2)

3. Repeat at level 1

Use the newly reconstructed level 2 image as context. G1 predicts a level 1 residual from that context and fresh z1; add it to the upscaled level 2 image.

I~1=up(I~2)+h~1,h~1=G1(up(I~2),z1)

4. Finish at level 0 and check the order

Finally, G0 predicts the finest residual from the upscaled level 1 image and fresh z0. The order is coarse base first, then residual additions 2, 1, 0. The lecture says the models at each level are trained independently.

I~0=up(I~1)+h~0,h~0=G0(up(I~1),z0)

Check: every stage adds a predicted difference to an upscaled prior image; none of the later generators replaces that image outright.

Adversarially Learned Inference (ALI) adds an encoder to the generator. The encoder samples z for a data image, creating a joint pair (x,z); the generator samples z from a prior and creates x̃, forming (x̃,z). A shared discriminator distinguishes these two joint distributions. The lecture reports that at ideal Nash equilibrium the joints, their marginals, and their conditionals match; it also reports image-reconstruction and downstream semi-supervised examples. Those are the source’s theoretical and historical claims, not newly reproduced outcomes.

q(x,z)=q(x)q(z∣x),p(x,z)=p(z)p(x∣z)
Keep the two ALI joint pairs intact
  1. Sample a data image x~q(x) and encode z~q(z∣x). Send the paired values (x,z) to D.
  2. Independently sample z~p(z) and generate x~~p(x∣z). Send the paired values (x̃,z) to the same D.
  3. D’s task is to distinguish encoder/data pairs from generator/prior pairs.

Check: each x is paired with the z produced from that same route. Do not pair a real x with an unrelated prior sample.

Image-conditioned outputs: super-resolution and unpaired translation

Super-resolution gives the generator a low-resolution image and asks it for a higher-resolution result. The slide compares the original, bicubic interpolation, SRResNet, and SRGAN outputs. It prints PSNR/SSIM pairs of 21.59 dB/0.6423, 23.44 dB/0.7777, and 20.34 dB/0.6562 for bicubic, SRResNet, and SRGAN respectively. These are values reported by the historical slide, not new measurements. In this displayed comparison SRGAN has lower PSNR and SSIM than SRResNet; the perceptual goal of sharper, plausible detail can trade off against pixelwise similarity, so no method wins every criterion.

CycleGAN addresses translation between two image collections without matched pairs. It learns mappings G:X→Y and F:Y→X, with a discriminator for each target domain. Adversarial losses encourage translated outputs to resemble their target collections. Cycle consistency adds the constraint that a round trip should recover its own input: F(G(x))≈x and G(F(y))≈y. The original photo and painting examples include Monet-style translation, horses↔zebras, summer↔winter, and object or fruit transformations. Cycle consistency is a constraint that helps make unpaired learning useful; it does not uniquely identify the intended semantic mapping.

ℒcycle=𝔼x~pX[‖F(G(x))−x‖1]+𝔼y~pY[‖G(F(y))−y‖1]
Trace both CycleGAN round trips

Given: an unpaired sample x from collection X and a separate unpaired sample y from collection Y.

  1. Translate x with G to y~=G(x), then map it back with F to x^=F(y~). Compare x^ with the same original x.
  2. Translate y with F to x~=F(y), then map it back with G to y^=G(x~). Compare y^ with the same original y.

Check: the cycle loss compares reconstructed inputs, not an unavailable paired X-to-Y target. The two source collections need no one-to-one image correspondence.

Common mistakes

  • Read each DCGAN label as a tensor shape: spatial size and channel count change separately; the output has 3 channels.
  • Keep latent and image dimensions architecture-dependent. The source’s own 100-to-12,288 example contradicts its broad “typically higher” statement.
  • A hinge discriminator score or EBGAN energy is not a probability. For the EBGAN convention here, low energy means more real-like.
  • In InfoGAN, z is unstructured noise and c is a sampled code with a chosen prior. Q estimates c; semantic disentanglement is encouraged, not guaranteed.
  • In a conditional GAN, both G and D receive the condition. A real image paired with the wrong text is a negative example.
  • CoGAN weight sharing does not mean its training examples are matched pairs.
  • In LAPGAN, generate from level 3 to 0; add each residual to the upscaled image.
  • ALI’s discriminator receives intact pairs produced by each route: (x,z) or (x̃,z).
  • CycleGAN learns from unpaired collections. Cycle consistency compares a round trip to its own starting image; it does not use paired cross-domain ground truth.
  • The printed super-resolution metrics are slide-reported historical values. They do not show SRGAN leading PSNR or SSIM in that comparison.

Exam focus

  • DCGAN generator: project/reshape, progressively upsample through the source-labelled channels, batch-normalize hidden layers, use ReLU internally and Tanh at output.
  • Latent arithmetic is an illustrative representation-space relationship, not a universal guarantee.
  • Label flipping requires matching changes to targets and loss signs. Hinge scores and reconstruction energies are not probabilities.
  • InfoGAN maximizes a variational lower bound on mutual information using Q(c|x); H(c) is constant for a fixed prior.
  • Conditional GANs feed conditions to both G and D. Text-image negatives include real/wrong-text and fake/right-text pairs.
  • CoGAN shares selected weights across domain paths; LAPGAN adds residuals coarse to fine; ALI discriminates between encoder/data and generator/prior joint pairs.
  • Super-resolution conditions generation on a low-resolution image. CycleGAN learns two maps and two cycle constraints from unpaired domain collections.

Practice questions

1. What are the exact spatial and channel shapes in the source DCGAN generator?

Check your answer

4×4×1024→8×8×512→16×16×256→32×32×128→64×64×3
The final tensor is an RGB image with three channels; there is no additional 64-channel stage in the pictured path.

2. In EBGAN, which direction is “real-like” energy and what does G do?

Check your answer

Real examples should have low reconstruction energy. The discriminator pushes fake energy high (with a margin in the full paper objective); the generator tries to lower energy on its generated samples.

3. What does the InfoGAN lower bound estimate, and what does Q contribute?

Check your answer

It lower-bounds mutual information between sampled code c and generated image G(z,c). Q(c|x) predicts the sampled code from generated image features; a fixed prior makes H(c) constant. It encourages, but does not guarantee, human-interpretable factors.

4. For text-to-image training, name the positive pair and both negative pair types.

Check your answer

Positive: real image with correct text. Negatives: real image with wrong text, and generated image with correct text. D receives image and text condition for each pair.

5. How does CoGAN relate its two domain paths without paired training data?

Check your answer

Both paths use the same latent z and share selected generator and discriminator weights. The slide describes learning from domain marginals without corresponding supervision; same-z outputs are not guaranteed semantic matches.

6. State the LAPGAN generation order and the operation at each finer level.

Check your answer

Generate the level-3 base from z₃. At levels 2, 1, then 0, predict a residual conditioned on the upscaled previous image and add that residual to form the next image.

7. Which two joint pairs does ALI’s discriminator compare?

Check your answer

(x,z), where x is drawn from data and z from the encoder q(z|x); and (x̃,z), where z is drawn from p(z) and x̃ from the generator p(x|z).

8. What does a CycleGAN cycle loss compare when training sets are unpaired?

Check your answer

It compares F(G(x)) with its own source x and G(F(y)) with its own source y. It does not compare x with a paired target from the other collection.

9. Which system wins PSNR and SSIM in the slide’s super-resolution comparison?

Check your answer

SRResNet has the highest displayed values (23.44 dB, 0.7777), ahead of SRGAN (20.34 dB, 0.6562). These are historical slide-reported figures, not fresh measurements.
