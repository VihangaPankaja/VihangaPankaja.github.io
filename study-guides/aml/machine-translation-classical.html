Machine Translation: Rule-Based and Statistical Methods

Classical machine translation must preserve a message while changing its word order and form. This page follows the shift from hand-written linguistic rules to phrase-based statistical translation.

Big picture

Machine translation (MT) automatically converts text from a source language into a target language while preserving meaning. That goal is harder than replacing each word with a dictionary entry: languages order ideas differently, mark grammatical information differently, and leave different information unstated. A useful system must preserve the message and produce a target sentence that reads naturally.

Rule-based MT (RBMT) encodes linguistic analysis and transfer rules by hand. Statistical MT (SMT) instead learns translation choices from parallel sentences, then ranks possible target sentences using both translation evidence and target-language fluency. The classical phrase-based decoder searches among phrase translations, but limited reordering and sparse phrase estimates constrain it. The following neural and decoding topics continue from this point; their detailed architectures, beam-search variants, and BLEU evaluation are covered separately.

Why translation is more than word substitution

Word order carries grammatical roles. English commonly uses subject–verb–object (SVO), while Sinhala, Tamil, and Japanese commonly use subject–object–verb (SOV). A translation may need to move a verb across a phrase, not just select equivalent vocabulary. Morphology adds another challenge: a single English word may correspond to a target form carrying case, number, tense, or honorific information.

Words can also be ambiguous or have no one-word equivalent. “Bank” can refer to a river edge or a financial institution; context selects the intended sense. A pro-drop language may omit a subject that English expects. The Sinhala sentence “ලියුම ලිව්වා” (liyuma liwwā, “wrote the letter”) does not identify whether the subject is I, he, she, or they. The discourse may supply that information; translating without it should not invent a subject. These differences explain why a translation system needs structure, context, and evidence about how languages correspond.

Rule-based translation: analyze, transfer, generate

An RBMT system is a pipeline of hand-written resources. It first analyzes the source, then maps the analysis into the target language, then generates a target form. Each stage has a distinct job: analysis identifies what the source says, transfer chooses target equivalents and structure, and generation expresses that structure in grammatical target forms.

Teaching example: preserve roles while changing order

This is a Teaching example, not a lexical translation table. Suppose analysis of a source sentence identifies the roles subject = “I”, verb = “read”, and object = “the book”.

  1. Analysis: record the subject, action, and object rather than treating the words as an unordered list.
  2. Transfer: for an SOV target structure, arrange the roles as subject → object → verb.
  3. Generation: choose target words and the inflected verb form required by the target grammar. No particular target-language words are assumed here.
  4. Check: the same person must still perform the same action on the same object. The changed order expresses the same role relations.

The hard part is coverage: hand-writing rules for language structure and exceptions is tedious, and an uncovered construction can fail even if the pipeline stages are conceptually sound.

Parallel data and the statistical objective

Data-driven translation learns from parallel data: source sentences paired with their translations. Such pairs provide evidence about how meanings and forms correspond across languages. The lecture motivates statistical MT with large-scale parallel data, but parallel data is not equally abundant for every language pair; the approach depends on what suitable data exists.

Let e denote one fixed source sentence and f a candidate target sentence. The SMT objective is to select the target with the greatest conditional probability given that source. The lecture briefly reverses these variable names on one page; here e is source and f is target throughout. With that convention, Bayes’ theorem gives the noisy-channel decomposition below.

f*=argmaxfP(f∣e)
P(f∣e)=P(e∣f)P(f)P(e)
f*=argmaxfP(e∣f)P(f)P(e)=argmaxfP(e∣f)P(f)

For a fixed source e, the denominator P(e) is the same for every candidate f; removing that positive constant cannot change which candidate maximizes the score. The term P(e∣f) is the translation channel: how well a proposed target could account for the source. The term P(f) is a target-language model: how probable or fluent the target sentence is in its own language. Both are needed: a fluent candidate may still express the wrong message, while a faithful candidate may be awkwardly formed.

Worked derivation: why the source denominator disappears
  1. Start with the desired conditional probability: P(f∣e).
  2. Apply Bayes’ rule: P(f∣e)=P(e∣f)P(f)P(e).
  3. Keep the source e fixed and compare two candidate targets, f₁ and f₂. Both scores divide by the same P(e).
  4. Therefore P(f1∣e)>P(f2∣e) exactly when P(e∣f1)P(f1)>P(e∣f2)P(f2) (for P(e)>0).
  5. So maximize the product P(e∣f)P(f). The channel rewards source compatibility; the target LM rewards target-language plausibility.

This is the classical noisy-channel objective described here, not a modern neural MT training loss.

Phrase translation: relative frequency and reordering

Phrase-based SMT groups consecutive words into spans and translates each source phrase into a target phrase. A phrase can contain multiple words; it need not be a grammatical phrase in the linguistic sense. Parallel sentence alignments supply extracted phrase pairs. For a source phrase ē, the target-given-source phrase probability is estimated by relative frequency over the extracted pairs:

ϕ(f¯∣e¯)=count(e¯,f¯)∑f¯′count(e¯,f¯′)

The denominator sums counts for that same source phrase over candidate target phrases f¯′; this direction is ϕ(f¯∣e¯), target phrase given source phrase. If the source phrase has no extracted pair, the denominator has no observed count and the relative-frequency estimate cannot provide a usable translation for it. The lecture gives no phrase counts, so there is no numeric phrase-table probability to calculate here.

A candidate sentence can be segmented into K phrase pairs. Its phrase translation score multiplies their conditional phrase probabilities and a distortion term for each jump between consecutive source spans:

P(f¯1K∣e¯1K)=∏k=1Kϕ(f¯k∣e¯k)d(startk−endk−1−1)

The distortion function d(·) penalizes the jump from the previously translated source span to the next one; its role is to discourage large reorderings. In practice, the phrase system also keeps the reverse conditional phrase probability ϕ(e¯∣f¯) and lexical weights that smooth estimates for rare phrase pairs. These components contribute evidence and robustness; the source supplies no extracted counts, distortion values, or learned feature weights, so they remain symbolic.

Worked interpretation: what each phrase score term contributes

For a segmented candidate with phrase pairs indexed 1,…,K, read the expression from left to right:

  1. For each pair (e¯k,f¯k), use ϕ(f¯k∣e¯k): how frequently this target phrase was observed with this source phrase relative to other target phrases for the same source phrase.
  2. Multiply the phrase probabilities across the chosen segmentation. A rare or unseen pair can make this evidence weak or unavailable.
  3. For each next source span, evaluate d(startk−endk−1−1). It penalizes the displacement between source spans; a long jump receives a penalty according to the learned/modelled distortion function.
  4. Combine with the reverse phrase direction, lexical weights, and target LM as the system’s scoring features. Their weights and exact numeric values are learned or estimated by the system; they are not provided in the lecture.

This is a symbolic reading of the source model. It does not invent phrase counts or a numeric candidate ranking.

The target n-gram language model

The language model estimates the target sentence’s fluency from monolingual text in the target language. It factors a sentence into next-word predictions conditioned on preceding words. In an n-gram model, the context is limited to the preceding n−1 words; for example, a trigram predicts a word from the previous two. This target-side score supports natural local word sequences, while the translation channel supplies source-conditioned evidence.

P(f)=∏j=1mP(fj∣f1j−1)≈∏j=1mP(fj∣fj−n+1j−1)

For a trigram, the maximum-likelihood estimate uses observed counts in the target-language text:

PMLE(fj∣fj−2,fj−1)=c(fj−2,fj−1,fj)c(fj−2,fj−1)

The numerator counts occurrences of the preceding two words followed by the proposed next word. The denominator counts that two-word history. With a positive history count, an unseen continuation has numerator zero and therefore estimated probability zero. If the history itself has no occurrences, this raw ratio is undefined; a model needs a strategy such as backing off to a shorter context. These are count dependencies, not assumed numerical counts.

An unsmoothed n-gram model may assign probability zero to an unseen n-gram. Since the sentence probability is a product, one zero term makes the whole product zero and can veto an otherwise good translation. Smoothing avoids treating every unseen sequence as impossible. The lecture names Kneser–Ney, backoff, and interpolation: these methods redistribute or combine probability evidence so a model can score events beyond the exact observed counts. No counts or smoothing values are given, so none are fabricated here.

The slide’s example shows the target German candidates for the English source “the house is small.” Its displayed values are illustrative logP(f) scores under a trigram LM:

Source sentence Candidate target f Illustrative logP(f)
the house is small das Haus ist klein -7.4
the house is small das Haus ist gering -10.2
the house is small klein das Haus ist -16.1
Worked ranking: compare the illustrative target-LM scores
  1. Compare the three log scores: −7.4, −10.2, and −16.1.
  2. Since −7.4>−10.2>−16.1, the first candidate has the greatest log probability (it is least negative).
  3. Thus this target German trigram LM term ranks das Haus ist klein highest among these three candidates.
  4. Check the direction: larger log probability is better; all three values are negative, so the one closest to zero wins this LM-only comparison.

The values are illustrative and cannot be regenerated from the supplied material because trigram counts and smoothing details are absent. This ranking says nothing by itself about source faithfulness: P(f) assesses target fluency, while translation compatibility comes from P(e∣f).

Phrase decoding: cover source spans and prune search

Phrase decoding searches through target hypotheses by extending them with phrase pairs that cover source spans. The lecture describes building translations left to right. Hypotheses are grouped into stacks according to how many source words they have covered, so candidates at comparable coverage compete. Because the search space grows exponentially, histogram pruning retains only a fixed number of high-scoring hypotheses per stack, while threshold pruning discards hypotheses too far below the best score in that stack. The lecture does not specify a beam width, a tie rule, or a complete set of phrase options.

Supplemental teaching example: symbolic phrase coverage and pruning

Given: source positions {1, 2, 3}; a schematic phrase span covering {2, 3}; then a schematic span covering {1}. Phrase text, scores, beam width, and tie-breaking are intentionally unspecified.

1. Start with uncovered source words

Teaching example. Represent a three-word source abstractly as positions 1, 2, 3. The initial hypothesis has coverage set ∅, so it belongs to the 0-covered stack. No phrase translations are assumed.

2. Extend with one phrase span

Suppose a phrase pair covers source positions 2–3. The new coverage set is {2, 3}, containing two source words, so place this hypothesis in the 2-covered stack. Its uncovered position is 1. This illustrates that phrase extension can cover a span; it does not assert the source’s next selected phrase.

3. Extend to complete coverage

Extend that partial hypothesis with a phrase pair covering position 1. Coverage becomes {1, 2, 3}, so the hypothesis reaches a complete-coverage stack. The target pieces are appended in decoder order; no target phrase text or source order is invented.

4. Prune comparable hypotheses and check

Within each coverage stack, histogram pruning keeps the best fixed number and threshold pruning removes candidates too far below that stack’s best score. The lecture provides neither width nor scores, so the result is a completed coverage path, not a numeric winner. Check: every source position 1, 2, and 3 is covered exactly once in this schematic path.

Limitations and the neural handoff

The phrase-based system’s reordering is local: its distance penalty systematically discourages long jumps, even when an SOV–SVO language pair needs them. It does not generalize across surface forms; forms such as “walk,” “walks,” and “walked” can be unrelated phrase-table entries, and morphological richness fragments the evidence further. Sparse estimates make unseen phrases brittle, while the table itself grows as phrase pairs accumulate.

Finally, alignment, phrase extraction, scoring, and tuning are separately trained or optimized components. Each can pursue a different objective, so errors can accumulate across the pipeline instead of one end-to-end objective coordinating them. Continue with Neural Machine Translation, Attention, and Transformers, then Translation Decoding, Multilingual Models, and Evaluation for the architectures and detailed search/evaluation procedures.

Common mistakes

  • Treating translation as dictionary substitution. Check structure, morphology, ambiguity, lexical gaps, and information supplied only by discourse.
  • Reversing source and target variables. This page fixes e as source and f as target: P(f∣e) is the translation objective.
  • Calling P(f) a source-language model. It scores the target sentence; the example’s target language is German.
  • Maximizing fluency alone. A fluent sentence may not explain the source; combine P(f) with translation-channel evidence P(e∣f).
  • Reversing the phrase conditional. ϕ(f¯∣e¯) conditions on a source phrase and scores a candidate target phrase; its denominator sums over target phrase alternatives for that same source phrase.
  • Assuming phrase counts or decoder settings that are not supplied. Keep phrase scores symbolic and do not invent count tables, weights, beam widths, or tie rules.
  • Assuming an unseen n-gram is harmless. In an unsmoothed product, one zero factor makes the whole sentence probability zero.
  • Calling stacks a target-length grouping. In this phrase decoder they are grouped by the number of source words covered.
  • Expecting the phrase model to handle any reordering or morphology. Its local distortion, fragmented surface forms, and sparse estimates limit those cases.

Exam focus

  • State the RBMT order: analysis → transfer → generation, and name each stage’s role.
  • Explain why source and target languages differ in order, morphology, lexical choice, and omitted information.
  • Derive the noisy-channel score with e=source, f=target; explain why the fixed-source denominator can be dropped.
  • Distinguish translation channel P(e∣f) from target fluency model P(f).
  • Read the direction and denominator of ϕ(f¯∣e¯), and identify the roles of phrase segmentation, distortion, reverse probability, and lexical weights.
  • Rank log probabilities correctly: a greater, less-negative score is preferred by that LM term.
  • Describe coverage stacks, phrase-pair extension, and histogram/threshold pruning without claiming exact settings.
  • Connect local reordering, surface-form fragmentation, sparsity, and independent components to phrase-SMT limitations.

Practice questions

1. What information does the Sinhala sentence “ලියුම ලිව්වා” leave to discourse?

Check your answer

It says “wrote the letter” but omits the subject. Context must determine whether the writer is I, he, she, they, or another understood subject; do not invent one without evidence.

2. In RBMT, what does each of analysis, transfer, and generation contribute?

Check your answer

Analysis identifies source morphology, parts of speech, and structure. Transfer uses bilingual vocabulary and structural rules to map that representation. Generation realizes target syntax and inflection.

3. With e fixed as the source and f as the target, which factor can be removed from Bayes’ argmax and why?

Check your answer

The denominator P(e) is constant across candidate targets for the fixed source, so dividing every candidate score by it cannot change their ordering. The ranking can use P(e∣f)P(f).

4. For the German candidates, which target n-gram LM score ranks highest: -7.4, -10.2, or -16.1? What does that result not establish?

Check your answer

−7.4 is greatest and least negative, so das Haus ist klein ranks highest under the illustrative target LM term. This does not establish that it is the most faithful translation; source compatibility must also be assessed.

5. A phrase table has no extracted pair for a source phrase. What can you conclude from the relative-frequency formula?

Check your answer

No observed phrase-pair count is available to estimate its target-given-source relative frequency. The lecture’s phrase table is sparse for this case; no numeric probability can be recovered from absent counts.

6. How do coverage stacks and the two pruning methods control phrase-decoder search?

Check your answer

Stacks group partial hypotheses by how many source words are covered, so comparable coverage states compete. Histogram pruning keeps a fixed count per stack; threshold pruning removes candidates too far below that stack’s best score. Both reduce an exponential search without making it an exact exhaustive search.

7. Name two phrase-based SMT limitations and explain their effect.

Check your answer

Distance-penalized local reordering discourages the long jumps some SOV–SVO pairs require. Surface-form fragmentation and sparse phrase counts leave related forms or unseen phrases without robust shared evidence. Independent alignment, extraction, scoring, and tuning can also accumulate errors.
