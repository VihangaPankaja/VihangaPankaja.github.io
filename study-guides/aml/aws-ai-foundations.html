Big picture

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a broad field for making machines perform tasks associated with human intelligence. Machine learning (ML) is one family of approaches within that field: instead of writing every decision as a rule, a learner uses data to build a model that can make predictions on new inputs. Deep learning uses layered neural networks for complex patterns. Generative AI uses learned patterns to produce new content in response to a request or context.

These labels are useful ways to organize the introductory course material, not a complete taxonomy with rigid borders. A reliable way to reason about any system is to ask what task it performs, what data and learning approach support it, what happens when it receives new input, and how its outputs will be evaluated. This AWS material is optional companion reading and is separate from the assumed CS4681 exam route.

AI, machine learning, and deep learning

AI includes strategies and techniques for tasks that typically require human intelligence. The course uses computer vision and smart devices as examples; these can incorporate ML. ML learns patterns from existing data by training a mathematical model. Deep learning is ML based on neural networks with an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. More complex networks can represent more complex relationships, and the course associates deep learning with large, varied datasets and tasks involving unstructured data.

The course introduces generative AI through a deep-learning and foundation-model lineage. Treat that as a teaching map rather than an exhaustive classification: fields and model families can overlap, and a model's capabilities depend on its training and design. A foundation model (FM) is broadly pretrained and can be adapted to respond to a range of requests, but no single FM should be assumed to perform every modality or task well.

AI can help analyze complex collections of sounds, images, and environmental information, and the course gives wildlife protection, food insecurity, and disaster recovery as areas where organizations apply it. It also highlights risks: biased or erroneous design can lead to unfair or inaccurate decisions, while limited transparency can make accountability harder. Its written examples include reported racial bias in some health-care treatment recommendations and lawyers submitting fabricated citations from AI tools. These are reminders to scrutinize outputs and systems; they are not evidence that all AI has the same outcomes.

The course groups responsible AI practice into three areas: accurate, fair, safe, and secure; explainable and transparent; and governed and controlled. These connect protecting people and data with making system behavior inspectable and maintaining ways to steer it throughout its lifecycle. They are goals supported by practices and checks, not guarantees conferred by an AI label. The responsible-AI and governance companion develops these concerns in detail.

From data to prediction

An ML model is the result of a learning process: an algorithm is applied to training data so the model can recognize patterns and make predictions. A team selects data and a suitable approach, trains the model, evaluates performance, and may tune it, collect more data, and repeat training and testing. The quality of the data, techniques, and model affects output quality.

Inference is different: it is the output produced when a trained model applies what it learned to new input data. For example, a payment system may send an incoming card transaction to a fraud model; its prediction then informs the next processing step. Training uses examples to shape the model. Inference uses a trained model to produce a prediction for a new case.

Evaluation does not stop at launch. In production, teams monitor and evaluate prediction quality because the environment can change. New fraud patterns, for example, may no longer resemble the historical training data. Monitoring can reveal when the model or data needs investigation and may motivate additional data collection, tuning, or retraining.

Worked example: a new card transaction

The course's fraud example helps locate each phase without implying that a prediction is a verified fact.

Trace the training, inference, and monitoring roles
  1. Training: historical transactions form the training examples. If fraud labels are available, they identify the target the model learns to predict.
  2. Model: training produces a model that recognizes patterns associated with the examples and learning method.
  3. Inference: a newly arriving transaction is passed as new input. The model returns a prediction for that transaction; this is an inference.
  4. Use and check: an application may use the prediction to select a next step. Evaluation checks whether predictions are useful, and production monitoring looks for quality changes.
  5. Respond to change: if fraud tactics change, later evaluation may motivate reviewing the data, tuning, or retraining. Monitoring is evidence to investigate, not an automatic guarantee that the model remains reliable.

Data, labels, and learning approaches

Data must fit the task. Before choosing a model, consider whether there is enough relevant, high-quality, representative, and current data; what preparation it needs; whether it contains personally identifiable information; how it will be protected across development and deployment; and how duplicates or inconsistencies will be handled. The required accuracy, transparency, and response speed matter too, along with the team's skills, resources, and the cost relative to the problem's impact.

The course groups data by its organization. The useful distinction is the schema and structure available for the task, not only the file extension: the same kind of file can be organized differently.

Data categories used in the course, interpreted through their organization
CategoryHow it is organizedCourse examples
StructuredRows and columns with a defined schema; easier to query consistently, usually less flexible.Relational database tables, spreadsheets.
SemistructuredTags, attributes, or other markers organize some elements without imposing one rigid table schema.Email fields, JSON, XML; the course also lists CSV.
UnstructuredNo shared predefined schema for all content; flexible, but often needs more preparation to analyze.Documents, audio, images, movies, and clickstream data.

Labels specify a target in training data. Dog and cat image labels tell a learner which category each image belongs to; a historical transaction table can label each transaction as fraud or not fraud. Labels can be added by subject-matter experts or supported by ML. With unlabelled data, an approach must find patterns without a supplied target; this can take more data or more complex methods.

Learning paradigms answer different questions

The paradigms describe how learning receives information or feedback. They can be combined in a project, or different approaches can be used in different phases; they are not mutually exclusive project labels.

Choosing analytics, ML, or generative AI

Start with the task, not with a technology label. Traditional programming or statistical analytics can be a good fit when business rules are explicit, variables are limited, and an answer can be produced quickly enough. ML is worth considering when patterns are difficult to specify and sufficient relevant training data, required performance, skills, resources, and cost-benefit conditions are present. A generative approach may suit content synthesis or creation, but its flexible output brings additional quality and review demands.

Retail: monthly inventory versus personalized recommendations

A retailer can use historical sales and straightforward statistical forecasting to set monthly inventory levels when real-time forecasts are not required. This is a case where conventional analytics may fit.

For customer grouping and recommendations, the same retailer may combine purchase history, browsing behavior, demographics, and social interactions, then use ML to identify buying-pattern groups and suggest items in real time. That is an illustrative fit, not a universal rule: data sufficiency and quality, privacy, performance needs, cost, and the actual business impact still need review.

Fraud: labeled prediction versus pattern discovery

A supervised fraud model can learn from historical transactions labeled fraud/not fraud and predict a label for a new transaction. If the task instead is to find unusual spending patterns without a supplied fraud label, an unsupervised method may help surface anomalies for investigation. These approaches answer different questions; an anomaly is not automatically confirmed fraud.

Foundation models and generative AI

The course describes generative AI as using large deep-learning models called foundation models, trained on extensive data, to produce new content in response to requests. A prompt is the natural-language instruction or question supplied to request a task. At inference, the prompt is part of the new input. The generated result is a prediction shaped by learned patterns and context—not a known fact simply because it sounds fluent.

The course's modality examples include a text comparison of dogs and cats; an image request for a black puppy with large ears and a white kitten playing with a ball; a joyful puppy bark; and a prediction of the 3D structure of the canine olfactory receptor protein OR5B17. These are examples of different task and output types. FM categories are not exhaustive, and models are generally designed or trained for particular output categories. Some accept multiple input types, but do not assume that every FM generates every modality.

Tokens and embeddings

An LLM first divides text into tokens: units that may be whole words, subwords, or individual characters depending on the tokenizer. Embedding maps a token to a numerical vector representation. The course's two-dimensional sketch is a schematic illustration that similar terms can have nearby vector representations (for example, “cat” closer to “kitten” than to “puppy”). Real embedding vectors have hundreds or thousands of dimensions. The sketch gives no numerical coordinates, so none should be inferred from its plotted positions.

Tokenization creates the model's input units; embedding represents those units numerically. These steps are distinct from training the model's learned parameters. In the course's high-level account, vectors are inputs to neural-network processing, while training on data teaches patterns and relationships that support tasks such as language understanding, generation, and translation.

Context changes a next-token prediction

The course illustrates how surrounding words change which continuation is more likely. “The cat jumped onto the…” leaves a range of plausible continuations. “Sleepy” can make a soft resting place more likely; “curious” and nearby mention of a fish plate can make a table more plausible; a story about a performing cat balancing on round objects can make a bowling ball plausible. The point is that the model conditions its prediction on the whole context, not that it applies a fixed word-to-word rule.

The slide places three numeric values beside several different prompt contexts without assigning them to a single conditional distribution. Those values are therefore omitted here. A probability is conditional on a context; a displayed score is not automatically a probability, and neither is deterministic truth.

Supplying more task-relevant information

The course lays out a rough continuum for influencing an FM: (1) add relevant information, instructions, examples, or reference material in the prompt; (2) let the model refer to an external source such as internal policy documents; or (3) further train or customize a model with a smaller task-specific dataset. Its classroom analogy is a worksheet, an open-book test, and a subject tutor. The sequence generally adds complexity and often requires more resources and cost; it is a conceptual comparison, not a prescribed implementation. The detailed mechanics belong to the separate prompting and FM-use companions.

The course also contrasts more restrictive inference settings, described as producing more deterministic outputs, with less restrictive settings that allow more creative variation. Model parameters are model-specific. Less variation does not establish factual accuracy; outputs still need evaluation against the task and evidence.

The course offers a short comparison rather than a full treatment. It also notes that these are common approaches, not an exhaustive list; architectures can be adapted across tasks and combined, and choices depend on the task, data, and computational resources.

Choosing a suitable generative task

Work backward from the business problem. Generative AI may fit when a task needs analysis of diverse, unstructured sources; synthesis of complex information; creative or repetitive content creation; high-resolution image generation; or personalized content at scale. Examples include virtual assistants, personalized search, code generation, summarization, extraction, and content creation.

Potential benefits must be made measurable. The course groups possible impact areas as customer experience, employee productivity, research and creativity, and operational processes. It suggests starting where a baseline is already tracked and where a change can be measured in a short period. Examples of candidate measures include help-desk call time and the number of deployments in a period. These are measurement scenarios, not promised improvements or results.

When the fit is poor

Generative AI may be a poor fit when a task requires high accuracy and consistency, a transparent explanation, or dependable handling of a rapidly changing or poorly defined problem. It is also a weak choice when high-quality data, necessary skills, or resources are missing, or when the business value is too unclear to justify implementation and operating costs. Ethical concerns matter too: if risks such as offensive output, bias, or misuse of protected or private data cannot be tolerated or mitigated, the use case may not be acceptable. High-stakes medical or legal decisions and content for children are examples where errors or offensive content may have very low tolerance.

The course names hallucinations (plausible but verifiably incorrect output), toxicity, bias, and content resembling protected intellectual property as challenges. What counts as acceptable can depend on audience and context; even a quotation may be appropriate in one setting and unsuitable in another. Model choice, prompt design, data quality, safeguards, human review, and lifecycle data protection all affect output quality. Detailed prompting, adaptation, responsible-AI practice, and security/governance belong in the related AWS companions.

Common mistakes

  • Treating AI, ML, deep learning, and generative AI as interchangeable: use them at their proper levels in the introductory map, and remember the map is not exhaustive.
  • Confusing training with inference: training changes what the model has learned; inference applies a trained model to new input.
  • Calling an output a fact: a model output is a prediction that needs evaluation; fluent wording is not verification.
  • Selecting ML before checking data and constraints: evaluate relevance, quality, representation, timeliness, data preparation, required accuracy/transparency/speed, resources, and cost.
  • Assuming a file extension determines data structure: look at the schema and how content is organized for the task.
  • Treating the learning paradigms as mutually exclusive: a project can combine them or use them in separate phases.
  • Assuming every FM handles all modalities: task and output capabilities vary by model.
  • Reading the token sketch as a coordinate chart: it is a schematic geometry only; the actual vectors are high-dimensional and their coordinates are not supplied.
  • Treating next-token values as a universal distribution across prompts: each prediction is conditioned on its context; the slide's numbers are not assigned to one context here.
  • Assuming generative AI is automatically a good fit: test quality needs, tolerable risks, measurement, resources, and business value first.
  • Treating a 2025 service map as current advice: service comparisons here describe the course edition only.

Concept review

  • AI is the broad field; ML learns patterns from data; deep learning uses layered neural networks; generative AI produces requested content using learned patterns.
  • Training fits a model from training data. Inference produces a prediction for a new input. Evaluation and monitoring check whether predictions remain useful as conditions change.
  • Structured, semistructured, and unstructured describe different organization/schema conditions. Labels identify targets; supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning use different signals and can coexist in one project.
  • FMs are broadly pretrained, but task and modality support differs. LLMs process text as tokens represented by high-dimensional embeddings.
  • Context affects generated continuations. Generated output remains a prediction and must be assessed for the task.
  • Use-case fit depends on data, quality requirements, risks, skills, resources, cost, and measurable impact.
  • AWS service descriptions are retained as 2025 course context only.

Practice questions

1. A rule-based monthly inventory forecast can meet a retailer’s needs. What should the team compare before choosing ML?

Check your answer

Check whether analytics and statistical forecasting already meet accuracy and timing needs. ML also requires relevant high-quality data, sufficient skills and resources, lifecycle management, and a cost justified by the likely impact. The retail example uses monthly historical sales rather than a real-time task.

2. What is the difference between training and inference?

Check your answer

Training uses examples and a learning method to produce or adjust a model. Inference applies a trained model to a new input and returns a prediction for that case.

3. A transaction has no fraud label, but the team wants to find unusual spending patterns. Which learning approach matches the question, and what can its output establish?

Check your answer

An unsupervised approach such as anomaly detection matches the unlabelled pattern-finding task. It can surface unusual cases for review; an anomaly alone does not establish that a transaction is fraud.

4. Why can the same data file be described differently for a learning task?

Check your answer

The category depends on its organization and schema. A spreadsheet with consistent fields and rows is structured; a file with tagged elements or flexible records can be semistructured; free-form text, audio, or images without a shared schema are unstructured. The extension alone does not decide the category.

5. Name the six LLM task types in the course and one distinct purpose for each.

Check your answer

Text generation composes new text; question answering responds to a question; summarization condenses existing content; extraction returns requested details; conversation uses the ongoing thread; code generation writes or audits development code.

6. What do tokenization and embedding do, and how are they different from training?

Check your answer

Tokenization divides text into words, subwords, or characters. Embedding maps tokens to numerical vectors. Training uses data to teach learned model parameters and patterns; the text's token and vector representations are not themselves the training process.

7. Why does context matter for a next-token prediction?

Check your answer

The model conditions likely continuations on the sequence and surrounding information. A “sleepy cat” context can favor a different continuation than a “curious cat” near a fish plate, and a performance story can change the likely object again. These are predictions, not fixed rules or deterministic facts.

8. Give one reason a generative-AI task may be a poor fit and one way to assess a candidate benefit.

Check your answer

A task may be unsuitable when it requires strict accuracy, consistency, explainability, or low tolerance for harmful errors, or when data, skills, resources, or clear value are missing. For a candidate benefit, start from an existing baseline—such as help-desk call duration—and define a measurable outcome and time window before claiming improvement.

9. A team needs to categorize incoming support tickets using the content of each ticket and a history of categorized examples. Which approach does the written course scenario favor, and why?

Check your answer

The course-version answer is traditional ML: it can learn from historical ticket data to categorize new tickets by their content. The course contrasts this with rigid software rules, manual sorting, and generative AI as a less cost-effective fit for this specific classification task. This is concept practice based on the dated written scenario, not a live assessment or a claim about university exam coverage.

10. In the written course scenario about comparing multiple FMs without managing infrastructure, what is the course-version answer and why?

Check your answer

The course-version answer is Amazon Bedrock because that edition describes it as giving access to multiple FMs for evaluation without managing the underlying infrastructure. This answer belongs to the dated written scenario; it is not live assessment activity or current product guidance.
