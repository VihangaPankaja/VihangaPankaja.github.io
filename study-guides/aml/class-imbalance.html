Imbalanced Data: Metrics and Resampling

Rare events can be easy to miss even when a classifier is correct on most rows. This guide connects the shape and causes of imbalance to class-aware metrics, sampling choices, threshold decisions, and leakage-safe evaluation.

Big picture

Class imbalance means one label has far fewer examples than another. The small class is often the event we care most about, such as fraud, a rare disease, a manufacturing defect, customer churn, spam, or intrusion. A model can be correct on nearly every row by predicting the common class every time, yet fail at the important task. The useful response is to define the event, inspect class-aware metrics, choose a decision rule for the costs and goal, and compare resampling only through a leakage-safe evaluation.

What imbalance means and why difficulty is more than a ratio

The lecture calls the more frequent label the majority class and the underrepresented label the minority class. In its convention these correspond to negative and positive, respectively. In an application, positive should name the event being monitored; it does not mean “good.” Define that event before interpreting any metric.

Imbalance may arise because an event is naturally rare, collecting positive cases costs more, or the sampling process under-represents a class or subgroup. The resulting data may distort associations as well as counts. Imbalance is common in fraud detection, rare-disease screening, defect detection, churn, spam, and intrusion detection.

The lecture lists four factors: degree of imbalance, concept complexity, overall training-set size, and classifier type. It emphasizes the first two: a large ratio need not make an easy-to-learn concept unlearnable, while overlap between classes or small disjuncts (small, separate pockets of a class) can make the minority pattern difficult to learn. The same ratio can therefore describe very different problems.

IR=NnegativeNpositive

This lecture defines IR as negative count divided by positive count. For example, an IR of 99 means 99 negatives for each positive. State the convention whenever reporting a ratio because other sources may define its reciprocal.

Why ordinary loss and accuracy can hide missed positives

If most examples are negative, getting those right can make the average loss look small even when the learner misses many positives. Distance-based voting can also be dominated by the majority points nearby, and probability-based decisions can be affected by how class prevalence appears in training. These effects explain why inspecting the positive class matters before selecting a classifier.

A probability threshold turns a score into an action. A threshold of

0.5

does not require balanced class priors: with calibrated posterior probabilities and equal costs for false positives and false negatives,

0.5

is the equal-cost decision boundary even when priors are unequal. The posterior already reflects the prior. Different error costs, goals, or poor calibration can make another threshold more useful.

Supplemental decision rule: how unequal error costs move a threshold

Assume calibrated posterior probability p=P(positive∣x), zero cost for a correct decision, false-positive cost CFP, and false-negative cost CFN. Predict positive when expected false-positive cost (1−p)CFP is no greater than expected false-negative cost pCFN.

(1−p)CFP≤pCFN⟺p≥CFPCFP+CFN

Equal costs give threshold 12. If missing a positive costs more, the threshold falls. This is a labeled decision-theory supplement, not a new threshold claim from the lecture.

Changing a threshold often trades precision against recall for a fixed scoring model, but there is no universal inverse relationship across all models or datasets. Choose thresholds on validation data for the intended operating goal; do not tune them on the final test set.

Choose what to change: reduce negatives or add positives

The lecture groups sampling into under-sampling and over-sampling. These techniques change the training examples seen by a learner; they do not by themselves change the real-world error costs or make the evaluation distribution representative.

Sampling choices from the lecture and their main trade-offs
MethodWhat changesTrade-off or limit

Random under-sampling lowers the majority count by removing examples, so the learner may lose useful regions of the majority distribution. Random over-sampling raises minority frequency by drawing minority records with replacement; exact copies can lead to overfitting. The lecture’s plot also labels a shrinkage setting, but does not explain the perturbation, so it should not be described as the same operation as duplication.

A Tomek link is a pair of examples from different classes that are mutual nearest neighbors. The lecture presents removing the majority point near a minority point; that is the policy used in this sketch. Other software may support removing both members, so the policy matters. “Close to a minority example” by itself is not the full mutual-neighbor definition. The imbalanced-learn guide states both the definition and the majority-only versus both-points choices.

SMOTE and its variants

SMOTE grows the minority class by interpolating between a minority example and a nearby minority example. In the lecture, nearest neighbors are drawn only from minority examples, with a typical neighbor count of five. For a selected pair, interpolation creates a point along their feature-space segment. The method assumes that the chosen distance is meaningful and the intermediate feature combinations are valid.

Subtract P coordinate by coordinate:

The first feature changes by −1 toward B; the second changes by 1.

Multiply both differences by 14, then add to the corresponding P coordinates:

Check that the point lies on the chosen segment

Because 0≤λ=14≤1, the result lies between P and B. It is one quarter of the way from P toward B, closer to P. This geometric check confirms the interpolation arithmetic, not that the synthetic record is valid for the real domain.

(1−λ)P+λB=34(2,2)+14(1,3)=(1.75,2.25)

The coordinates are chosen only to make the procedure calculable; they are not measurements from the lecture. With the stated continuous features and Euclidean distance, the five candidate distances order as A, B, C, D, E. Feature scaling can change that ordering, so the distance metric and scaling should be justified. Ordinary numeric interpolation is not appropriate for category labels; use a variant designed for feature types.

SMOTE does not check the majority distribution before adding a point. A segment can cross a majority region or create an implausible combination, increasing overlap. Validity also depends on domain rules and, where relevant, causal constraints. On large or high-dimensional data, neighbor search and repeated pipeline fitting can add runtime.

Split first and keep evaluation honest

Resampling must happen after the evaluation split and only within each training fold. Fit learned preprocessing on that training fold too. Then fit the classifier and use validation data to choose a model and threshold. Keep the final test set untouched and at its natural distribution so the reported result reflects the task the model will face.

If records have a meaningful time order, split chronologically before preprocessing or resampling; never synthesize across the time boundary. In cross-validation, each fold gets its own training-only preprocessing and resampling. Resampling the full dataset before splitting can let duplicated or generated relatives leak into validation or test data.

Balancing counts is only one possible intervention. It does not fix mislabeled positives, sampling bias, distribution shift, concept overlap, or unequal decision costs. Compare resampling against alternatives such as a suitable decision threshold or cost-sensitive learning on the same untouched validation/test distributions.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the minority event “negative” because negative sounds undesirable, or assuming positive means beneficial. Define the event explicitly.
  • Reporting only accuracy on a skewed task. A high value can hide missed positives.
  • Reading a confusion matrix without checking which axis is actual and which is predicted.
  • Treating undefined precision as zero without stating a reporting convention. In the all-negative example precision has no predicted-positive cases, while count-form F1 evaluates to zero because actual positives were missed.
  • Calling F1 undefined in that all-negative example. Its count-form denominator is positive because false negatives remain. F1 is algebraically undefined only when there are no true positives, false positives, or false negatives.
  • Claiming a half-probability threshold requires balanced class priors. With calibrated posterior probabilities and equal error costs, that remains the equal-cost threshold.
  • Assuming precision and recall are always inversely related across different model comparisons.
  • Treating a Tomek link as any close cross-class pair; it requires mutual nearest neighbors, and the removal policy must be stated.
  • Confusing random duplication with the lecture’s unexplained shrinkage illustration.
  • Assuming SMOTE creates realistic points or checks the majority class. It interpolates in the chosen feature space and may increase overlap.
  • Applying continuous interpolation to nominal values or comparing unscaled features without checking whether the distance is meaningful.
  • Resampling before a split or balancing validation/test data, which leaks information or hides natural prevalence.
  • Expecting resampling to fix label noise, shift, overlap, collection bias, or the costs of errors by itself.

Practice questions

1. A dataset has 9,900 negatives and 100 positives. What is IR under the lecture’s convention, and what accuracy does an all-negative classifier get?

Check your answer

IR=9900100=99:1. The classifier is correct on 9,900 of 10,000 rows, so accuracy is 99%, but positive recall is zero.

2. With TP=0,FN=100,FP=0,TN=900, which of precision, recall, specificity, balanced accuracy, and count-form F1 are defined? Give each value where defined.

Check your answer

Precision is undefined because TP+FP=0. Recall 0/100=0; specificity 900/900=1; balanced accuracy (0+1)/2=0.5; count-form F1 0/(0+0+100)=0.

3. For TP=6,FN=4,FP=9,TN=981, calculate precision, recall, and balanced accuracy.

Check your answer

Precision 6/(6+9)=2/5. Recall 6/(6+4)=3/5. Specificity 981/(981+9)=109/110. Balanced accuracy (3/5+109/110)/2=35/44≈0.79545.

4. What makes two opposite-class examples a Tomek link, and which point is removed under the majority-only policy used here?

Check your answer

They are mutual nearest neighbors and have different labels. The stated majority-only cleaning policy removes the majority member; some configurations can remove both, so name the policy.

5. In the supplemental SMOTE example, P=(2,2), B=(1,3), and λ=14. Compute the new point and give a segment check.

Check your answer

B−P=(−1,1). P+λ(B−P)=(2−14,2+14)=(1.75,2.25). Since 0≤14≤1, it lies on segment P–B.

6. A calibrated model estimates P(positive∣x)=0.35. With equal false-positive and false-negative costs, is the positive class chosen at threshold 0.5? What changes if missing a positive is more costly?

Check your answer

No: 0.35 is below the equal-cost threshold 0.5. If false negatives cost more, the cost-based threshold CFP/(CFP+CFN) is lower, so 0.35 may qualify. The threshold is a decision choice, not a consequence of balanced priors.

7. Why must resampling and learned preprocessing be fitted separately inside each training fold?

Check your answer

Fitting them before splitting can expose information from validation/test rows or create related examples on both sides. Split first; fit preprocessing and resampling on training rows only; keep evaluation rows untouched.

8. Name two things ordinary resampling cannot fix by itself.

Check your answer

Examples include label noise, distribution shift, class overlap, collection bias, and unequal error costs. Resampling changes the training sample counts; it does not repair these underlying problems.
