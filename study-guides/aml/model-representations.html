Classifier boundaries and decision-tree representations

How the model’s representation shapes the boundaries and regions it can learn, illustrated with Spam/Ham email examples.

Big pictureRepresentation and hypothesis familiesLinear scores, decision boundaries, and marginsDecision trees: tests, branches, and leavesCompact comparisonCommon confusionsRevision remindersFurther studyPractice

Big picture

A learning method chooses both a representation for possible models and a way to search among them. Those choices determine which patterns it can express. A linear classifier draws one straight boundary in feature space; a decision tree builds a set of feature tests whose branches carve that space into labeled regions. Email Spam/Ham classification illustrates how these representations fit training examples—and why lower training error alone does not settle which model is best.

Representation and hypothesis families

A hypothesis is one candidate model: a possible function from inputs to predictions. The hypothesis family is the set of candidates allowed by a representation. Learning searches within that family using examples and an evaluation rule. If the family is too restricted, no candidate may describe the pattern; if it is very flexible, a candidate can match peculiarities of the training set.

Common representation families include:

Representation family What the model stores or computes Examples
Numerical functions A numeric mapping from features to a score or value Linear regression, neural networks, support vector machines
Symbolic functions Explicit tests or rules that partition cases Decision trees; propositional and first-order logic rules
Instance-based functions Keeps examples/cases and predicts from similar stored instances Nearest-neighbour, case-based methods
Probabilistic graphical models Represents variables and probabilistic relationships Naïve Bayes, Bayesian networks, HMMs, PCFGs, Markov networks
Ensembles Combines multiple models Model ensembles

A numerical score, symbolic rule set, remembered-neighbour rule, probability graph, and ensemble can all solve prediction tasks, but they express different kinds of structure. “Representation” answers what forms a candidate model may take; “evaluation” says how candidates are judged; “optimization” describes how the search is carried out. These are the three components of a machine-learning algorithm; the focus here is their role, not deriving a search procedure.

Linear scores, decision boundaries, and margins

Supplemental formalization: a standard mathematical description of a linear classifier uses a weighted score. With feature vector x, weights w, and offset b, its boundary is where the score is zero:

f(𝐱)=𝐰𝖳𝐱+b

One side has a positive score and the other a negative score; the classifier maps the two sides to class labels. The boundary itself satisfies f(𝐱)=0. For email classification, the feature axes are New Recipients horizontally and Email Length vertically. Place a new email at its feature values and assign the label associated with the side containing it. The next schematic uses different line styles to show alternative separators; circles and squares distinguish two illustrative class groups, and a diamond marks a constructed new email on the square side.

Each of the three candidate lines in the schematic leaves its two constructed groups on opposite sides, so several separators can fit one training arrangement. Their clearances differ. The margin is the width through which the boundary could move before it hits a training example. The next figure marks a centered maximum-margin separator and the two limits of its margin band. The lecture introduces this maximum-margin linear classifier as the simplest kind of SVM (LSVM).

Why several separators can all fit

Imagine two labeled groups with a clear gap between them. Any line through that gap leaves every shown point on its original side, yet the distance from the closest points differs. The margin criterion compares that clearance without changing the feature axes or class labels.

When a straight boundary is not enough

The email example then reaches a pattern for which no single linear classifier covers all instances. The final schematic constructs one such case: squares surround circles, so no straight cut can put every square on one side and every circle on the other. A nonlinear closed boundary can separate this constructed arrangement, as a curved boundary can fit a nonseparable pattern. This teaching geometry illustrates the idea; it is not a reconstruction of the lecture’s plotted records.

Fitting the observed examples still does not settle performance on novel input. That is the generalization issue; see Evaluation and generalization.

Decision trees: tests, branches, and leaves

A decision tree is a flow-chart-like symbolic model. Each internal node tests an attribute, each outgoing branch records a test outcome, and each leaf predicts a class (or can represent a class distribution). With numeric features, successive tests form axis-parallel regions in feature space; each terminal region receives a class label. A one-level tree is a decision stump.

The email examples use the class labels Spam and Ham, and tests on Email Len and New Recip. Read a path by applying each threshold test in order, then use the leaf prediction. The displayed error count beside each leaf counts training examples in that leaf that disagree with its prediction. To get a tree's total training error, add its leaf counts.

The deepest tree is wider than a phone screen. Scroll sideways inside its diagram frame to inspect both sides; you can also focus the frame and use the left and right arrow keys. The page itself stays within the screen width.

Compare tree training-error states

Fixed contextTarget labels: Spam / HamFeatures: Email Len and New RecipTraining-error total = sum of the displayed leaf errorsThe two first stumps are alternatives; states 3 and 4 extend the Email Len stump.

1. Stump A — Email Len at 1.8 (9 errors)

One test gives two terminal predictions. The Spam leaf has 1 error and the Ham leaf has 8, so this state totals 1+8=9 training errors.

2. Alternative stump — New Recip at 2.3 (15 errors)

This is a competing one-test tree, not the next edit to Stump A. Its leaves have 4 and 11 errors: 4+11=15. It is a different split choice.

3. Extend Stump A — split Email Len ≥ 1.8 at 4 (5 errors)

Continue from the first stump. The left Spam leaf remains; the right branch is tested again. The three leaf counts give 1+3+1=5.

4. Split the middle branch on New Recip at 1 (3 errors)

Only the 1.8≤Email Len<4 branch is split. Its leaves now predict Spam below 1 and Ham at or above 1. Summing all four leaves: 1+0+1+1=3.

5. Deeper alternative — nine zero-error leaves

The deeper alternative uses thresholds 1.8, 3, 4, 4, 1, 4, 2, and 3 on the labeled features. Each of its nine leaves has 0 errors; therefore its training total is 9×0=0. This is a training-set result, not evidence by itself that the tree will generalize best.

The first two trees are alternative stumps: Email Len at 1.8 totals 9 errors, while New Recip at 2.3 totals 15. The later 5-error and 3-error trees grow the Email Len stump. The final 0-error tree is a separate, deeper alternative. Its perfect fit to these training examples does not establish that it is the best model for unseen emails; training fit and generalization are different questions.

Common confusions

  • A hypothesis family is the set of model forms a representation permits; it is not the training dataset itself.
  • A decision boundary partitions feature space. Keep the feature axes straight: New Recipients and Email Length.
  • A large margin means clearance from the nearest training points, not merely a visually centered line.
  • “No linear classifier can cover all instances” means the displayed classes are not perfectly separable by one straight line. It does not mean no classifier can make useful predictions.
  • The 9-error and 15-error stumps are alternatives. The 15 is not a step that worsens the 9-error tree.
  • Zero training errors describe the training examples only. Generalization to new email is the central aim, not a consequence guaranteed by a perfect training fit.
  • A schematic class plot does not provide a learner's exact feature values. Do not infer numeric point coordinates or individual record paths.

Revision reminders

  • State the three algorithm components: representation, evaluation, optimization.
  • Contrast numerical and symbolic representations; recognize instance-based, probabilistic graphical, and ensemble families.
  • Explain how a linear score induces a boundary, what the margin measures, and why different separators may all fit.
  • Explain a tree using the terms test, branch, leaf, threshold, prediction, and training error.
  • Reproduce the tree totals in order of the displayed states: 9, 15, 5, 3, 0—remembering that the first two are competing stumps.
  • Separate training fit from expected performance on novel examples.

Practice

1. What are the three components of a machine-learning algorithm?

Check your answer

Representation (the model form), evaluation (how candidates are judged), and optimization (how the search is carried out).

2. A linear score is positive on one side of its zero set and negative on the other. What does the zero set represent?

Check your answer

It is the decision boundary: the locations where the score changes sign and the classifier changes sides.

3. For the Email Len stump at 1.8, what are the two leaf labels and total training errors?

Check your answer

Email Len<1.8 predicts Spam with 1 error; Email Len≥1.8 predicts Ham with 8 errors. Total: 1+8=9.

4. Why is the New Recip stump's total of 15 not the next training-error state after 9?

Check your answer

It is an alternative stump using a different root test (New Recip at 2.3), not a modification of the Email Len stump.

5. A deeper tree has zero displayed training errors. What conclusion is justified, and what remains unknown?

Check your answer

It fits the displayed training examples with zero errors. Its performance on novel emails remains unknown; zero training error does not establish the best generalizing model.
