Reinforcement Learning: MDPs, Values, and Exploration

Follow how actions, delayed rewards, values, and exploration fit together in a finite Markov decision process.

Big picture

Supervised learning uses labeled examples, while unsupervised learning looks for structure in data; reinforcement learning (RL) instead learns what to do by acting and seeing what follows. The agent receives a state and chooses an action; the environment returns an immediate reward and a next state. A move can have no immediate payoff yet still be valuable because it leads toward a later reward. That delay connects interaction, return, value, policy, and exploration.

The lecture motivates RL with a robot docking at a charger, factory control, games, autonomous driving, and robotic pushing. Its Atari illustration describes DeepMind use of DQN to exceed human-level performance on Atari 2600 games. In tic-tac-toe, two machines without previous knowledge learn while playing 20,000 games. TD-Gammon uses +100 for a win, −100 for a loss, 0 for intermediate states, and self-play over 1.5 million games; the lecture reports performance approximately equal to the best human player. These examples highlight delayed reward and active exploration. The lecture also notes that state may be only partially observable and that multiple tasks may share sensors and effectors.

From interaction to a trajectory

At discrete time t, the agent acts from its current state. One interaction produces a transition and reward; the sequence of transitions is a trajectory.

(st,at,rt,st+1)

Here, r_t is the immediate reward associated with taking a_t in s_t. The reward and next state are feedback from the environment, not supplied correct-action labels. The target is a policy π that maps states to actions. Because the best consequence may arrive later, the goal is to maximize expected return from each starting state.

Worked return: an immediate reward versus a delayed one

Teaching example. Compare two choices using γ=0.9 and the lecture’s sequence convention rt+γrt+1+γ2rt+2+⋯ .

  1. Choice A: rt=5, rt+1=0, then no more reward. Return: 5+0.9(0)=5.
  2. Choice B: rt=0, rt+1=10, then no more reward. Return: 0+0.9(10)=9.
  3. Since 9>5, B is preferred under this objective, despite paying nothing immediately.

Check: the t+1 reward is multiplied by γ once; the time-t reward is not discounted.

Finite MDP: what the current state must tell us

The lecture's Markov decision process has a finite state set S and action set A. At each time, the agent observes s_t∈S, chooses a_t∈A, receives r_t, and moves to s_{t+1}. In the deterministic case, the transition and reward are written:

st+1=δ(st,at),rt=r(st,at)

The Markov assumption says the next state and immediate reward depend on the current state and action, rather than needing the full earlier history. The transition δ and reward function r need not be known to the agent. In a nondeterministic environment, they describe distributions or outcomes rather than one fixed result, so values are expectations. If relevant information is hidden, the agent's observation may not be a full Markov state; partial observability is a practical complication.

Worked transition: classify each part of one action

Teaching example. A robot is beside a charger, facing it, with a low battery. It chooses “move forward”; the environment returns the charger location and a positive docking reward.

  1. Current state s_t: beside the charger, facing it, low battery.
  2. Action a_t: move forward.
  3. Next state s_{t+1}: at the charger.
  4. Immediate reward r_t: the docking payoff.

Check: “move forward” is the chosen action, the charger location is the next state, and the score is the reward. The state must include enough relevant facts for the Markov assumption to be reasonable.

Policies, discounted return, and state value

A policy π:S→A specifies which action to take in each state. Following it from s_t generates rewards r_t,r_{t+1},…. The lecture discounts later rewards by γ, with 0≤γ<1, and seeks a policy that maximizes expected return:

Gt=∑i=0∞γirt+i=rt+γrt+1+γ2rt+2+⋯
Vπ(s)=𝔼π[Gt∣st=s],V*(s)=maxπVπ(s)

γ controls how strongly future rewards count. A value is an expected discounted return from a state under a policy; it is not the immediate reward attached to one move. The lecture first discusses deterministic worlds, then extends values to expectations when outcomes are nondeterministic. The condition γ<1 makes a continuing return bounded when rewards are bounded. A finite episodic task can also have a finite return at γ=1, but that is outside the lecture's stated discounted setup.

Derive the six-cell values in dependency order

Givens: 2×3 grid; γ=0.9; entry reward 100 only on top-middle → G and bottom-right → G; other shown moves and G self-loop reward 0; orthogonal shown neighbors only.

1. Set the goal continuation value

The payoff is collected on entry. At G itself the only shown action is the zero-reward self-loop, so set V*(G)=0. Do not add 100 again.

2. Value the two entry states

Top-middle → G and bottom-right → G each pay 100 immediately. For each, 100+0.9(0)=100. These values become dependencies for neighboring cells.

3. Compare top-left actions

Right: 0+0.9(100)=90. Down: 0+0.9(81)=72.9. Therefore V*(top-left)=90 and right is the shown greedy move.

4. Compare bottom-middle actions

Up: 0+0.9(100)=90. Right: 0+0.9(100)=90. Left: 0+0.9(81)=72.9. Therefore V*=90 and up/right tie; the source policy chooses right.

One-step lookahead when the model is known

If the agent knows the deterministic transition δ and reward r, it can evaluate each available action by adding its immediate reward to the discounted value of the resulting state:

π*(s)=argmaxa∈A[r(s,a)+γV*(δ(s,a))]

This is one-step lookahead. It distinguishes the action now from the state value after that action. When δ or r is unknown, a value V alone does not reveal which next state or reward each action produces; the lecture therefore introduces Q(s,a), an action value.

Action values when the model is unknown

The source defines the deterministic action value as immediate reward plus discounted optimal value at the resulting state:

Q(s,a)=r(s,a)+γV*(δ(s,a)),V*(s)=maxa∈AQ(s,a)

Q scores a state–action pair; V scores a state under its best policy. If the agent learns Q, it can select a greedy action by maximizing Q(s,a) without explicitly knowing δ. The maximization is over actions available in the current state. This page introduces that connection; the sampled Q-learning update and its multi-transition examples belong to the linked Q-learning topic.

Exploration and exploitation

Exploitation chooses an action that currently has the largest estimated value. Exploration deliberately randomizes to discover whether another action may lead to better outcomes. Always exploiting can preserve a mistaken early estimate; exploring every time can waste known good choices.

The lecture's ε-greedy rule uses two branches. With probability 1−ε it chooses a greedy action. With probability ε it chooses uniformly from all k available actions, including greedy actions. Thus, for one unique greedy action:

P(greedy)=1−ε+εk,P(each other action)=εk

For m tied greedy actions, use a uniform tie rule in the greedy branch: each maximizing action has probability (1−ε)/m+ε/k, and each nonmaximizing action has probability ε/k. This is a stated tie convention for the calculation. The ε branch is uniform over every action; it is not restricted to nongreedy moves.

Worked ε-greedy choice: source values and draw convention

Source givens. There are two actions from s₁, leading to s₂ or s₃; V(s₁)=10, V(s₂)=50, V(s₃)=20; ε=0.2. Thus s₂ is the unique greedy destination. The random branch draws uniformly from both actions, including the greedy one.

  1. Exploration branch probability is 0.2; within it, P(s2)=12, so its contribution is 0.2×12=0.1.
  2. Greedy branch probability is 1−0.2=0.8, and it selects s₂.
  3. Total P(select s₂)=0.8+0.1=0.9. The other action has probability 0.2×12=0.1.
  4. Check the total: 0.9+0.1=1.

To illustrate the slide’s random-number implementation, take a teaching draw u=0.12. Since u∈[0,0.2], use the random branch, then draw uniformly between the two actions; either may result. For u=0.65, use the greedy branch and choose s₂. The slide includes both interval endpoints; a continuous draw has probability zero of landing exactly on a boundary, so the endpoint convention does not change these probabilities.

Tied greedy actions: calculate the action probabilities

Teaching example. Let k=4,m=2 actions tie for the maximum, and ε=0.2. In the greedy branch, split 0.8 evenly across the two tied actions; in exploration, split 0.2 evenly across all four.

  1. Each maximizing action: 0.82+0.24=0.4+0.05=0.45.
  2. Each other action: 0.24=0.05.
  3. Total: 2(0.45)+2(0.05)=0.9+0.1=1.

This tie split is an explicit uniform greedy tie convention. A policy probability describes selection; it is not a state value or a Q value.

The lecture's simplified value update

For selected greedy moves, the lecture displays this illustrative rule:

V(st)←V(st)+α[V(st+1)−V(st)],0<α<1

α is a learning rate: it sets how far the current estimate moves toward the next state's estimate. This displayed formula contains neither an explicit immediate reward nor a discount factor, so it is not the general reward-and-discount temporal-difference update. Do not silently substitute it for that broader rule.

Worked value update: apply exactly the displayed rule

Supplemental calculation. Given V(st)=10, V(st+1)=50, and α=0.1:

  1. Difference: 50−10=40.
  2. Scaled correction: 0.1×40=4.
  3. New estimate: 10+4=14.

Check: the change is α times the difference, 14−10=0.1(50−10)=4, and the estimate remains between 10 and 50.

Common mistakes

  • Treating immediate reward r_t as the whole return. Future rewards enter with successive powers of γ.
  • Treating V(s) as an immediate reward or as a state–action score. V evaluates a state under a policy; Q evaluates a particular action from a state.
  • Requiring the whole history in a fully observed MDP. The Markov assumption uses the current state and action; a partial observation may fail to encode enough state.
  • Counting the goal-entry reward again at G. The payoff is on a transition into G; the goal self-loop pays 0.
  • Reading a single optimal policy as the only one. The grid has verified ties at bottom-left and bottom-middle.
  • Repeating the printed 72 as exact arithmetic. With γ=0.9 and next value 81, the calculation is 72.9; the slide's reason for printing 72 is not specified.
  • Assuming the ε branch means “choose nongreedy.” The source samples from all actions, including the greedy one.
  • Confusing ε action selection with learning target construction. ε controls behavior probabilities; max Q in a later Q-learning target serves a different role.

Exam focus

  • Name each part of a transition: current state, chosen action, immediate reward, and next state.
  • State the finite MDP and Markov assumptions; explain the partial-observation caveat.
  • Write the source-indexed return beginning r_t+γr_{t+1}+γ²r_{t+2}, and distinguish it from the immediate reward.
  • Define V^π(s) as expected return starting at s and following π; V* is the best policy value.
  • With a known model, compare r(s,a)+γV*(δ(s,a)); with learned Q, choose an action maximizing Q(s,a).
  • For the grid, keep reward-on-transition, value-on-cell, policy arrows, and Q-on-action separate. Use V*(G)=0 after entry reward collection.
  • For ε-greedy, state the uniform random branch includes greedy actions and check the total probability sums to one.
  • Describe the V update as the lecture's simplified displayed rule, not a general reward/discount update.

Practice questions

1. A transition has rt=2 and the next reward is rt+1=10. With γ=0.5 and no later reward, what is G_t?

Check your answer

Gt=2+0.5(10)=7. The time-t reward is undiscounted; the next reward is discounted once.

2. In a deterministic MDP, what information does the Markov assumption say is enough to determine the next-state and reward outcome?

Check your answer

The current state s_t and chosen action a_t. The full preceding history is unnecessary if the state captures all relevant information.

3. Why is V*(G)=0 in the source grid even though entering G pays 100?

Check your answer

The 100 is the immediate reward on the transition into G. Once at G, the source’s self-loop reward is 0, so the entry payoff is not counted a second time.

4. From the top-left grid cell, compare right and down when γ=0.9.

Check your answer

Right: 0+0.9(100)=90. Down: 0+0.9(81)=72.9. Right is greedy by value.

5. With ε=0.2 and four actions, one unique greedy action, what is its total probability under the source rule?

Check your answer

1−0.2+0.24=0.85. Each other action has probability 0.24=0.05; total 0.85+3(0.05)=1.

6. With ε=0.2, four actions, and two tied maxima under uniform tie-breaking, what is each maximizing action’s probability?

Check your answer

1−0.22+0.24=0.45 each. The two other actions each get 0.05, and the total is 1.

7. In the lecture’s simplified V update, let V(s_t)=8, V(s_{t+1})=20, and α=0.25. What is the update?

Check your answer

8+0.25(20−8)=8+3=11. This applies the displayed simplified rule only; no reward or γ term is added.
