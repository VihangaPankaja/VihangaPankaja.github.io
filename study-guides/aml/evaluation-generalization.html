Evaluation, generalization and model choice

How to judge a learner, choose an appropriate model, and interpret what a score can and cannot tell us about new cases.

Big picture

A learning system needs three things to become useful: a representation of the hypotheses it may express, an evaluation rule for comparing candidates, and an optimization or search method for finding a candidate. Evaluation is therefore part of the problem definition: a model that scores well under the wrong measure is still the wrong model.

Even a low training error does not guarantee good predictions on new examples. Model choice balances fit with generalization, guided by evidence and assumptions about the problem. No single learner is best for every possible problem; dimensionality reduction can help or distort; and several quite different models can earn nearly the same score.

Representation, evaluation, and optimization

These are complementary roles, not three names for the same step.

Role Question it answers What the lecture supplies
Representation What forms can the learned knowledge or hypothesis take? Functions, neural networks/graphs, logic programs and rule sets, finite-state machines, grammars, and problem-solving systems.
Evaluation What makes one candidate better for this task? Accuracy, precision/recall, squared error, likelihood, posterior probability, cost/utility, margin, entropy, KL divergence, and related criteria.
Optimization / search How does the learner find or construct a candidate? Combinatorial, convex, and constrained optimization; gradient descent, perceptron and backpropagation; dynamic programming; divide and conquer; evolutionary computation.

The lecture gives examples within these families: greedy search for combinatorial optimization, gradient descent for convex optimization, linear programming for constrained optimization, HMM/PCFG learning with dynamic programming, decision-tree induction and rule learning with divide and conquer, and genetic algorithms, genetic programming, and neuro-evolution within evolutionary computation. These are a map of search families, not a recipe to run one algorithm. A representation says what can be expressed; an evaluation criterion says what to prefer; a search algorithm controls how candidates are found. A learner's success depends on their fit to the task.

Evaluation sits inside a larger learning loop

The lecture's practice sequence begins by understanding the domain, prior knowledge, and goals; integrates, selects, cleans, and preprocesses data; learns models; interprets results; then consolidates and deploys the discovered knowledge. It loops back as the problem or evidence changes. Evaluation supports interpreting results and deciding whether the current model meets the goal; it does not replace a clear goal or careful data preparation.

Read a confusion matrix before calculating

The lecture's binary confusion matrix places actual classes in rows and predicted classes in columns. Pick the positive class for the question, then identify the four cells relative to that choice.

Actual \ Predicted Negative Positive
Negative True negative (TN) False positive (FP)
Positive False negative (FN) True positive (TP)

Precision asks: of the examples predicted positive, what fraction really is positive? Its denominator is the positive prediction column. Recall asks: of the examples actually positive, what fraction was found? Its denominator is the actual positive row.

Precision=TPTP+FP
Recall=TPTP+FN

The source lists accuracy but does not print its formula. The following is a Supplemental formalization used by the labeled Teaching example: accuracy is the fraction of all predictions on the diagonal.

Accuracy=TP+TNall cases

Confusion-matrix metric calculation

Input matrix (actual rows; predicted columns)

Positive class: positive. Keep this input visible while stepping through the calculations.

1. Read cells using actual rows and predicted columns

Teaching example — constructed counts, not lecture data. Suppose rows are actual negative/positive and columns are predicted negative/positive. The matrix is [[5, 1], [2, 6]]. Thus TN = 5, FP = 1, FN = 2, and TP = 6. Check: 5+1+2+6=14 cases.

2. Calculate accuracy

Correct predictions are the diagonal. Divide by all cases: TN+TP14=5+614=1114=0.785714…≈0.786. Independent check: FP+FN=1+2=3 are incorrect, and 14−314=1114, the same result.

3. Calculate precision for the designated positive class

Predicted-positive cases are in the positive column: FP+TP=1+6=7. Of these, 6 are actual positive. Precision=TPTP+FP=66+1=67=0.857142…≈0.857. Column check: 1+6=7.

4. Calculate recall for the designated positive class

Actually-positive cases are in the positive row: FN+TP=2+6=8. Of these, 6 were predicted positive. Recall=TPTP+FN=66+2=68=0.75. Row check: 2+6=8.

Multiclass questions use one-vs-rest counts

For a chosen class in a multiclass table, treat that class as positive and all other classes together as negative. Precision still uses the chosen class's predicted column; recall still uses its actual row. The full table must be read with its stated orientation before either sum is made.

1. In an actual-row/predicted-column multiclass matrix, the predicted “Not a Rumour” column contains 986, 250, 474 and 105 cases; its diagonal count is 474. Treat this class as positive. Calculate precision and identify the denominator.

Check your answer

The matrix orientation is actual rows and predicted columns. For “Not a Rumour” as positive, TP = 474. The predicted “Not a Rumour” column sums to 986+250+474+105=1815. Precision = 4741815=0.261157…≈0.261 to three decimals. Independent check: the class's actual row is [231, 657, 474, 635], so 474 is the diagonal cell; it is not the precision denominator.

2. In an actual-row/predicted-column multiclass matrix, the actual “True Rumour” row contains 313, 740, 953 and 898 cases; its diagonal count is 313. Treat this class as positive. Calculate recall and identify the denominator.

Check your answer

“True Rumour” is positive. Its TP is the diagonal value 313. The actual “True Rumour” row sums to 313+740+953+898=2904. Recall = 3132904=0.107782…≈0.108 to three decimals. Independent check: only about 10.8% of actual True Rumour cases were found.

Training fit and generalization

Training error measures fit to the examples used in learning. Generalization error concerns performance away from that training set; the lecture labels it the off-training-set loss. In practice, a held-out test score estimates performance on new cases only under assumptions about how those cases relate to training data. The same-distribution, independent-sampling assumption is a sampling condition (often abbreviated i.i.d.); it is distinct from No Free Lunch below.

Some earlier lecture wording calls the same-distribution condition a “No Free Lunch rule.” Keep the concepts distinct here: i.i.d. is a sampling assumption for evaluating new cases; No Free Lunch is the later average-risk result about comparing learning algorithms.

The lecture's qualitative chart uses Loss on the vertical axis and Epochs on the horizontal axis. Curve A is generalization error and is U-shaped; curve B is training error and falls. C marks the stopping point. D marks underfitting: the model is too simple and both errors are large. E marks overtraining/overfitting: training fit keeps improving while error on test/generalization cases worsens. The sketch gives no numeric ticks or values, and epochs are not model complexity.

A lower training loss alone is not enough to choose a model. Use validation evidence to select model settings or a stopping point, and reserve a separate test set for a final report when the workflow allows. A test set repeatedly consulted during tuning stops being an untouched final check.

Complexity, Occam, and model choice

Occam's razor (the law of parsimony) says that when explanations make equivalent predictions, prefer one with fewer assumptions. In machine learning this is a guide for comparing adequate explanations, not a command to choose the simplest possible model regardless of fit. The lecture explicitly calls out a tension: predictive accuracy and simplicity/interpretability can conflict.

Model complexity and epochs describe different things. Complexity concerns how expressive or intricate a model is; epochs count passes through training data. The page 68 chart varies epochs to illustrate under/overtraining, while the later model-choice discussion concerns simple versus complex classifiers. In either case, select with evidence for the intended task and data, and explain the trade-off.

No Free Lunch: a scoped result

The lecture presents No Free Lunch as an average over appropriately weighted learning situations, not as a claim that all algorithms tie on every practical task. With no prior information that favors a problem family, one algorithm's advantages are balanced by disadvantages elsewhere under the theorem's stated risk setup. Page 71 defines these terms:

The practical conclusion on page 72 is that prior knowledge about data distribution, prior probabilities, problem complexity, or underlying physics can make one learner a better match for a particular problem.

This does not say that training and test data must be independently drawn from the same distribution. That is a separate evaluation assumption. Nor does the result say that model selection is pointless: it says the choice is justified by assumptions, evidence, and the problem class rather than a universal winner. Wolpert's primary 1996 paper describes its result as the absence of a priori distinctions in particular average off-training-set risk senses; this is the scope used here.

Dimensionality, sparsity, and projection limits

The curse of dimensionality is a data-coverage problem: as the number of feature dimensions grows, the combinations or regions that need examples multiply. With a fixed sample count, examples become sparse across the feature space and predictive power can decline. The slide illustrates one, two, and three dimensions as increasingly subdivided spaces; it gives no sample-count formula or numeric threshold.

Dimensionality reduction can counter this by preserving useful structure in fewer dimensions, when done properly. But a projection removes or combines information. Page 74 warns that it can distort data misleadingly; pages 75–77 use a three-dimensional world and its two-dimensional view to motivate the warning. The projected picture is not automatically the full structure. Validate that the properties important to the task survive the projection.

Rashomon effect and explanation instability

The Rashomon effect describes a set of substantially different explanations that fit the evidence similarly. In the lecture's machine-learning example, many classifiers have about the same minimum error rate. For decision trees, a slight perturbation of training data can yield a quite different tree with almost the same test-set error.

Therefore, a similar score does not imply the same reasoning or explanation. If a model is used to explain decisions, report that multiple near-equivalent explanations may exist and check whether the explanation is stable under small data changes.

Common mistakes

  • Swapping the confusion-matrix axes: rows are actual; columns are predicted.
  • Using the actual-positive row for precision or the predicted-positive column for recall. Precision conditions on predicted positives; recall conditions on actual positives.
  • Calculating a multiclass denominator from the wrong class axis. Convert the selected class to one-vs-rest, then sum its column for precision or row for recall.
  • Treating a training score as an estimate of new-case performance without a sampling assumption or held-out evidence.
  • Confusing the same-distribution/i.i.d. evaluation assumption with the No Free Lunch result.
  • Reading the source's qualitative curves as measured numerical data or treating epochs as model complexity.
  • Applying Occam's razor to discard a more complex model that materially predicts better.
  • Treating dimensionality reduction as lossless, or treating a similar score as proof of a unique stable explanation.

Exam focus

  • Define representation, evaluation, and optimization by their roles.
  • Read the binary confusion matrix with actual rows and predicted columns; state which class is positive.
  • Precision uses TP over the predicted-positive column total. Recall uses TP over the actual-positive row total.
  • In a multiclass matrix, use one-vs-rest for the chosen class.
  • Distinguish training error from generalization error; identify A, B, C, D, E and the underfitting/overfitting regions in the qualitative loss-versus-epochs graph.
  • Scope Occam's razor to comparably predictive explanations; state the accuracy/interpretability trade-off.
  • State No Free Lunch as a properly scoped average result absent assumptions or prior knowledge; keep it separate from i.i.d. sampling.
  • Explain why high-dimensional data become sparse with fixed data, how reduction can help, and why projection can distort.
  • Define the Rashomon effect and explain why equal performance need not mean equal model structure or explanation.

Practice questions

3. A screening system should miss as few actual positive cases as possible. Which confusion-matrix metric directly measures this, and which cells form its denominator?

Check your answer

Recall measures the fraction of actual positives correctly found. Its denominator is the actual-positive row total, TP+FN. Reducing false negatives tends to increase recall.

4. A learner has a very low training error but a much higher held-out error. What pattern does this suggest, and what does the training result alone establish?

Check your answer

It suggests overfitting: training fit is strong while performance away from training is worse. The training result establishes only that the learner fits those training cases; it does not establish generalization.

5. State one key difference between No Free Lunch and the i.i.d. assumption.

Check your answer

No Free Lunch concerns average comparisons between learning algorithms across appropriately weighted problems under a stated risk setup. The i.i.d. assumption concerns how training and evaluation examples are sampled from a distribution. They address different questions.

6. Two very different decision trees have almost the same test error; a small training-data change selects the other tree. What effect is illustrated?

Check your answer

The Rashomon effect: multiple distinct classifiers can perform similarly, and a small data perturbation can change the selected explanation without much changing test error.

7. Why can a useful dimensionality-reduction projection still be dangerous?

Check your answer

It may preserve useful structure in fewer dimensions, but can also remove or distort information in a misleading way. Check that properties relevant to the task remain after projection.
