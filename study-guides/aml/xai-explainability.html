Explainable AI: Questions, Methods, and Presentation

Big picture

An accurate prediction can still be hard to trust when it affects a diagnosis, loan, crop decision, or another important outcome. Modern CNNs, LSTMs, and Transformers may contain millions of parameters and complex internal logic, so a person can see an input and output without being able to follow the calculation in between. Explainable AI (XAI) uses techniques to make model behaviour inspectable and communicable. The useful starting point is a stakeholder question: what needs explaining, to whom, and when?

An explanation can describe the whole model, one prediction, similar past cases, or a change that would flip an output. It can then be shown as a picture, rule, sentence, score, or interactive view. Those choices are separate: no single method or display suits every question.

What is the person trying to understand?

Interpretability is the degree to which a person can follow a model's own operation and see how it arrives at predictions without an external explanation tool. It is a spectrum. A linear regression or decision tree can often be read directly; a deep network is much harder to follow. The lecture's “black-box problem” names this gap: high accuracy does not by itself mean high trust.

Explainability uses a method to translate complex or opaque model behaviour into something a person can inspect and communicate, commonly after the model has produced a prediction. In the lecturer's concise distinction, interpretability is clarity; explainability is translation. A simple model can still benefit from explanation and presentation, and a post-hoc explanation does not make the original model intrinsically transparent.

The same model may need different explanations for a developer, doctor, policy maker, student, or user. First specify the question and audience; then choose the scope and method.

Keep the three method dimensions independent

The lecture gives three distinct ways to describe a method. They answer different questions and should be recorded separately. For example, calling a technique local does not tell us whether it is model-specific, and calling a model a black box does not tell us whether an explanation is global or local.

Global question: Across the diabetes model’s dataset, which patterns matter? The lecture’s illustration names age and BMI as strong overall influencers.

Local question: Why was this patient’s prediction made? In the lecture’s illustration, high sugar level triggered the prediction for that patient.

These are different scopes. The example supplies no numerical model or effect size, so it supports the distinction but no quantitative comparison. Either explanation could still come from a white-box or black-box model, and the method’s dependence must be considered separately.

A decision tree or linear regression is a white-box example; a deep neural network or ensemble is a black-box example in the lecture. Grad-CAM is CNN-specific, while LIME and SHAP are presented as model-agnostic examples. These labels describe model type and method dependence respectively; they do not form one exclusive ranking.

Which input or internal signal is associated with a prediction?

Feature attribution asks which inputs, regions, tokens, or internal neurons are associated with a selected output. Depending on the method and data, the unit can be a tabular feature, image region or pixel, word, audio region, or neuron. Attribution is often local to a prediction and a specified output; aggregating across cases can describe a dataset, but a signed mean may hide cases with opposing directions. Sign and magnitude convey different information, and neither alone establishes a causal effect.

Can a readable model imitate the complex model?

A surrogate is a simpler interpretable model trained to approximate another model's behaviour. It uses the same inputs as the complex model and treats that model's predictions as imitation targets. The target is therefore the black box's output, not ground-truth labels and not a transcript of its exact internal reasoning.

Worked reasoning: a small tree approximates a loan model

Suppose a complex loan model returns approve/reject predictions for applicant inputs. Give the same applicant inputs and those model predictions to a small decision-tree surrogate. The tree is fitted to reproduce the complex model’s outputs as closely as its chosen training sample and structure allow; one can then inspect its branch rules.

This explains the surrogate’s approximation. It does not establish why the black box internally produced each output, whether the surrogate agrees everywhere, or whether either model is correct against real outcomes. In the source’s notation, the surrogate output y^′ should approximate the black-box output y^.

The lecture names LIME as a local linear surrogate and decision-tree surrogates as global approximations. It also names RuleFit for rule-based explanations, Anchors for high-precision decision rules, and Probing as an MLP classifier used to measure what a layer encodes.

Which past cases resemble this prediction?

An example-based explanation selects relevant cases from training data so a person can compare a new prediction with familiar examples. Search may use distance in input space or embedding space, similarity patterns, or influence scores. A prototype is a representative example; a criticism is an example the model finds difficult; influential samples are training cases that affected a prediction. The selected comparison is an explanation through resemblance, not necessarily the model’s actual decision path.

Worked reasoning: the yellow query and its five nearest neighbours

In the lecture’s schematic, a yellow new point is classified as red. Select its five nearest training samples using Euclidean distance; the diagram shows that most of those five are red. The resulting explanation is: “The model predicted red because it resembles these examples.”

The figure gives no exact coordinates or distances, so none are needed to follow the reasoning. In another task, the distance could be measured in an embedding space; the meaning of “similar” depends on the chosen representation and metric. The lecture also names k-nearest neighbours, prototype-and-criticism selection, influence functions, embedding-space search, and case-based reasoning.

What input change would flip the model’s output?

A counterfactual explanation compares the original input with a changed input that produces a different model output. The lecture’s workflow is to start with the original, try small realistic variations, query the model, and present a minimal, realistic change that flips the prediction. Contrastive explanations answer a related “why this rather than that?” question. Actionable recourse adds a need to consider which changes a person can actually make.

Named methods include Wachter counterfactuals, DiCE, actionable recourse methods, and contrastive explanations.

What can the model’s own structure or signals show?

Some models expose structure that can be inspected directly. A tree gives a rule path; a linear model exposes weights. The lecture also points to CNN filters/activations and Transformer attention maps as useful internal views. A visible signal is evidence to examine, not automatically a faithful human rationale, causal explanation, or fairness guarantee.

Worked reading: follow the decision-tree branch shown in the lecture

The tree first tests X2≤5.0. Follow the matching branch:

  • When X2≤5.0, test whether X1≤4.5 holds. A true result reaches LM1. Otherwise, test X2≤2.5: true reaches LM2; false reaches LM3.
  • When X2>5.0, test whether X1≤7.5 holds. A false result, X1>7.5, reaches LM4. A true result leads to X2≤8.0: true reaches LM5; false reaches LM6.

Each branch condition narrows the path to a leaf. The figure’s thresholds and leaf labels are the explanation; it gives no new applicant values to classify. Follow this path only for the tree represented in the figure.

Taxonomy clarification: the lecture groups CNN Grad-CAM with internal model signals on this slide. Grad-CAM uses gradients and convolutional activations, but it is still a post-hoc attribution procedure; it should not be called an intrinsically interpretable predictor.

How should the explanation be presented?

An explanation is not automatically understanding. Even a white-box path, attention map, score list, or counterfactual can fail if it is too technical, confusing, or mismatched to the audience. The same output may need a different presentation for a developer and a patient. Presentation is the bridge between a model signal and a person’s ability to reason with it.

Supplemental reading on attention. The lecture lists attention maps as a possible internal view. Research has debated when those weights support an explanation: Jain and Wallace (2019) report limits for standard attention as a rationale, while Wiegreffe and Pinter (2019) argue that conclusions depend on how explanation is defined and tested. The practical lesson here is to label an attention map as a displayed model signal and validate any stronger claim.

Worked choice: match a stakeholder and question to a display

A developer asking how model errors vary across tasks can use a visual heatmap or numerical comparison to inspect patterns. A person asking why one decision occurred needs a local explanation, such as a clearly labeled feature display, a tree path, or a counterfactual, with wording suited to that person. A text-input explanation may show selected tokens or a short generated sentence. If a user benefits from spoken feedback, an interactive multimodal view can combine text, audio, and image.

These formats communicate different evidence. A heatmap’s color should be accompanied by labels or a scale; a highlighted token should identify whether it displays attention or contribution; a numerical score needs its target and reference context. Clear presentation helps a human inspect an explanation, but it cannot by itself prove the explanation faithful or the conclusion correct.

Common mistakes

  • Collapsing the three axes: global/local, white/black-box, and specific/agnostic answer independent questions. Record all three where known.
  • Treating higher scores as the only story: signed attribution can be negative; direction and magnitude differ, and a mean can cancel mixed case-level values.
  • Reading attribution as causation: attribution describes a model-relative association for an output and method, not what would happen in the real world after changing a feature.
  • Calling a surrogate the original model: it is trained on black-box outputs as imitation targets. It approximates behavior over its sampled region and does not reveal exact internal reasoning.
  • Treating similar examples as the model’s path: example-based methods compare selected cases; the similarity metric and representation matter.
  • Treating a counterfactual as a promise: the example reports a changed model output under one stated input change. It does not prove feasibility, causal impact, fairness, or guaranteed recourse.
  • Calling every internal signal intrinsic explanation: attention, weights, and activations need interpretation and validation; Grad-CAM is post-hoc even though it uses internal CNN information.
  • Equating explanation and understanding: the audience, purpose, and display determine whether a person can use what is shown.
  • Over-reading the source’s plots: the Titanic contribution chart does not define a baseline or scale, and the nearest-neighbor schematic gives no coordinates or distances.

Exam focus

  • Explain why accuracy alone does not settle trust in a high-stakes prediction.
  • Distinguish built-in interpretability from post-hoc explainability and recognize interpretability as a spectrum.
  • Classify an explanation on three independent axes: global/local, white/black box, and model-specific/model-agnostic.
  • For each family, name the question it answers: attribution (what inputs/signals?), surrogate (can a simple model imitate?), example-based (which cases resemble it?), counterfactual (what input change flips it?), and intrinsic structure (what can be read directly?).
  • State that a surrogate learns from black-box predictions; it does not learn ground-truth labels unless those are separately used.
  • Preserve the distinction between an output change in a counterfactual and a causal intervention or feasible recourse.
  • Match visual, symbolic, textual, numerical, and interactive/multimodal formats to the evidence and audience.
  • Remember that a presented explanation does not automatically establish human understanding, faithfulness, correctness, causality, or fairness.

Practice questions

1. Why can a highly accurate model still need an explanation?

Check your answer

Accuracy reports predictive performance under an evaluation setup; it does not show a person how a particular decision was reached or make the model’s internal operation understandable. This matters when decisions affect people or resources.

2. A report explains the whole diabetes dataset, uses a deep neural network, and queries no internal structure. Which dimensions does that describe?

Check your answer

Global describes the scope. Black box describes the model type. Model-agnostic describes the method’s dependence if it can be used across model types. These are separate labels; the report does not give enough information to infer a method’s dependence merely from “global.”

3. What does a surrogate use as its imitation target, and what does that target not establish?

Check your answer

It uses the complex model’s predictions for the same inputs as targets. This aims to imitate black-box outputs; it does not establish agreement with ground-truth labels or reveal the black box’s exact internal reasoning.

4. In the source nearest-neighbor example, why is the yellow point labeled red?

Check your answer

The five most similar training samples are selected using Euclidean distance, and most are red. The lecture’s explanation is that the query resembles these examples; it supplies no exact coordinates or distances.

5. In the loan counterfactual, which values change and what is the proper interpretation?

Check your answer

Salary changes from 50,000 to 60,000; debt remains 20,000 and credit score remains 580. The model changes from Rejected to Approved in the illustration. This is a conditional model-output example, not a causal or feasibility guarantee.

6. Name three distinct ways to present an explanation and one suitable example for each.

Check your answer

Visual: a heatmap for image regions. Symbolic: a decision-tree path. Textual: a short natural-language reason or highlighted token. Other valid forms include numerical score tables and interactive or multimodal dashboards.

7. Why is Grad-CAM not automatically an intrinsically interpretable model?

Check your answer

It uses gradients and internal convolutional activations to create a post-hoc attribution map. The procedure depends on CNN internals, but the existence of an internal signal does not make the predictor itself inherently transparent or prove the map is faithful or causal.
