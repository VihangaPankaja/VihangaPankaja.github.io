Unsupervised Learning: Clustering and Evaluation

Learn how clustering discovers structure without target labels, how k-means and hierarchical methods form groups, and how to judge whether the resulting clusters are useful.

Big picture

Supervised learning uses a target attribute to connect observations with answers and predict targets for new cases. Unsupervised learning starts without that target. It looks for structure already present in the observations. Clustering groups nearby or otherwise similar observations and separates observations judged different under a chosen proximity rule.

The lecture uses a map of cholera deaths to illustrate how a spatial pattern can suggest polluted wells, and shows image segmentation, customer groups, clothing sizes, and document organization as applications. The intended use determines what “similar” should mean: groups useful for tailoring may not be the same as groups useful for targeted marketing. Clustering is one unsupervised task; association discovery is another, but this guide focuses on clustering.

Proximity defines what “near” means

A clustering method needs a representation of each observation, a proximity measure, a criterion for judging groups, and an algorithm. A similarity score is better when it is larger; a distance or dissimilarity is better when it is smaller. The metric is a modeling choice, not a neutral detail.

For vectors with d features, Euclidean distance measures the straight-line distance. Manhattan distance adds the absolute change along each feature, like a path along city blocks. Both are Minkowski metrics for p≥1: p=2 gives Euclidean and p=1 gives Manhattan. Features measured on very different scales can dominate distance, so scaling or standardizing may be needed before clustering.

dE(𝐱i,𝐱j)=∑r=1d(xi,r−xj,r)2,dM(𝐱i,𝐱j)=∑r=1d|xi,r−xj,r|
dp(𝐱i,𝐱j)=(∑r=1d|xi,r−xj,r|p)1/p

Euclidean distance is translation-invariant: adding the same displacement vector to both observations leaves their difference, and therefore their distance, unchanged.

The illustration compares path shapes, not sample values. In more than two dimensions the same definitions sum over every feature. The source describes Manhattan distance as an approximation to Euclidean distance; the two formulas remain distinct, and they can rank candidate neighbors differently.

Cluster quality and the number of groups

Two common ideas are cohesion (points within a cluster are close to its center or to one another) and separation (different clusters are far apart). The lecture’s page-10 slide reverses the words “maximized” and “minimized” beside these distance definitions. For k-means, minimize within-cluster sum of squared error (SSE); for centroid-based separation, seek a large distance between centroids. Clustering methods do not all optimize these same measures.

Even with a quality measure, a dataset may support more than one useful grouping. The lecture’s sketch can be read as either two or three clusters. One approach fixes a chosen number k in advance; another compares results under a criterion that may allow the number of groups to vary. Domain knowledge matters because a compact mathematical grouping is not automatically a useful one.

SSE=∑j=1k∑𝐱∈Cjdist(𝐱,𝐦j)2

Here Cj is cluster j, and 𝐦j is its mean vector when the method uses centroids.

Clustering families

The lecture groups techniques broadly into partitional, hierarchical, and Bayesian families. A partitional method returns a flat set of groups, commonly all at once. A hierarchical method builds nested groups that can be displayed as a dendrogram. A Bayesian method seeks a posterior distribution over possible partitions. The taxonomy also names model-based, graph-theoretic, spectral, decision-based, and nonparametric branches.

K-means is a centroid-based partitional method. Hierarchical algorithms can build a hierarchy bottom-up by merging groups (agglomerative) or top-down by splitting them (divisive). A flat method can also be used repeatedly inside a divisive procedure, so the families describe different ways to organize the result rather than incompatible data types.

Other named clustering families include density-based, subspace, large-scale, neural, fuzzy, and co-clustering approaches.

K-means: assign, average, repeat

K-means asks for a fixed k and represents each cluster by its centroid, the mean of its assigned vectors. It starts with k seed centers, assigns every observation to its closest center, recomputes each center from the current memberships, and repeats the assignment and update steps. Under squared Euclidean distance, each assignment chooses the nearer center and each mean update minimizes the within-cluster squared distances for those fixed assignments.

The loop can stop when memberships stop changing (or change very little), centers stop moving (or move very little), or the decrease in SSE is sufficiently small. These are alternative stopping rules. A small change in SSE does not prove that the global best clustering has been found.

Cj={𝐱i:j=argmin1≤q≤kdist(𝐱i,𝐦q)},𝐦j=1|Cj|∑𝐱∈Cj𝐱

K-means limitations and practical choices

The mean is defined for numerical features; for categorical values the lecture briefly notes a mode-based analogue, k-modes. K-means is simple and often efficient; the lecture gives a time complexity of O(tkn) for t iterations, k clusters, and n observations, treating feature dimension and distance-computation cost as fixed. When k and t are small relative to the dataset size, this is often treated as linear in n. K-means remains popular for its simplicity and efficiency, although no clustering method is best for every dataset. Its result depends on the initial seeds, and with the SSE objective it reaches a local optimum, not a guaranteed global one.

Because the mean moves toward extreme values, outliers can distort a center. The lecture suggests checking unusually distant points over several iterations before deciding to remove them, or using a random sample to select centers and then assigning the remaining data. K-means also favors roughly compact, centroid-representable groups and can split a curved or elongated natural group incorrectly. Its requested k is a modeling choice, not something the algorithm discovers automatically.

When two centers are exactly tied for a point, this guide’s declared implementation policy is to assign that point to the lowest-indexed center. When a cluster becomes empty, the declared policy is to retain its previous center for that update and report the empty group; if it stays empty, reconsider k or restart. Neither rule is claimed as a lecturer-specified convention. Scaling, distance, parameter choice, and interpretation should be selected for the application.

Hierarchical clustering: nested groups

A hierarchical result is a nested sequence of clusters represented by a tree, or dendrogram. It can answer several “how many groups?” questions by cutting the tree at different levels. Agglomerative clustering starts with each observation alone and repeatedly merges the nearest pair of clusters until one root remains. Divisive clustering starts with one root containing all observations and repeatedly splits it until singleton groups remain. A fixed-size partitional method can be used for a split; the lecture sketches setting a split count of two and applying the procedure recursively. A split can consider the whole data structure at once, while a greedy merge initially compares pairwise distances; the lecture notes that agglomerative methods are generally faster, while divisive methods may be less blind to global structure.

In clustering, each internal node groups its child observations or earlier clusters, and every root-to-leaf path shows progressively narrower groups. In the lecture’s five-point example, the internal numbers label groups; they are not linkage distances.

Linkage defines the distance between two clusters

An agglomerative algorithm needs a rule for comparing groups, not only individual points. Single link takes the closest cross-cluster pair. It can preserve elongated or irregular shapes, but a few noisy bridge points may chain otherwise separate groups together. Complete link takes the farthest cross-cluster pair. It favors compact groups, but a distant outlier can dominate and elongated groups can be split. Average link averages every cross-cluster point-pair distance, often balancing single-link chaining against complete-link sensitivity. Centroid link measures the distance between the two cluster means. These choices can produce different merge sequences. Single-link merging can be represented by a minimum spanning tree on the observations: cutting its edges above a chosen distance threshold yields the same single-link clusters.

dsingle(A,B)=minx∈A,y∈B‖x−y‖,dcomplete(A,B)=maxx∈A,y∈B‖x−y‖
daverage(A,B)=1|A||B|∑x∈A∑y∈B‖x−y‖,dcentroid(A,B)=‖μA−μB‖

Evaluation: ask what evidence is available

Clustering has no universally known “correct” partition for a real unlabeled dataset. User inspection can examine centroids and spreads, inspect rules that describe groups, or read representative documents. Evaluation also depends on the application and the method’s assumptions.

External evaluation uses known class labels after clustering. Its reference-partition assumption is that each class is a cluster. This is an evaluation assumption, not a guarantee that the classes form compact geometric groups in every application. The labels must not guide the clustering if the goal is an unbiased comparison of the unsupervised grouping. A contingency table records how many members of each class land in each cluster and can support measures such as entropy, purity, precision, recall, and F-score. Internal evaluation uses the data and the resulting groups, such as within-cluster SSE or between-center separation. Indirect evaluation asks whether the clustering improves a downstream task, such as book recommendations. Good performance on labeled benchmark data does not guarantee good structure for a different unlabeled application.

External evaluation with entropy and purity

Entropy measures how mixed the class labels are inside one cluster. Let pij be the proportion of class j inside cluster i. A zero proportion contributes 0log20=0 by continuity. Lower entropy means less class mixing. Purity is the largest class proportion in a cluster; higher purity means one class dominates. Both are averaged across clusters with cluster-size weights.

H(Di)=−∑j=1qpijlog2pij,Htotal=∑i|Di||D|H(Di)
purity(Di)=maxjpij,Ptotal=∑i|Di||D|purity(Di)

Common mistakes

  • Treating clustering labels as ground-truth class labels. Cluster IDs are group names; their numeric labels have no semantic meaning.
  • Saying “maximize cohesion” without specifying a quantity. For within-cluster distance or SSE, lower is better; for a similarity score, higher is better.
  • Recomputing a centroid before holding its current membership fixed, or forgetting that the next assignment uses the updated center.
  • Assuming the requested number of clusters or the initial seeds do not affect the result.
  • Assuming every clustering method optimizes SSE, or that one metric captures application usefulness.
  • Reading internal cluster numbers in a dendrogram as numeric linkage heights.
  • Confusing fixed-center nearest-cluster prediction with a K-means fitting update.
  • Reporting high purity as proof of meaningful unsupervised groups; singleton clusters expose the metric’s limitation.
  • Treating performance with labels as a guarantee for a different unlabeled application.

Exam focus

  • Define unsupervised learning and clustering; explain why the target labels are absent during clustering.
  • Distinguish similarity (larger is more alike) from distance (smaller is more alike); write Euclidean, Manhattan, and Minkowski distance.
  • State that k-means minimizes within-cluster SSE; when centroid separation is used, larger distance between centroids indicates greater separation.
  • Trace k-means in order: initialize, assign to nearest center, recompute coordinate means, test a stopping criterion, repeat.
  • Name the effects of k, initialization, outliers, scaling, noncompact shapes, local optima, empty clusters, and ties.
  • Contrast agglomerative bottom-up merging with divisive top-down splitting.
  • Define single, complete, average, and centroid linkage and state a characteristic limitation or effect.
  • Distinguish external ground-truth, internal, and indirect evaluation.
  • Calculate per-cluster entropy and purity, then apply cluster-size weights to the totals; state why these scores do not establish semantic usefulness.

Practice questions

1. A dataset has no target column. What does an unsupervised clustering method try to discover?

Check your answer

It tries to organize observations into groups that are similar under a chosen representation and proximity rule. The group meaning still needs interpretation for the application.

2. For fixed cluster memberships, what is the k-means center update and what does it minimize?

Check your answer

For cluster Cj, set 𝐦j=|Cj|−1∑𝐱∈Cj𝐱. It is the coordinate-wise mean and minimizes the sum of squared Euclidean distances to that center for those fixed members.

3. In an agglomerative method, what changes when single link is replaced by complete link?

Check your answer

The cross-cluster distance changes from the closest pair to the farthest pair. Single link can chain groups through a bridge; complete link favors compact groups and can be sensitive to outliers. The merge choices may change.

4. For a cluster with 9 items from class A and 1 from class B, calculate its purity.

Check your answer

Its class proportions are 9/10 and 1/10. The largest is 9/10=0.9, so purity is 0.9.

5. A clustering has excellent purity on a labeled benchmark. What can and cannot be concluded about an unlabeled production dataset?

Check your answer

The result gives some evidence about agreement with labels on that benchmark. It does not guarantee the same algorithm, metric, or parameters will find useful groups in a different unlabeled dataset.

6. Why is an internal cluster number in a dendrogram different from its linkage distance?

Check your answer

The cluster number is an identifier for a group in the tree. A linkage distance is a numeric measure of how far apart two clusters were when they merged; the two values play different roles.

7. What is the difference between external, internal, and indirect clustering evaluation?

Check your answer

External evaluation compares clusters with labels or other outside information; internal evaluation uses the data geometry and cluster structure; indirect evaluation checks how much clustering improves a downstream task.

8. A newly observed point is assigned to the nearest of two already computed centroids. Has K-means been refit?

Check your answer

No. This is nearest-centroid prediction with fixed centers. A fitting pass would also assign the training observations and recompute the means from the current memberships.
