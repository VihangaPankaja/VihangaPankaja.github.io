Time Series Analysis and Forecasting

A forecast is only as trustworthy as its time ordering, information boundary, and error measure. This guide moves from recognizing temporal structure to building and evaluating leakage-safe forecasts.

Big picture

Tomorrow’s electricity demand, exchange rate, or temperature cannot be forecast by treating observations as an unordered pile. A time series is an ordered record; past values and other information available at a forecast origin provide context for future values. The useful questions are: what pattern repeats or changes, what information was available at the time of prediction, how far ahead is the forecast, and how should uncertainty and error be described?

The lecture’s motivating questions are prompts, not forecasts. A reliable workflow first identifies the kind of series, studies its components and dependence, makes preprocessing and splits respect time, forms input windows, and evaluates the resulting forecasts at the intended horizons.

Recognize the data before modeling

A time series is a sequence measured at successive, evenly spaced time intervals, such as hourly, daily, weekly, monthly, or yearly. Write the ordered observations as

Y=(y1,y2,…,yT),yt=the value observed at time t.

Time order matters because nearby observations may depend on one another, and the process may change through distribution shifts, concept drift, or structural breaks. The lecture’s examples span finance (prices, exchange and interest rates), meteorology (temperature, rainfall, air pressure), retail demand, economic measures, IoT sensors and electricity, traffic, and healthcare counts or measurements. Its illustrated real-world examples are an LKR/USD movement over six months (Central Bank of Sri Lanka), Sri Lankan annual inflation from 1960–2024 (World Bank), daily Sri Lankan electricity generation by source over 30 days (Ceylon Electricity Board), and monthly Sri Lankan tourism revenue over a year (Trading Economics).

One variable recorded over time is univariate (for example, a stock’s daily closing price). Two or more variables recorded together are multivariate (the slide’s example is rainfall, surface temperature, and precipitation for a location). A panel time series records one or more variables for multiple entities over time, such as monthly sales for several stores. In a panel, observations have both a time index and an entity identity.

Read the four component lenses

The four components describe different aspects of a temporal record. They help distinguish long-run direction, regular repetition, longer variable oscillations, and short unpredictable deviations. Use these lenses to interpret a plot before choosing a decomposition model.

A trend is the long-term general direction, apart from short-term fluctuations. It may be upward, downward, roughly horizontal, or nonlinear (for example, exponential, logarithmic, or polynomial). A seasonal component repeats at a fixed period, often because of calendar or natural timing: electricity can vary by hour of day, retail sales by weekday, expenditure within a month, or tourism by season of the year. Its repeat period is fixed for the pattern being described.

A cycle is a longer oscillation around the trend whose duration is not fixed and whose peak heights and trough depths may vary. Business expansions and recessions and industry cycles are examples. The slide says cycles are “typically 2–10+ years”; this is a heuristic illustration, not a universal duration rule. Cycles are hard to isolate because their timing varies, they overlap irregular variation, and long histories are needed. Seasonality is shorter, calendar-linked, and more predictable; a cycle is longer, less regular, and often tied to economic conditions.

The irregular component, also called noise or residual, is the short-term unpredictable variation not attributed to the other patterns. It can reflect natural disasters, policy changes, random or measurement errors, and unexpected events. The slide says it is often assumed to have a normal distribution with mean zero; that is a simplifying modeling assumption, not a definition or guarantee. Random fluctuations and extreme event outliers are both irregular variations, though an outlier may need separate investigation.

For optional practice, use the AirPassengers series to identify and describe its components.

Stationarity and transformations

Stationarity asks whether a process’s statistical behavior is stable through time. A useful precise version is weak (covariance) stationarity: the mean and finite variance do not change with time, and covariance depends on the separation (lag) between observations rather than their absolute dates. Strict stationarity is stronger: every finite collection’s joint distribution is unchanged by shifting all its time indices together. These definitions are related but are not interchangeable in general. A visible trend or changing seasonal pattern is a warning sign, but a plot alone cannot prove or disprove stationarity.

The lecture introduces the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test. Its null hypothesis is that a unit root is present; its alternative is no unit root. A small p-value (the slide uses 0.05) is evidence against that null under the selected test regression and lag specification. It is not proof of every stationarity condition. The regression’s deterministic terms matter: an ADF specification may include no constant/trend, a constant, a constant plus linear trend, or a constant plus linear and quadratic trend. State what was included when interpreting an ADF result. Statsmodels’ ADF reference documents the null, alternative, and deterministic-term choices; Berkeley’s dependence notes distinguish weak and strict stationarity.

Transform only when the data domain and goal support it

The lecture suggests logarithm or square-root transformations to reduce large differences between values and stabilize variance. A real-valued logarithm requires positive values; the ordinary real square root requires nonnegative values. These transformations do not guarantee stationarity. Differencing subtracts one observation from its predecessor:

Δyt=yt−yt−1,Δ2yt=Δyt−Δyt−1.

The lecture presents first differences as removing a linear trend and second differences as removing a quadratic trend. This holds for an appropriate deterministic polynomial-trend model; differencing does not guarantee stationarity and repeated differencing can remove meaningful structure. Examine the transformed series and retain the transformation needed for the intended model.

Worked example: first and second differences

Teaching example. Let the observed values be [5, 8, 13, 20]. First differences: 8−5=3,13−8=5,20−13=7, giving [3, 5, 7]. Differencing again: 5−3=2,7−5=2, giving [2, 2]. The constant second difference is consistent with a quadratic sequence in this example. This check illustrates the operation; it does not show that arbitrary series become stationary after two differences.

Lag, autocorrelation, ACF, and PACF

A lag is how many time steps we look backward. Lag 1 compares the current value with the immediately previous one; lag k pairs values k steps apart. The lecture defines autocorrelation as correlation between a series and its lagged version:

Corr(yt,yt−k),k=1,2,3,…

Estimating autocorrelation from a finite sample requires choices about centering, normalization and missing observations; state those conventions when comparing computed values. The ACF summarizes overall correlation at each lag, including effects transmitted through intermediate lags. The PACF describes association at a particular lag after accounting for intermediate lags. Positive values indicate positive correlation, negative values negative correlation, and values near zero weak or no correlation in that diagnostic.

The Los Angeles traffic-accident example, adapted from Ye, Hu, and Wang (2020), pairs a time-series plot with its ACF and PACF. Look for patterns across lags; an illustrative plot alone does not establish exact bar values or a model order.

Define the forecast and its horizon

Forecasting predicts future values from historical observations by modeling underlying patterns. It supports decisions, planning, and resource allocation. At origin time t, a forecast h steps ahead can be written as:

y^t+h=f(yt,yt−1,yt−2,…,y0),

where h is the forecast horizon, f is the forecasting model, the input sequence is past context, and the output is one or more future values. In practice, a finite lookback window is often used. A one-step forecast has h=1; a multi-step forecast predicts through horizon H.

For multiple steps, the recursive approach repeatedly predicts one step and feeds its prediction into the next step. The direct approach fits a separate model for each horizon. A multiple-output approach fits one model to predict all requested horizons at once. These are different forecasting designs, not different names for the same evaluation procedure.

Horizon labels depend on the data frequency and decision. The lecture’s examples are: short term, hours to days (up to weeks) for operations and quick response such as electricity load, traffic, daily sales, and weather; mid term, weeks to several months for tactical planning, production, inventory, or seasonal energy demand; long term, several months to years for strategy such as economic growth, infrastructure, and climate trends.

1. Fix the origin and observed history

Teaching example: use the series [10, 12, 13, 15, 18, 20] and issue a two-step forecast immediately after index 4. The observed history ends at 15; values 18 and 20 are still future targets.

2. Predict one step ahead

Use the persistence baseline: predict the most recently available value. Thus y^5=15.

At this fixed origin, index 5 actual (18) is not available to the model. Feed the first prediction 15 back as the next input, so y^6=15.

4. Compare with the held-out targets

Actuals are [18, 20]; predictions are [15, 15]. Absolute errors: [|18−15|,|20−15|]=[3,5]; squared errors: [9, 25].

5. Summarize and check

MAE=(3+5)/2=4. RMSE=(9+25)/2=17≈4.1231. Check: the second prediction used the previous forecast, not the unavailable actual 18.

Split chronologically and prevent leakage

Time series are not split like an exchangeable bag of examples. Random shuffling can put later information into training while earlier periods appear in validation or test. Split in chronological order: train → validation → test. The lecture gives 70:10:20 as a typical ratio, not a law. Fit model choices and learned preprocessing on training data; use validation to select choices; reserve the final test period for an honest end evaluation. At a simulated forecast origin, only information available by that origin may enter a feature, imputation, smoothing, scaling, or model fit.

Six-observation forecasting trace

Synthetic teaching series y=[10,12,13,15,18,20] · chronological indices 1–6 · window length 2 · persistence baseline

1. Split by time; do not shuffle

Synthetic teaching series: indices 1–6 have values [10, 12, 13, 15, 18, 20]. Train indices 1–3 = [10, 12, 13]; validation index 4 = [15]; test indices 5–6 = [18, 20]. The boundaries stay in chronological order.

2. Fit min–max parameters on train only

Training minimum = 10 and maximum = 13; range: 13−10=3. Fit z=x−1013−10 from training values only. Do not inspect validation/test extrema to refit this transform.

3. Apply the fixed transform

Training: 10↦0,12↦23,13↦1. Validation: 15↦53. Test: 18↦83,20↦103. Values above 1 are valid: the fixed training transform is applied unchanged, with no clipping and no refit.

4. Make length-2 one-step windows

For each target, use the preceding two values available at its one-step origin: train [10,12]↦13; validation [12,13]↦15; test [13,15]↦18 and [15,18]↦20. Each input ends before its target.

5. Predict at rolling one-step test origins

Persistence predicts the last observed value. At origin 4, input [13, 15] gives y^5=15 for index 5 (actual 18). After index 5 is observed, at origin 5 input [15, 18] gives y^6=18 for index 6 (actual 20). Predictions [15, 18], actuals [18, 20].

6. Compute errors and verify

Absolute errors [3, 2]; squared errors [9, 4]. MAE=(3+2)/2=2.5; MSE=(9+4)/2=6.5; RMSE=6.5≈2.5495097568. MAPE=100%×((3/18)+(2/20))/2=13.3333333333%. Source-formula sMAPE=100%×((3/16.5)+(2/19))/2=14.3540669856%. Check: RMSE2=MSE up to rounding; each rolling prediction uses only observations available at that origin, and the MAPE actual denominators are nonzero.

For this synthetic six-observation example, MAPE is 13.3333%: 3/18=0.1667,2/20=0.1,(0.1667+0.1)/2=0.1333,100%×0.1333=13.3333%.

The rolling one-step evaluation above updates its observed history after each test point arrives. That availability rule differs from the fixed-origin recursive forecast: when forecasting several steps at once from the end of index 4, the actual at index 5 is unknown and cannot replace the first prediction.

Optional exercises

Use AirPassengers monthly passenger counts from 1949–1960 for three practice activities: separate trend, seasonal, cyclical, and irregular components; preprocess an example with injected missing values, noise, and outliers (including normalization, smoothing, imputation, augmentation, feature/window creation, and batch processing); then train and evaluate forecasting models.

Preprocess with the forecast origin in mind

Preprocessing can make records usable and patterns easier to learn, but every operation must obey the information boundary. A valid technique can still leak if it uses observations that would not yet exist at prediction time.

Missing values and smoothing

Missing observations can result from sensor failures, recording errors, or transmission loss; gaps break continuity and may bias analysis or lower forecast accuracy. The lecture lists mean, median, or mode imputation; forward fill (carry the last valid value); backward fill (use the next valid value); linear or spline interpolation; and deep approaches such as autoencoders, BRITS (bidirectional recurrent imputation for time series), and GAN-based imputation. Interpolation and bidirectional methods can use future values. Split first, then still check each simulated forecast origin: a missing input at time t cannot be filled using observations after t. Backward fill is therefore not automatically causal.

Noise is unwanted random variation that can obscure patterns. Moving averages or rolling means average a sliding window; exponential smoothing gives more weight to recent observations. Centered smoothing can use future points, so it is useful for retrospective visualization but not a causal forecast-time feature unless those future points are genuinely available.

Outliers

An outlier departs strongly from the expected pattern and may come from a rare event, a measurement error, or a sudden change. It can distort a trend and mislead a forecast. The lecture lists Z-scores and the interquartile range (IQR) applied to residuals as detection approaches; handling options include imputation (mean, median, interpolation) and smoothing (moving average, exponential smoothing). Investigate what produced the point before replacing it: a genuine event may matter for future decisions. Any learned threshold or imputation parameter must be fitted without future targets.

Normalization

Scaling can improve gradient stability and keep large-range variables from dominating smaller-range predictors. The lecture gives min–max scaling and z-score standardization:

zminmax=x−xminxmax−xmin,zscore=x−μσ.

Fit a scaler using only the training portion and apply those fixed parameters to validation and test. A held-out value may transform outside [0,1]; clipping or refitting is an additional choice and changes the stated transform. Handle a zero training range separately because the min–max denominator would be zero.

Augmentation and feature engineering

Augmentation creates modified or synthetic training examples to increase diversity and may improve robustness. The slide gives jittering (small random value changes) and scaling (change series magnitude while preserving its temporal shape). Augmentation is optional and is usually considered when data are small or lack diversity. Generate training variants only from training data; never augment by borrowing validation or test targets.

Time features can include lag values, rolling mean/standard deviation/minimum/maximum, and differences. Calendar features include weekday, month, quarter, holidays, and seasonal indicators. Domain features can include weather, promotions, or economic indicators. Deep models may learn temporal patterns from raw sequences, so feature engineering is often optional. For the Friday 2024-03-15 prompt in a daily series, the listed features are lag-1 = yesterday’s value, lag-7 = last Friday’s value, 7-day mean, weekday = Friday, and holiday indicator = 0. Rolling windows and external predictors must be aligned so they do not reach past the forecast origin.

Evaluate forecasts with the right error lens

Forecasting predicts continuous numeric values, so the lecture frames evaluation as regression error. Let yₜ be an actual value, ŷₜ its forecast, and n the number of evaluated forecasts. The slide defines five metrics:

MAE=1n∑t=1n|yt−y^t|
MSE=1n∑t=1n(yt−y^t)2
RMSE=1n∑t=1n(yt−y^t)2
MAPE=100%n∑t=1n|yt−y^t||yt|
sMAPE=100%n∑t=1n|yt−y^t|(|yt|+|y^t|)/2

The source comparison is compactly summarized below. “Scale-independent” means the metric itself is a percentage/relative measure, not that it is safe for every series.

Metric Outlier sensitive? Scale independent? Zero-value note Interpretation
MAE No (relative to squared-error metrics) No Defined when actual is zero Same units as data
MSE Yes No Defined when actual is zero Squared units
RMSE Yes No Defined when actual is zero Same units as data
MAPE No Yes Undefined when actual is zero; unstable near zero Percentage
sMAPE (source denominator) No Yes A zero actual with nonzero forecast is defined; both zero makes the formula undefined unless a convention is stated Percentage

“Less sensitive” does not mean immune: MAE and percentage metrics still respond to large errors. Squaring in MSE/RMSE gives large errors greater influence. The slide marks MSE and RMSE as unsuitable when the actual equals zero, but their displayed formulas do not divide by the actual and remain defined at zero. For percentage errors, MAPE is undefined at a zero actual and unstable near zero. The displayed sMAPE formula also needs a convention when actual and forecast are both zero because its term is undefined. See Hyndman and Koehler’s forecast accuracy discussion for percentage-metric limitations.

Metric check on the rolling teaching forecast

Using actuals [18, 20] and forecasts [15, 18], errors are 3 and 2; squared errors are 9 and 4. Therefore MAE=(3+2)/2=2.5, MSE=(9+4)/2=6.5, and RMSE=6.5≈2.5495097568. MAPE uses actual magnitudes: 100%×((3/18)+(2/20))/2=13.3333333333%. For source-formula sMAPE, denominators are (18+15)/2=16.5 and (20+18)/2=19, giving 100%×((3/16.5)+(2/19))/2=14.3540669856%. Check: RMSE2=MSE apart from rounding. These hand calculations use the synthetic teaching series.

Probabilistic forecasts describe uncertainty

A point forecast gives one value. A probabilistic forecast represents a range or distribution of plausible future outcomes, making uncertainty visible for decisions in finance, energy, weather, inventory, and healthcare. The lecture’s figure contrasts a point forecast with a set of forecast quantiles (20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th percentiles). The curves below are schematic labels, not predicted values or a fitted interval.

The slide lists ARIMA prediction intervals and quantile regression as statistical approaches, gradient boosting for machine learning, and DeepAR and the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) for deep learning. Evaluation examples are pinball (quantile) loss, continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), and prediction interval coverage probability (PICP). A point-forecast error metric alone does not describe whether a forecast range is calibrated or appropriately sharp.

Multivariate and panel forecasting

Multivariate forecasting predicts one or more target variables using several related time-dependent features. For electricity demand, the target may be demand while predictors include temperature, humidity, and past demand; high temperature may raise cooling demand. The target is what is forecast, and predictors are inputs. High correlation among predictors (multicollinearity) complicates interpretation or estimation; too many predictors raise complexity and overfitting risk (the curse of dimensionality); different scales call for normalization; missing values require imputation or omission. Every predictor still needs to be known at the forecast origin—future weather observations, for example, are unavailable unless represented by their own forecasts or other genuinely known information.

Panel forecasting predicts series for multiple entities while using time patterns and cross-entity patterns. Multiple stores’ monthly sales are one example. Entities can be heterogeneous, one entity’s values may depend on others (cross-sectional dependence), time gaps can make the panel unbalanced, and differing ranges can bias a model unless handled. A panel therefore has to preserve both entity identity and time order.

Common mistakes

  • Treating timestamps as enough to establish a time series: check that observations are an ordered, meaningfully sampled process.
  • Calling the source’s 2–10+ year cycle duration a universal definition, or claiming its components were estimated by an additive decomposition.
  • Reading an ADF p-value below 0.05 as proof of all stationarity conditions; the test targets a unit root under a chosen deterministic specification.
  • Comparing sample ACF values without stating estimation conventions, or inferring exact values or model order from an illustrative plot.
  • Randomly shuffling train, validation, and test periods; fitting scaling or imputation parameters on the full series; or using future observations in a feature at an earlier forecast origin.
  • Confusing a rolling one-step evaluation, where newly observed data can enter at the next origin, with a fixed-origin recursive forecast, where prior predictions are fed back.
  • Refitting or clipping the min–max teaching transform because later values exceed 1; the train-fitted transform remains unchanged.
  • Using MAPE when an actual is zero or close to zero, or using the source-formula sMAPE at an actual and forecast both equal zero without specifying a convention.
  • Choosing a model by its publication year instead of its assumptions and validation performance.

Exam focus

  • Define a time series as ordered, regularly spaced observations; distinguish univariate, multivariate, and panel structure.
  • Identify trend direction/shape, fixed-period seasonality, non-fixed cycles, and irregular/residual variation; state component assumptions only when specified.
  • Define weak stationarity correctly; interpret ADF as a unit-root test with specification-dependent evidence.
  • Explain lag k, autocorrelation and ACF versus PACF; distinguish the population definition from sample estimation conventions.
  • State the forecast origin and horizon; compare recursive, direct, and multiple-output multi-step forecasts.
  • Split train → validation → test chronologically, fit learned preprocessing on training data only, and enforce availability at every forecast origin.
  • Build overlapping windows carefully and preserve X=(5,3,1), y=(5,1) for the lecture’s eight-point, input-three example.
  • Choose among MAE, MSE, RMSE, MAPE, and sMAPE by units, scale, outliers, and zero behavior; verify an arithmetic result independently.
  • Distinguish point forecasts from probabilistic forecasts, quantiles, intervals, and uncertainty metrics.

Practice questions

1. A series has a gradual upward movement, weekday peaks, and an irregular two-year oscillation. Name the three components.

Check your answer

The gradual movement is trend; weekday repetition is seasonality; the non-fixed two-year oscillation is cyclical. Short unpredictable deviations are irregular variation.

2. What is the ADF null, and what does a small p-value support?

Check your answer

The null is that a unit root is present. A sufficiently small p-value is evidence against that null under the chosen ADF regression and lag specification; it does not prove every stationarity condition.

3. Explain lag 7 and the distinction between ACF and PACF.

Check your answer

Lag 7 compares observations seven time steps apart. ACF shows overall lag correlation including indirect intermediate-lag effects; PACF describes the lag’s direct association after accounting for intermediate lags.

4. For eight scalar observations and input length three, how many one-step windows are formed, and what shapes does the source give?

Check your answer

There are 8−3=5 windows; with one feature per time step, shapes are X=(5,3,1),y=(5,1).

5. Why can validation or test values exceed 1 after training-only min–max scaling?

Check your answer

The minimum and maximum were fitted on the training portion and are applied unchanged. A later value above the training maximum naturally maps above 1; refitting would leak held-out information.

6. When may a rolling one-step evaluation use the first test actual to form the next prediction?

Check your answer

Only after that observation would have arrived at the next rolling origin. It cannot be used for a fixed-origin multi-step forecast issued earlier.

7. Which metrics share the data unit, which is squared, and what is the MAPE zero caveat?

Check your answer

MAE and RMSE use the data unit; MSE uses squared units. MAPE divides by the actual magnitude, so it is undefined at zero and unstable near zero.

8. Why is the source’s sMAPE check for zero values incomplete?

Check your answer

If both actual and forecast are zero, the displayed term is 0/0. A zero/zero convention must be stated explicitly.

9. Distinguish a panel series from a multivariate series.

Check your answer

Multivariate data records several variables together over time. Panel data records one or more variables for multiple entities over time, so it also has entity structure.
