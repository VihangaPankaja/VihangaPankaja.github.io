Big picture

An AI application succeeds only when a model choice fits a real need, organizational capacity, data, acceptable risk, and a way to tell whether the system helps. This course module connects requirements and readiness to foundation-model selection, performance improvement, evaluation, deployment, and ongoing monitoring. Those stages form a feedback loop: evidence from use can lead to a changed model, design, or even the original problem definition.

The companion also includes agents, two contrasting application cases, and an AWS-service comparison as presented in the 2025 course. A separate module maps AIF-C01 certification domains and preparation resources from that same course edition. It explicitly says the course focuses primarily on domains 2 and 3; neither the course nor this optional reading is complete certification preparation.

Start with a customer problem and organizational readiness

“Working backwards” means first describing the customer experience and its value, then iterating toward what should be built. The course emphasizes customer centricity and using analytics and cloud computing to address business problems. The customer may be an employee, partner, patient, or another stakeholder. Business and technical teams need a shared description of the problem, constraints, expected outputs, and performance.

Before selecting a model, define requirements and check whether generative AI fits the problem. Identify responsible-AI risks, privacy and security needs, and regulatory or compliance considerations early. Organizational readiness includes infrastructure and technical capabilities, data availability and quality, budget, skills, and cultural readiness to adopt AI. The same technical design may be feasible for one team and unrealistic for another.

Teaching example: decide whether an HR policy assistant is ready for a pilot

Need: employees spend time finding HR policies and routine answers. Inputs: policy documents, handbooks, FAQs, and the employee’s location. Readiness evidence: the course scenario has high-quality documents, existing HR systems on AWS with IAM access controls, limited development expertise, and a small operations team.

Reasoning: the document corpus and existing identity context make an internal document assistant plausible; limited staff makes a lower-operations entry path relevant. Before a pilot, stakeholders still need to define which questions are in scope, what a useful answer means, who owns updates, and how employees can report a bad answer. The 2025 course scenario selects Amazon Q Business; this is not a universal or current product recommendation.

Check: the deployment is not ready merely because documents exist. Confirm data quality, access boundaries, budget, staff capacity, safeguards, and a measurable pilot plan.

Turn goals into success metrics

Metrics make the intended value observable. Candidate measures in the course include productivity gain, time saved, customer-satisfaction rating, quality improvement, and cost savings. Select measures that match the problem and agree on their definitions with stakeholders: baseline, population, measurement window, denominator, and acceptable trade-offs. A target is a desired future value, not evidence that the system achieved it.

Select a model and decide how to improve it

The use case drives foundation-model choice. The course lists model capability, model size and complexity, inference speed, cost, available information about training data, ethical considerations, and implementation considerations. Production choices also depend on throughput, expected request volume, context-window needs, and whether the application creates longer inputs or outputs. Consider upfront license fees as well as per-request/token charges described in the course; cost is tied to the actual workload and course-era pricing model.

The team should consider that a pre-trained model’s training data may not be fully known or controlled. That uncertainty can raise questions about bias and fairness and about practices needed to mitigate risks. Match model capability to the task: a larger context window may matter for long documents, while a chat task may need a smaller context window. Do not choose “largest” as a proxy for best fit.

Performance improvement options include inference-parameter changes and prompt engineering, adding external context through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), or customizing model parameters through fine-tuning or continued pre-training. These are not interchangeable:

Ways to improve an application and what changes
ApproachWhat changesTypical role in the course framing
Prompt engineering and inference parametersThe request instructions, context arrangement, or generation settingsGuide the existing model behavior without updating model weights
RAGExternal information is retrieved and added to the request context at inference timeGround answers in a use-case data source that can be updated separately
Fine-tuningModel parameters are adapted using training examplesCustomize behavior using domain examples such as labeled historical question-answer pairs
Continued pre-trainingModel parameters are further trained on additional dataAnother customization route; the course lists it separately from retrieval

RAG is a context/data operation, not a weight update. Fine-tuning and continued pre-training change model parameters using data. The course’s qualitative comparison says RAG can require less upfront effort and cost than fine-tuning but may respond more slowly in-domain; treat that as a course example, not a universal performance law. Retrieval still depends on source quality and access controls. Fine-tuning does not guarantee recall of changing documents.

Balance quality, latency, throughput, and operating effort

Performance choices affect more than answer quality. In the course framing, well-structured prompts and concise outputs can reduce the work per request; inference parameters can limit output length. Use real-time inference when a person needs an immediate response, and batch inference for bulk requests that can complete asynchronously. The course describes Provisioned Throughput as an option for consistent workloads that need guaranteed throughput, and batch inference as a lower-cost option than standard on-demand pricing. Those comparisons are 2025 course context, depend on workload, and are not current pricing advice.

Availability needs also depend on the user’s task: a real-time medical-support line has less tolerance for downtime than an application offering gardening tips. A managed cloud service can reduce the amount of infrastructure the team operates, while a more self-managed design can retain greater control. Match availability expectations, desired control, skills, and operating capacity to the use case.

Teaching example: choose RAG, fine-tuning, or both for support answers

Given: an online shop answers questions about order tracking, returns, products, and troubleshooting. Product catalogues and troubleshooting guides change; historical support questions and answers show the desired response style.

Reason: retrieve current catalogue and troubleshooting passages for each request so the model receives relevant, changeable information. Separately, fine-tune on labeled historical question-answer pairs if the goal includes adapting response behavior. Retrieval supplies current context; training examples update parameters. Neither operation replaces evaluation.

Check: verify retrieved sources and permissions, then evaluate factual answers against current product material and assess style/quality separately. If the documents change, update the knowledge source; do not assume a model-weight update is necessary.

Follow the application lifecycle

The diagram redraws the course’s six-stage lifecycle and feedback relationships. It is iterative rather than a one-way release pipeline.

  1. Define a use case. State the problem, requirements, customer experience, organizational fit, risks, and success measures.
  2. Select a foundation model. Match capabilities, data knowledge, speed, cost, context, ethical concerns, and implementation constraints to the use case.
  3. Improve performance. Test prompts and inference parameters, RAG, fine-tuning, continued pre-training, or a combination where justified.
  4. Evaluate results. Use relevant metrics and test datasets against objectives. Iterate toward a minimally viable solution that meets release criteria.
  5. Deploy the application. Integrate the model, infrastructure, and monitoring into production; plan maintenance, security, access, staff preparation, and change management.
  6. Monitor, audit, and get feedback. Observe infrastructure, model quality, compliance, business outcomes, and user feedback. Use findings to refine or revisit earlier decisions.

Evaluate across methods, datasets, and time

Align evaluation criteria to business outcomes and combine quantitative measures with qualitative judgment. Human reviewers can assess relevance, coherence, and appropriateness, but review takes time and can be costly. Benchmarks named in the course include GLUE, SuperGLUE, and SQuAD. Automated measures include perplexity and BLEU. A benchmark or metric only covers the dimensions represented by its task, data, and scoring method; language metrics may miss important quality nuances.

Use multiple test datasets. The course’s schematic chart shows different trends across datasets A, B, and C as model versions change; it contains no numeric values. A model can improve on one dataset and worsen on another. Data distributions also shift, so evaluation should recur over the lifecycle rather than serve as a one-time gate. Human and automated methods complement one another; neither alone proves application quality.

Teaching example: interpret mixed dataset trends

Given: across later model versions, the schematic trend rises for datasets A and B and falls for dataset C. The graph is qualitative and gives no scales or measurements.

Reasoning: the model is not simply “better” in every context. Identify what differs in dataset C, inspect failures, and check whether its distribution or task reflects an important use case. Compare against the business criteria and affected users before deciding whether to deploy.

Check: do not calculate a percentage change or claim statistical significance from the schematic. It supports only the need to evaluate more than one dataset and monitor drift.

Deploy, monitor, audit, and respond

Deployment includes configuring infrastructure and integrating the model with application components, securing the environment, setting up monitoring, preparing staff, and managing change. Plan compute, storage, networking, traffic, and data volume. Implement scoped access and data protections. The course distinguishes several kinds of evidence:

Operational signals, what they show, and possible response
SignalQuestion it helps answerExample response
Infrastructure: compute, network, storageIs the system constrained or using resources unexpectedly?Investigate bottlenecks; adjust resource allocation
System logs and errorsWhere did an operational failure occur?Inspect logs and trace the cause
Model performance and latencyIs quality or response time degrading?Evaluate examples, compare versions, consider model or deployment changes
Business metricsAre users completing the intended task?Review engagement, satisfaction, task completion, or time saved
Bias, fairness, and compliance indicatorsAre outcomes or processes diverging from defined expectations?Investigate with suitable human review and update thresholds or controls

Monitoring is signal collection; an alert does not itself diagnose or fix a problem. Responses can include root-cause investigation, additional human review, resource changes, revised deployment strategy, changed monitoring thresholds, or retraining when evidence supports it. For models managed through SageMaker AI, the course describes Model Monitor for data/model drift signals and A/B testing to compare versions before full rollout. Amazon Augmented AI (A2I) is described as a way to route random samples or low-confidence predictions to human review and feed feedback into improvement. These names and capabilities are 2025 course context.

In the customer-chat example, AWS CloudTrail is used to monitor and audit API calls made to the model. Amazon CloudWatch logs are used to monitor and analyze responses; Bedrock runtime metrics in CloudWatch support performance monitoring. API-call audit records and runtime metrics/logs answer different operational questions; one is not a substitute for the other.

Teaching example: respond to a rise in latency

Given: production latency rises while task completion falls. Those are two separate signals: one technical, one business-related.

Investigate: inspect infrastructure use, system logs, model performance, and recent changes. Check whether slower responses coincide with larger context/request volume or an integration issue; do not assume the model alone is responsible.

Respond and verify: adjust resources or deployment strategy if evidence supports it, then compare latency and task completion against the baseline. If model behavior also changed, route examples for evaluation or human review. Keep monitoring to confirm the response improved the intended outcome.

Agents coordinate models, data, and tools

An AI agent is autonomous or semi-autonomous software designed to perform tasks for a user. Generative-AI agents often combine an LLM with external tools, databases, or APIs. They can process natural-language requests, make decisions, divide tasks into smaller steps, collect information, and take actions. Amazon Bedrock Agents are presented as orchestrating interactions among a foundation model, data sources, software applications, and user conversations; they can call APIs and query data sources.

The travel example below preserves the course’s six numbered roles. Its diagram labels the calculator connection “Search and retrieval,” while the described calculator task is to calculate totals. This view keeps itinerary retrieval with the trip catalog and treats the calculator as a calculation tool. The conceptual flow leaves authentication, authorization, validation, failure handling, retries, injection defenses, privacy controls, and human approval unspecified.

  1. User input: asks for a priced weekend itinerary for a named destination and style.
  2. Agent decision: determines it needs trip options and prices from the catalog and a calculated total before asking the LLM to formulate the response. This is an illustrative decision, not a demonstrated planning algorithm.
  3. Catalog retrieval: sends a query to the trip-catalog database; the database returns itinerary options and prices.
  4. Calculator call: sends the relevant trip options and prices to a calculator app; the app returns totals.
  5. Prompt assembly: combines the original request, catalog results, and calculator results into the context sent to the LLM.
  6. Model response: the LLM uses the supplied context to generate an answer that returns to the user.

The agent’s plan is not authorization. In a real design, independently decide which tools are allowed, scope the identity and permissions behind each action, validate inputs and outputs, and define failure handling and human escalation where appropriate. These are supplemental design questions, not properties demonstrated by the course diagram. The course says this approach improves the travel model’s performance but reports no measured baseline or result.

The course’s comparison is qualitative. It places Amazon Q at a lower technical-skills/control threshold for internal document use, Amazon Bedrock in a middle position for integrating pre-trained models into applications, and SageMaker AI at the higher-control end for teams that build or customize models and manage more of the ML lifecycle. The chart’s horizontal axis indicates increasing complexity and cost; it gives no numeric scale and is not a universal ranking. Prompt engineering and RAG appear toward the lower-complexity end, model customization farther along, and building a model from scratch at the higher-control and complexity end. Two worked application cases

HR policy assistant: identity-scoped document answers

The course scenario has limited development expertise and operations staff, existing HR systems on AWS with IAM access controls, urgency for a pilot, and high-quality policy documents, handbooks, and FAQs. It selects Amazon Q Business. The team uploads those sources, uses existing IAM implementation to grant location-appropriate subsets, applies global and topic-level controls with guardrails, gathers employee ratings/feedback, and updates the knowledge base when policies change.

For evaluation, the course proposes randomly selected HR queries and a 95% or higher correct-response target. It also tracks usefulness/ease feedback, HR ticket-volume and time reductions, and usage/question patterns. The 95% is a target, not an observed result, a universal threshold, or evidence that answers are safe. The example begins with one department, integrates through the intranet/HR portal, trains employees, and monitors use and performance with CloudWatch and CloudTrail; CloudWatch logs are described for monitoring and analyzing responses. Identity-scoped retrieval does not train the model, and an answer can still be wrong or incomplete.

Teaching example: evaluate the HR pilot without overstating the target

Plan: sample HR queries, compare answers with authoritative policy, define what counts as correct, and track employee feedback plus ticket volume/time. The course states a goal of at least 95% correct responses.

Interpretation: before drawing a conclusion, record sample size and selection method, policy version, answer rubric, and how partially correct or risky answers are handled. The slide gives no measured sample result.

Check: report the target separately from observed pilot outcomes. High accuracy on sampled queries would not prove permission correctness, privacy compliance, fairness, or safety for every employee question.

Customer-service chat: retrieval and fine-tuning together

The e-commerce scenario aims to answer tracking, returns, and product-information questions around the clock, while improving accuracy and satisfaction and reducing human-agent workload. The team has software-development experience, no models built in-house, one analytics data scientist, and leadership concern about customer experience. The course chooses Bedrock and compares several LLMs, choosing a smaller/faster model that balances language understanding.

The team configures a Bedrock Knowledge Base with product catalogues and troubleshooting guides, and separately fine-tunes on labeled historical questions and answers. At inference, retrieval adds relevant product/troubleshooting context; fine-tuning changes model parameters using the labeled examples. Evaluation combines human review and automated accuracy metrics. KPIs include response accuracy, customer satisfaction, and successful query resolution. A 25% improvement over historical KPI values is the target, not a measured result.

The rollout starts with a small percentage of traffic and grows gradually, integrates with the existing customer-service platform and website, collects customer ratings/feedback, and monitors operation. CloudTrail audits model API calls; CloudWatch logs help analyze responses and Bedrock runtime metrics support performance monitoring.

The course’s 2025 pricing slide lists customization training by tokens processed, monthly customized-model storage, standard on-demand input/output-token charges, and Provisioned Throughput charged hourly by model units, with longer commitments described as discounted. It says customized Bedrock models require Provisioned Throughput to run in that course edition. These are historical course statements, not current prices or purchase advice.

Teaching example: separate the customer-chat target from evidence

Baseline: record historical accuracy, satisfaction, and successful-resolution measures using stable definitions and the same eligible population intended for comparison.

Target: the course scenario seeks 25% improvement in those indicators versus historical values. It does not provide the starting numbers or a post-launch result, so no achieved score can be calculated.

Check: pilot with a small traffic fraction, combine automated accuracy checks and human review, monitor customer feedback and runtime signals, and compare like-for-like observations before expanding. A target is not proof of effectiveness.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with a model or service before agreeing on the customer problem, requirements, organizational readiness, and success criteria.
  • Treating a target such as 95% HR accuracy or 25% KPI improvement as a measured result.
  • Confusing RAG’s request-time context retrieval with fine-tuning or continued pre-training, which update model parameters.
  • Choosing from a single benchmark or one test dataset and overlooking changing data distributions.
  • Assuming deployment ends the lifecycle, or treating a monitor alert as a diagnosis or an automatic fix.
  • Treating CloudTrail API-call auditing as interchangeable with CloudWatch logs and runtime metrics.
  • Assuming an agent’s model-generated plan is authorized, validated, or safe to execute.
  • Reading qualitative service-positioning axes as a numeric or universal ranking.
  • Treating 2025 certification format, domain weights, counts, candidate profile, resource duration, or pricing as current guidance.
  • Inferring that this optional companion is required CS4681 content or complete AWS certification preparation.

Exam focus

  • Application design proceeds from customer problem and organizational readiness to model choice, improvement, evaluation, deployment, and ongoing monitoring/feedback.
  • Define success metrics with stakeholders and keep targets distinct from measured outcomes.
  • Model selection considers capability, complexity, speed, cost, training-data information, ethics, implementation, context window, throughput, and request volumes.
  • RAG adds retrieved context at inference; fine-tuning and continued pre-training customize parameters.
  • Combine human evaluation, automated metrics, benchmarks, and multiple representative datasets; evaluate repeatedly as models and data change.
  • Monitor technical, business, responsible-AI, and compliance signals; investigate and choose a response based on evidence.
  • In the travel-agent trace, distinguish catalog retrieval from calculator totals and preserve the source’s six numbered roles.
  • In the HR scenario, IAM-scoped documents and a 95% accuracy target are design context, not proof of correctness or safe access.
  • In the customer-chat scenario, retrieval and fine-tuning are separate mechanisms; 25% improvement is a target.

3. Explain the difference between RAG and fine-tuning.

Check your answer
