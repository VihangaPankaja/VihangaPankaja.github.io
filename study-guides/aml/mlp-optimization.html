Multilayer Networks, Gradient Descent, and Training Practice

How multilayer networks combine affine layers and activations, and how gradients, objectives, and training schedules shape their learning.

Big picture

A multilayer perceptron (MLP) composes weighted affine transformations and activation functions. Training chooses parameters so predictions match a task-specific target while controlling how well the model behaves beyond its training examples. The main loop is: produce outputs, calculate a scalar loss, compute gradients, and let an optimizer update parameters. The network’s forward computations and backward error information serve different roles: backpropagation computes gradients; the optimizer applies the updates.

How a multilayer network is composed

Each layer takes the previous layer’s activations, forms a weighted sum, adds a bias, and applies an activation. The bias is an independent parameter that shifts the affine response; it is not another activation or an output correction.

𝐚(0)=𝐱,𝐯(ℓ)=W(ℓ)𝐚(ℓ−1)+𝐛(ℓ),𝐚(ℓ)=ϕ(ℓ)(𝐯(ℓ))

The output of one layer becomes the input to the next. Stacking affine maps without a nonlinear activation still gives one affine map; the activation is what lets the composition represent nonlinear relationships. An output layer’s activation must also fit the task.

Forward signals and hidden-layer credit

Function signals travel from input toward output. The output is compared with the training target to form a loss. Error information then moves back through the network so shared hidden units can receive credit or blame even though no desired response is supplied for each hidden neuron. The chain-rule calculation of those hidden gradients belongs to the backpropagation topic; keep the distinction clear here: a gradient describes how the objective changes with a parameter, while an optimizer rule decides how to change that parameter.

Gradient descent and learning-rate behavior

Treat the objective as a scalar function of all parameters. Its gradient points toward locally faster increase, so gradient descent takes a step in the opposite direction:

θt+1=θt−η∇θJ(θt)

Here θ collects weights and biases, and η>0 is the learning rate. The loss defines what “better” means; the gradient supplies local direction; the optimizer turns that direction into a parameter step. They are related but are not the same operation.

Three scalar gradient-descent iterations

Teaching exampleObjective: J(w)=(w−2)2; derivative: J′(w)=2(w−2)Initial parameter: w(0)=0; learning rate: η=0.1Update from the derivative at the old value: w(k+1)=w(k)−ηJ′(w(k))Each step reports the objective after the update. The index k counts optimizer iterations, not dataset epochs.

Iteration 1: move from w = 0

Teaching example. Minimize J(w)=(w−2)2. Its derivative is J′(w)=2(w−2). Start with w(0)=0 and use η=0.1. Evaluate the derivative at the old parameter:

J(0)=4,J′(0)=2(0−2)=−4

Gradient descent subtracts the gradient-scaled step:

w(1)=0−0.1(−4)=0.4,J(w(1))=(0.4−2)2=2.56

The negative gradient points toward larger w here, toward the minimizer at 2. This is one optimizer iteration, not an epoch.

Iteration 2: use the updated parameter

The current parameter is w(1)=0.4. Recompute both objective and derivative at this old state:

J(0.4)=(0.4−2)2=2.56,J′(0.4)=2(0.4−2)=−3.2

Apply the same rule, then evaluate the objective at the new value:

w(2)=0.4−0.1(−3.2)=0.72,J(w(2))=(0.72−2)2=1.6384

Use the newly updated parameter for the next iteration.

Iteration 3: check the third update

At w(2)=0.72, the old-state objective and derivative are:

J(0.72)=(0.72−2)2=1.6384,J′(0.72)=2(0.72−2)=−2.56

Substitute into the update and calculate the post-step objective:

w(3)=0.72−0.1(−2.56)=0.976,J(w(3))=(0.976−2)2=1.048576

Independent check. Since J′(w)=2(w−2), the exact minimizer is w=2. The three states move 0<0.4<0.72<0.976<2, and the directly evaluated objectives decrease 4>2.56>1.6384>1.048576. This verifies these steps only; it is not a convergence proof for a neural-network objective.

The lecture’s contour sketches show why the step size matters. A small η gives smooth but slow progress; a larger value can zigzag across a narrow valley; above a problem-dependent range, steps can become unstable and diverge. The example rates 0.1,0.01,0.001 are illustrative starting scales, not universally safe choices. Initialization also matters: an unfavorable starting point or scale can make progress difficult.

Match the output and loss to the task

The output representation and objective should reflect what the task asks the model to predict. For categorical classification, softmax converts class scores (logits) into a normalized distribution. For a real-valued prediction, a linear output with squared loss is a common pairing. The lecture also pairs linear regression with least squares and SVMs with hinge loss.

Training fit and generalization

Overfitting occurs when fitting the observed training set does not translate into good prediction on unseen examples. The lecture emphasizes the gap between training and test performance, especially when a model is complex relative to a small dataset. Its four-row illustration makes the limitation tangible:

The two weight vectors both fit all four displayed training rows

Worked example. The lecture gives these feature vectors and targets:

Features (x₁,…,x₅)TargetW̄₁ = [2,0,0,0,0]W̄₂ = [0,2,4,6,8]
(1,1,0,0,0)222
(2,0,1,0,0)444
(3,0,0,1,0)666
(4,0,0,0,1)888

For W¯1=[2,0,0,0,0], each prediction is 2x1: 2(1)=2,2(2)=4,2(3)=6,2(4)=8. For W¯2=[0,2,4,6,8], each row activates one of its last four coordinates, giving 2,4,6,8 again. Thus both weight vectors have zero error on the four shown rows.

On an unlisted input (0,1,0,0,0), the predictions differ: W¯1·x=0 while W¯2·x=2. The source supplies no target for this input, so the training rows do not tell us which prediction is correct. This is the concrete point: training fit alone does not settle behavior away from the observed examples.

Remedies change different parts of training

Regularization changes the objective by penalizing parameter values. Domain-informed architecture and parameter sharing change what the network can represent—for example, by reusing structure across words in a sentence or pixels in an image. Early stopping changes how long optimization continues: hold out part of the training data and stop when its error begins to rise. Trading breadth for depth changes the model’s shape; the lecture notes that compositions in deeper networks can need fewer units per layer. Ensemble methods combine models; the lecture lists dropout in this context. These are distinct choices, not interchangeable guarantees.

Data schedules: batch, stochastic, and mini-batch

An update uses a gradient calculated from some portion of the training data. Full-batch gradient descent uses all examples before one update. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) uses one example per update. Mini-batch learning uses a subset to approximate the full-data gradient; it is more memory-efficient than loading the whole set and has lower gradient variance than one-example SGD, though it need not point in the steepest full-data direction. The noise in mini-batch updates may sometimes help move out of shallow minima; it does not guarantee escape or a better final solution.

An iteration is one update on one chosen batch. An epoch is one complete forward/backward pass over the training examples. The slide’s example has 10,000 examples split into 10 minibatches, so each batch contains 1,000 examples and one epoch has 10 update iterations—not a batch size of 10.

With N examples and batch size B, the usual number of updates per epoch is ⌈N/B⌉ when a final partial batch is kept. If B=N, one update covers an epoch; if B=1, one epoch has one update per example. Shuffling batch order is common supplemental practice, but it does not change these definitions.

Regularization and norms

Underfitting means the model fails to capture useful structure even in its training data; overfitting means its training fit does not carry over to new cases. The lecture contrasts a simple model with high bias against a complex model with high variance. This is a useful explanation of the extremes, not a universal rule that complexity alone determines either quantity.

Why discourage large weights? In a linear response, a small change to an input is multiplied by its coefficient, so a large coefficient can amplify a small input change into a large output change. The lecture connects that sensitivity to variance and overfitting risk. Penalizing large weights can restrain this sensitivity, while too strong a penalty can make the model underfit; its effect depends on feature scaling, model structure and the task.

The lecture’s general objective is written as a sum of data losses plus a weighted regularizer. Keep that displayed sum distinct from the later illustrative penalties, which each show a separate division by the batch/sample count n.

min𝐰,b∑nl(yn,𝐰·𝐱n+b)+λR(𝐰,b)

Momentum and the slide’s sign discrepancy

Momentum carries direction from a previous step into a new step to smooth zigzags. A signed increment recurrence that preserves this direction is:

𝐯t+1=μ𝐯t−η∇J(θt),θt+1=θt+𝐯t+1

The lecture prints this weight-level expression:

winew=wiold−η∂E∂wiold+μ|wiold−wiolder|

This is one standard convention; the equivalent gradient-buffer form stores the gradient-like quantity and subtracts it from the parameters. The printed lecture expression instead adds μ|wiold−wiolder| to the gradient step. Absolute value removes the sign of the previous increment, so it cannot in general retain whether that move was positive or negative. That conflicts with the accompanying prose and zigzag-smoothing illustration. The signed recurrence above is the mathematically direction-preserving correction, consistent with the standard signed momentum update documented by PyTorch’s SGD reference; it is not the printed absolute-value formula. Momentum may smooth updates and sometimes help across shallow regions, but it does not guarantee escape from every local minimum. The slides give μ∈[0,1] and a typical value near 0.9.

Dropout as described in this lecture

In this dropout description, each weight is set to zero with probability π during each epoch, where π∈[0,1]. This masks individual connections. The later deep-learning treatment masks units, removing their incident connections together. These are different sampling granularities. See the deep-learning foundations topic for unit dropout.

Other deep-network training difficulties

Backpropagating through many layers multiplies local derivatives. Early-layer updates can become negligibly small (vanishing gradients) or excessively large (exploding gradients), and very deep networks may converge slowly. The slide points to sigmoid saturation as one source of vanishing gradients and mentions ReLU, adaptive learning rates, and batch normalization as responses. For the unit-slope logistic sigmoid, σ′(v)=σ(v)(1−σ(v)) is at most 1/4; equality occurs at v=0, so the correct bound is ≤0.25. For a positive slope a, the maximum is a/4.

Training also faces high learning rates, poor initialization, local optima in a nonlinear objective, and substantial computation time. The lecture notes that large text and image networks may require weeks. It also points to non-sequential layouts, changes to the loss, and variational autoencoders as alternative designs; it gives no additional measured training results.

Common mistakes

  • Treating backpropagation and the optimizer as synonyms. Backpropagation computes gradients; an optimizer applies a selected update rule.
  • Calling every update an epoch. An epoch covers the whole dataset; one mini-batch update is one iteration.
  • Reading the 10,000-example example as batch size 10. Ten batches means 1,000 examples per batch and ten updates per epoch.
  • Assuming two different parameter vectors that fit the shown rows must agree on unseen inputs—or claiming one is right without a target.
  • Replacing a sum with a mean, or changing only the regularization term’s normalization without saying so.
  • Calling ‖𝐰‖22 the L₂ norm; it is the squared L₂ norm.
  • Substituting p=0 directly into the displayed formula despite its undefined reciprocal exponent.
  • Copying the absolute-value momentum term as though it retained direction, or claiming momentum always escapes local minima.
  • Treating the lecture’s weight-level dropout as identical to later unit-level dropout.

Exam focus

  • Layer composition: affine weighted sum plus bias, followed by activation; nonlinear activations let stacked layers represent nonlinear functions.
  • Gradient descent: θt+1=θt−η∇J; a gradient is not itself an update rule.
  • Pair categorical outputs with softmax/cross-entropy; pair real-valued outputs with linear/squared loss when appropriate.
  • Overfitting means training fit does not ensure unseen-data performance. Distinguish objective penalties, architecture/parameter sharing, validation-based early stopping, and ensembles.
  • Full batch uses all examples/update; SGD uses one; mini-batch uses a subset. One iteration is one batch update; one epoch covers all examples.
  • Keep the general summed data objective separate from the displayed λ/n L₁/L₂ penalties. L₂ uses squared norm; the p=0 case cannot be obtained by direct substitution.
  • Know the signed momentum recurrence and explain the printed absolute-value conflict. Momentum has no universal local-minimum guarantee.
  • State dropout’s granularity: this lecture says weights; the later deep-learning material says units.

Practice questions

1. What happens to a layer’s output when its affine map is followed by a nonlinear activation?

Check your answer

The affine map forms weighted sums and adds biases; the activation transforms those values. Because the activation is nonlinear, stacking layers can represent nonlinear compositions rather than collapsing into one affine map.

2. Does backpropagation itself choose the learning rate and apply a parameter update?

Check your answer

No. Backpropagation computes loss gradients with the chain rule. The optimizer uses those gradients with its learning rate and any state such as momentum to update parameters.

3. For 10,000 examples split into 10 equal mini-batches, what are the batch size and updates per epoch?

Check your answer

Each batch has 10000/10=1000 examples. One update uses one batch, so all 10 batches require 10 updates for one epoch.

4. Why do the two displayed weight vectors not identify a unique prediction for every possible input?

Check your answer

Both match all four shown training rows, but on the unlisted input (0,1,0,0,0) they predict 0 and 2. No true target is supplied for that input, so the slide cannot establish which prediction is correct.

5. What is the difference between the slide’s general regularized objective and its L₁/L₂ examples?

Check your answer

The general expression adds λR(𝐰,b) to a sum of data losses and displays no averaging factor. The later L₁/L₂ illustrations scale their shown weight penalties by λ/n; L₂ uses the squared norm. Do not transfer one normalization silently.

6. Why is the printed absolute-value momentum increment inconsistent with retaining direction?

Check your answer

Absolute value erases whether the previous increment was positive or negative. A signed velocity retains that direction: 𝐯t+1=μ𝐯t−η∇J and θt+1=θt+𝐯t+1. The correction matches the accompanying direction-retaining prose, while the printed absolute-value expression loses that direction.

7. When does the unit-slope sigmoid derivative reach its maximum, and what is the maximum?

Check your answer

It is at most 1/4, with equality when the pre-activation is zero. A strict “less than” statement misses the equality case.
