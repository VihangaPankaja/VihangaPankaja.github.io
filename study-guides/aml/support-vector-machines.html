Support vector machines, margins and kernels (supplemental)

A maximum-margin linear classifier, extended to tolerate violations and form nonlinear boundaries through feature maps and kernels.

Supplemental lecture material · Not covered in class. This block is presented as supplemental material.

Big picture

A support vector machine (SVM) chooses a separating hyperplane by maximizing the distance between the closest examples of the two classes. With labels encoded as +1 and −1, one signed inequality describes both classes. The maximum-margin objective becomes a constrained optimization problem. Its Lagrangian dual depends only on training-point dot products, which makes support vectors and kernel functions useful. Slack variables relax the constraints when a perfect separation is unavailable.

The route is: define a score and geometric margin; derive the hard-margin primal and dual; read the KKT conditions; add soft-margin slack penalties; then replace input-space dot products with feature-space kernels.

A score defines a hyperplane

For an input vector x and weight vector w, the lecture defines the linear score

f(x)=⟨w,x⟩+b

The decision boundary is the set of points with score zero. Its normal vector is w: moving along w changes the score most directly, and w is perpendicular to the hyperplane. The bias b shifts the boundary without changing its orientation. Prediction is positive for positive score and negative for negative score. A zero score lies on the boundary; the convention in the following displayed rule assigns that tie to +1.

y^={+1,f(x)≥0−1,f(x)<0

For a training pair (xi,yi), the signed score yif(xi) is positive on the labeled side, zero on the decision boundary, and negative on the wrong side. Score units depend on the scale of w and b.

ds(x,H)=f(x)‖w‖,d(x,H)=|ds(x,H)|=|⟨w,x⟩+b|‖w‖,‖w‖=⟨w,w⟩

Assume w≠0. The signed perpendicular distance ds is positive on the positive-score side and negative on the other; for a labeled training point, yids(xi,H) is its distance in the direction of its label. Scaling both w and b by the same positive constant leaves the zero-score hyperplane unchanged. For separable data, choose canonical scaling so the closest labeled examples satisfy yif(xi)=1. The margin boundaries then have scores +1 and −1. Each is 1/‖w‖ from the decision boundary, so their full separation is 2/‖w‖. The lecture motivates a large margin through a generalization-error bound under the assumptions of that analysis. This is a theoretical rationale, not an unconditional guarantee of better test accuracy. Maximizing that width is equivalent to minimizing 12‖w‖2.

Take x−=(−1,0) with y−=−1 and x+=(1,0) with y+=+1. Choose w=(1,0) and b=0.

  1. Scores: f(x−)=−1 and f(x+)=1; both labels are correct.
  2. Signed scores: y−f(x−)=1 and y+f(x+)=1. Both are on canonical margin boundaries.
  3. Norm: ‖w‖=12+02=1.
  4. Half-width is 1/‖w‖=1; full width is 2/‖w‖=2.

Hard margin: from the primal to the dual

Assume all examples are linearly separable. With canonical scaling, each positive example has score at least +1 and each negative example at most −1. One signed constraint covers both: for yi=+1 it says f(xi)≥1; for yi=−1 it says f(xi)≤−1. The negative-class inequality follows by multiplying the signed constraint by −1, which reverses its direction.

minw,b12⟨w,w⟩subject toyi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)≥1, i=1,…,r

Introduce one nonnegative multiplier αi for each signed constraint. These are optimization variables in the dual, not class labels or slack amounts. The primal Lagrangian subtracts each multiplier times its constraint residual.

LP(w,b,α)=12⟨w,w⟩−∑i=1rαi[yi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)−1],αi≥0

The primal variables are w and b. Stationarity with respect to w balances w against a weighted sum of training vectors. Stationarity with respect to b forces the signed multipliers to sum to zero.

∇wLP=w−∑iαiyixi=0⟹w=∑iαiyixi
∂LP∂b=−∑iαiyi=0⟹∑iαiyi=0

KKT conditions keep the original constraints feasible and require complementarity: a multiplier can be positive only when its point’s margin constraint is tight. Substituting stationarity into the Lagrangian eliminates w and b and leaves pairwise dot products.

primal feasibility: yi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)−1≥0dual feasibility: αi≥0complementarity: αi[yi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)−1]=0stationarity: w=∑iαiyixi,∑iαiyi=0
maxαLD(α)=∑iαi−12∑i∑jαiαjyiyj⟨xi,xj⟩subject to∑iαiyi=0,αi≥0

The primal optimizes a separator (w,b) directly; the dual optimizes one multiplier per training example. For a convex quadratic objective with linear constraints, KKT characterizes the optimum, and the Wolfe dual shares the primal optimum. A numerical optimizer is still required to find numeric multipliers.

Derivation walkthrough: identify what each transition removes

Hard-margin primal to dual

Inputs: labeled examples with yᵢ∈−1,+1, linear separability, and canonical margin constraints. Output: a dual optimization problem; no numeric α values are supplied here.

1. Start with primal variables

Choose w and b to minimize 1/2⟨w,w⟩, with yᵢ(⟨w,xᵢ⟩+b)−1≥0 for every point. This is the canonical margin scale.

2. Attach one multiplier per constraint

Introduce αᵢ≥0 and form Lₚ=1/2⟨w,w⟩−Σᵢαᵢ[yᵢ(⟨w,xᵢ⟩+b)−1]. A multiplier belongs to a constraint, not a feature.

3. Enforce stationarity in w

∂Lₚ/∂w=w−Σᵢαᵢyᵢxᵢ=0, hence w=Σᵢαᵢyᵢxᵢ. This expresses the normal as a combination of training vectors.

4. Enforce stationarity in b

∂Lₚ/∂b=−Σᵢαᵢyᵢ=0, so the dual equality constraint is Σᵢαᵢyᵢ=0.

5. Keep feasibility and complementarity

Original constraints remain feasible; every α is nonnegative; and αᵢ[yᵢ(⟨w,xᵢ⟩+b)−1]=0. A positive α means that point is on a margin boundary.

6. Substitute and collect dot products

Substitution removes w and b. The quadratic term becomes −1/2ΣᵢΣⱼαᵢαⱼyᵢyⱼ⟨xᵢ,xⱼ⟩; the linear term is Σᵢαᵢ. Maximize subject to Σᵢαᵢyᵢ=0 and αᵢ≥0.

7. Recover and predict

After a numerical solver returns α, compute w=Σᵢαᵢyᵢxᵢ. Only nonzero α terms contribute. Score z by Σᵢαᵢyᵢ⟨xᵢ,z⟩+b; a zero score is a boundary tie.

A support vector has a nonzero multiplier in the solution. In the separable case, complementarity puts it on a margin hyperplane. Points beyond the margin have αi=0 and do not contribute to w or the decision sum.

f(z)=∑i∈SVαiyi⟨xi,z⟩+b

Soft margin: slack, penalty, and KKT cases

Noise, label errors, or overlap can make the hard constraints infeasible. Nonnegative slack ξi allows a point to fall short of the unit margin. The lecture gives a general power penalty k and says k=1 is commonly used.

minw,b,ξ12‖w‖2+C(∑iξi)ksubject toyi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)≥1−ξi,ξi≥0

Here ξi is per-example slack, C>0 weights its penalty against margin width, and k is the power applied to the aggregate slack sum. For convexity of this nonnegative-slack penalty, use k≥1. C is normally chosen with a validation set or cross-validation. For k=1, the source derives the dual below with 0≤αi≤C. That bound follows from the linear-slack derivation; do not transfer it to arbitrary k without deriving the dual.

Teaching check: where the penalty power applies

For slacks ξ1=1, ξ2=2 and k=2, the lecture's aggregate-slack penalty is C(1+2)2=9C. Raising each slack separately would give C(12+22)=5C, a different objective. At k=1, both expressions reduce to C(1+2)=3C, so the following linear-slack derivation is unchanged.

For k=1:maxα∑iαi−12∑i∑jαiαjyiyj⟨xi,xj⟩,∑iαiyi=0,0≤αi≤C

Let mi=yif(xi) be the signed score. The minimum slack is ξi=max(0,1−mi). Beyond the correct margin, slack is zero; between the boundary and margin it is between 0 and 1; on the decision boundary it is 1; for a misclassified point (mi<0) it exceeds 1.

ξi=max(0,1−yif(xi))
Soft-margin cases: read signed score, slack, and multiplier together

Soft-margin geometry and KKT states

Definitions: signed score mᵢ=yᵢf(xᵢ); slack is the minimum nonnegative amount satisfying mᵢ≥1−ξᵢ. The multiplier partition is for the source’s k=1 case.

1. Correct side, beyond the margin

Let mᵢ=1.4. Then ξᵢ=max(0,1−1.4)=0 and the constraint has surplus 0.4. KKT permits αᵢ=0. Conversely, an example with α equal to zero can lie exactly on the margin in a degenerate optimum; that converse concerns m equal to 1, not this example’s fixed 1.4.

2. Exactly on a margin boundary

mᵢ=1 gives ξᵢ=0 and a tight constraint. For k=1, 0<αᵢ<C implies mᵢ=1 and ξᵢ=0.

3. Correctly classified inside the margin

For mᵢ=0.4, ξᵢ=0.6. The signed score is positive, so the label is correct, but it misses the unit margin. KKT permits αᵢ=C.

4. On the decision boundary

mᵢ=0 gives ξᵢ=1. The score is tied at zero, so a tie convention determines the prediction.

5. Misclassified

For mᵢ=−0.3, ξᵢ=1.3. The negative signed score means misclassification; for k=1, αᵢ=C is permitted.

6. Read the complete k=1 KKT partition

αᵢ=0⇒mᵢ≥1,ξᵢ=0; 0<αᵢ<C⇒mᵢ=1,ξᵢ=0; αᵢ=C⇒mᵢ≤1,ξᵢ≥0. The last case includes an exact-margin point with zero slack, a point inside the margin, or an error.

The soft-margin Lagrangian adds μi≥0 for each constraint ξi≥0. For k=1, stationarity in ξi gives C−αi−μi=0. Since μi≥0, it follows that αi≤C. Slack and μi then disappear from the dual, leaving the hard-margin objective plus the upper bound.

LP=12‖w‖2+C∑iξi−∑iαi[yi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)−1+ξi]−∑iμiξi
∂LP∂ξi=C−αi−μi=0,μi≥0⟹0≤αi≤C

For a free support vector j with 0<αj<C, KKT gives ξj=0 and yjf(xj)=1. Solve for b and substitute w=∑iαiyixi. The slide’s bias formula has an index error and trailing “=0”; this corrected expression follows from its own equalities.

b=yj−⟨w,xj⟩=yj−∑iαiyi⟨xi,xj⟩

If no free support vector exists, this formula cannot be applied as written; another bias recovery convention or solver boundary information is needed. Since yj=±1, one could also write 1/yj=yj, but solving directly from the margin equality avoids that unnecessary division.

Supplemental calculation: recover b from a free support vector

Suppose a solved k=1 dual has a free vector j with yj=+1 and weighted dot-product sum ∑iαiyi⟨xi,xj⟩=0.25. Its equality is yj(⟨w,xj⟩+b)=1, so b=1−0.25=0.75. Check: the recovered point’s score is 0.25+0.75=1, hence signed margin 1. The sum is an explicit teaching assumption, not a source training result.

Feature maps and the kernel trick

A linear boundary in input space may not separate curved patterns. Map each input through a nonlinear feature map ϕ:X→F, then use a linear SVM in feature space. The map can add so many dimensions that explicitly constructing each vector is impractical. The dual and prediction need only feature-space dot products, so a kernel computes them from original inputs.

K(x,z)=⟨ϕ(x),ϕ(z)⟩

The lecture maps a 2D input into three coordinates. For (x1,x2)=(2,3), calculate 22=4, 32=9, and 2(2)(3)=62≈8.485, rounded to 8.5. The class label remains −1 because only the representation changes.

ϕ(x1,x2)=(x12,x22,2x1x2)
Source example: map the labeled point ((2, 3), −1)

Explicit feature-map calculation

Given: source example ((2,3),−1) and map φ(x₁,x₂)=(x₁²,x₂²,√2x₁x₂).

1. Map first coordinate

Given (x₁,x₂)=(2,3), the first feature is x₁²=2²=4.

2. Map second coordinate

The second feature is x₂²=3²=9.

3. Map the interaction coordinate

The third is √2x₁x₂=√2·2·3=6√2≈8.485, which rounds to 8.5 at one decimal place.

4. Keep the label and check

The mapped labeled example is ((4,9,8.5),−1), matching the slide. Coordinates changed; the class label did not.

For a degree-2 polynomial, expand the input dot product and match terms to a feature-space dot product. The 2 factor is essential: its product contributes the middle term.

(⟨x,z⟩)2=(x1z1+x2z2)2=x12z12+2x1z1x2z2+x22z22=⟨(x12,x22,2x1x2),(z12,z22,2z1z2)⟩
Derivation check: explicit feature dot product equals the kernel

Set x=(x1,x2) and z=(z1,z2). The explicit dot product is x12z12+x22z22+(2x1x2)(2z1z2). The last term is 2x1x2z1z2. Expanding (x·z)2 gives the same three terms, so K(x,z)=(x·z)2 computes that feature-space dot product without constructing either feature vector.

A kernel substitution is valid when the function is a dot product in some feature space; the lecture names Mercer’s theorem but does not develop its conditions. Not every parameter choice for every displayed formula is guaranteed to be a valid positive semidefinite kernel. Preserve the Gaussian parameterization as shown, with 2σ in the denominator and σ>0; do not silently replace it with the alternative 2σ2 convention.

Polynomial K(x,z)=(⟨x,z⟩+θ)d θ∈R, d∈N. The degree-2 zero-offset example is expanded above.

Sigmoid K(x,z)=tanh(k⟨x,z⟩−δ) k,δ∈R as listed; validity may require additional restrictions.

The kernel trick replaces each ⟨ϕ(xi),ϕ(xj)⟩ in the dual with K(xi,xj), and the test dot product with K(xi,z). This avoids explicit feature vectors, but still depends on a valid kernel calculation.

f(z)=∑i∈SVαiyiK(xi,z)+b
Teaching example: evaluate four kernel values for one pair

Kernel calculation cells

Teaching example inputs: x=(1,2), z=(2,0), and illustrative parameters θ=1,d=2,σ=2,k=0.5,δ=0.2. The dot product is 2 and squared distance is 5.

1. Set the shared pair and dot product

Choose x=(1,2), z=(2,0). Then x·z=1·2+2·0=2. Also ||x−z||²=(1−2)²+(2−0)²=1+4=5.

2. Linear and polynomial cells

Linear: Kₗᵢₙ=2. For the teaching parameters θ=1,d=2, polynomial: Kₚₒₗᵧ=(2+1)²=9.

3. Gaussian cell in the lecture parameterization

Choose σ=2. Keep the source denominator 2σ: KRBF=exp(−5/(2·2))=exp(−1.25)≈0.2865.

4. Sigmoid cell and interpretation

Choose k=0.5,δ=0.2. Then Kₛᵢg=tanh(0.5·2−0.2)=tanh(0.8)≈0.664. These chosen values illustrate the listed formulas; this calculation does not establish that the selected sigmoid parameters define a valid positive semidefinite kernel.

Practical boundaries of the method

The source treats SVM as a real-valued, two-class method. Categorical attributes need numeric representation. Multi-class tasks need strategies such as one-against-rest or error-correcting output coding. A learned hyperplane, especially with a nonlinear kernel, can be difficult to interpret. Classifier performance depends on the data, representation, kernel and validation choices.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing score and distance: f(x) is a signed score; perpendicular distance is |f(x)|/‖w‖.
  • Forgetting canonical scaling: scores ±1 describe the margins only after rescaling the same hyperplane to unit functional margin.
  • Wrong negative-class inequality: yi=−1 in the signed constraint gives f(xi)≤−1.
  • Calling every example a support vector: only nonzero dual multipliers contribute to the solution.
  • Treating αi as a probability or error: it is a multiplier attached to a training constraint.
  • Confusing slack and error: 0<ξi<1 can mean correctly classified inside the margin; ξi>1 means negative signed score.
  • Applying αi≤C for every penalty power: the source derives it for k=1.
  • Recovering b from any margin point: the stated formula needs 0<αj<C.
  • Changing the Gaussian denominator: use the lecture’s 2σ form.
  • Assuming every sigmoid parameter pair is valid: validity needs more than the displayed formula.
  • Assuming a zero score has a universal class: it is a boundary tie; declare a policy if needed.

Revision checklist

  • Identify w as normal, b as offset, and f(x) as signed score.
  • Convert score to perpendicular distance by dividing by ‖w‖.
  • Explain canonical rescaling and why full margin is 2/‖w‖.
  • Write the hard-margin primal and both signed-label cases.
  • Derive stationarity in w and b, then list feasibility and complementarity.
  • Explain why nonzero multipliers identify support vectors and why the dual uses dot products.
  • Distinguish general k penalty from the k=1 bound 0≤αi≤C.
  • Determine slack from signed score; interpret the k=1 multiplier partition.
  • Recover b only from a free support vector using margin equality.
  • Reproduce the feature map and polynomial expansion; state kernel forms accurately.

Practice questions

1. A point has score −6 and the normal has norm 3. What is its distance from the boundary, and which side is it on?

Check your answer

Distance is |−6|/3=2. It is on the negative-score side. Correctness still depends on its true label.

2. Why can maximizing the canonical margin be written as minimizing one half the squared norm of w?

Check your answer

The full margin is 2/‖w‖. Maximizing it minimizes ‖w‖; minimizing 12‖w‖2 has the same minimizer and gives a differentiable quadratic objective.

3. For a negative example, what raw score does the hard-margin signed constraint require?

Check your answer

With yi=−1, −f(xi)≥1, so f(xi)≤−1.

4. A k=1 point has signed score 0.65. Find minimum slack and say whether it is correctly classified.

Check your answer

ξ=max(0,1−0.65)=0.35. Its signed score is positive, so it is correctly classified but inside the unit margin.

5. Under k=1 KKT conditions, what follows when αᵢ=C?

Check your answer

The signed score is mi≤1 and slack is nonnegative. The point may be exactly on the margin with zero slack, inside the margin, or misclassified; αi=C alone does not prove an error.

6. Why does the degree-2 feature map scale its interaction coordinate by √2?

Check your answer

The dot product contributes (2x1x2)(2z1z2)=2x1x2z1z2, the middle term of (x1z1+x2z2)2.

7. What does a kernel provide when the feature-space method only needs φ(x)·φ(z)?

Check your answer

It computes that feature-space dot product from the original inputs: K(x,z)=ϕ(x)·ϕ(z). Feature vectors need not be explicitly constructed.
