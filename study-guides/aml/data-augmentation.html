Data Augmentation for Images and Text

Augmentation creates varied training examples only when their task-relevant meaning and labels stay valid; this guide connects text strategies to a controlled image-classification assignment.

Big picture

Data augmentation makes additional training examples from existing examples. The goal is to expose a learner to useful variation so it can generalize beyond the exact training items. A transformation is useful only when it preserves the information the task depends on and keeps the label correct. More examples are not automatically better: a fluent sentence can change its meaning, and an image transform can change the visual cue that defines its class.

Augmentation depends on the kind of data and on what the task asks a model to recognize. The introductory multimodal illustration gives these concrete examples:

  • Images: a butterfly image is flipped or rotated, de-texturized, de-colored, edge-enhanced, or converted to a salient-edge map. These changes vary orientation or visual features; a useful transformation must retain the cues required by the image label.
  • Text: an airport example distinguishes the events “capture” and “airlifting” and their participant roles. Rule-based, generative, text-aware, and event-aware variants can alter the wording, event, or who fills a role. Check the event and its participants against the required annotation; changed roles or events can invalidate the original label even when the text remains grammatical.
  • Audio: adding noise, stretching in time, and shifting a signal produce different views of an original recording. Their suitability depends on whether the task's essential sound information remains intact. No numerical settings are assumed for these illustrations.

The separate flower assignment supplies a concrete image-classification workflow. Its split, preprocessing, model, and experiment numbers below describe the canonical instructional notebook, not mandatory choices for every valid student design.

Prerequisites: review Imbalanced Data: Metrics and Resampling and Convolutional Neural Networks first.

What makes an augmented example useful?

An original training example can produce one or more transformed descendants. Each descendant should add variation while remaining a valid example of the same task. This is label preservation: the target attached to the example remains correct after the change. If that condition fails, the training set gains conflicting supervision instead of useful diversity.

Augmentation is especially attractive when annotated examples are limited, models can overfit small training sets, or high-quality labels are costly to obtain. NLP applications include machine translation, text classification, question answering, and sentiment analysis. Each task requires its own meaning and label checks. Augmentation varies the training view; it does not create independent source facts or guarantee generalization. Keep validation and test examples separate from augmentation so they remain evidence about examples the learner did not train on.

Text strategies, from small edits to generated examples

The lecture groups text augmentation by the scale of the change. Moving from a word edit toward synthetic examples can create more surface diversity, while also increasing the chance that intended meaning or the task label changes. The labels in the comparison table are qualitative judgments from the lecture, not measured quantities.

Word-level changes

Word-level methods modify individual words while trying to preserve the sentence meaning. The lecture's sentiment example changes “The movie was good” to “The movie was excellent.” Its semantic-similarity example changes “The student completed the assignment” to “The learner completed the assignment.” Other listed techniques are synonym replacement, random insertion, random deletion, word swapping, word replacement, and spelling or grammar correction.

A nearby synonym is not always equivalent in context. Check sentiment, entities, relations, negation, and the task label. A spelling correction may be safe for one task but harmful when spelling itself is the label.

Teaching example: check a word replacement against the task label

Givens. The original is “The movie was good.” and the task is sentiment classification. The proposed replacement is “The movie was excellent.”

  1. Identify what the label depends on: the sentence's positive or negative sentiment.
  2. Compare the changed word in context: both “good” and “excellent” express positive sentiment, with “excellent” often stronger.
  3. Check the complete sentence and its label. The example remains positive, so the label is preserved for this stated task.

Final check. The replacement is a plausible positive-sentiment example, but this one check does not prove every synonym replacement is safe. A different task, such as exact semantic similarity, may require a stricter equivalence test.

Phrase-level changes

Phrase-level augmentation changes groups of words rather than isolated tokens. The lecture lists phrase replacement, phrase paraphrasing, insertion or deletion, and entity replacement. Its example adds a modifier: “The government introduced a new policy” becomes “The government introduced a new environmental policy.” The added phrase must be grammatical, semantically appropriate, and consistent with the task label. Phrase edits can also alter which entity did what, so verify the full relation rather than checking only grammaticality.

Teaching example: audit the phrase “environmental policy”

Givens. Original: “The government introduced a new policy.” Augmented: “The government introduced a new environmental policy.”

  1. Grammar: the inserted adjective phrase fits before “policy.”
  2. Meaning: “environmental” narrows the kind of policy; it adds a claim that was not explicit in the original.
  3. Task label: retain the example only if that narrower claim is compatible with the original label and the task does not require exact semantic equivalence.

Final check. The sentence is grammatical, but grammaticality alone is insufficient. If the new domain claim is unsupported or changes the label, reject the augmentation.

Sentence-level paraphrasing

A paraphrase is a different sentence intended to express approximately the same meaning. The lecture lists rule-based, synonym-based, neural, and LLM-based paraphrasing. Its example is “The meeting was postponed because of heavy rain” → “The meeting was delayed due to heavy rainfall.” The benefit is linguistic diversity without completely new source information; the risk is unintended meaning change.

When checking a generated sentence, compare the event, participants, time, quantities, negation, and causal relation. Fluent output can still change an entity, role, or polarity. For example, changing who postponed a meeting or removing “not” can reverse a label even if the result reads naturally.

Teaching example: compare the heavy-rain paraphrase

Givens. Original: “The meeting was postponed because of heavy rain.” Candidate: “The meeting was delayed due to heavy rainfall.”

  1. Event: “postponed” and “delayed” both describe moving the meeting to a later time.
  2. Cause: both sentences attribute the change to heavy rain.
  3. Participants and polarity: the meeting remains the affected event; neither sentence introduces a negation or a new participant.
  4. Label check: keep the candidate only if the task label depends on this preserved event and cause rather than exact wording.

Final check. These two sentences are a plausible paraphrase pair. Review generated paraphrases case by case; approximate similarity does not certify label preservation.

Translation-based and language-model methods

Back-translation is a translation-based strategy: translate a sentence through another language and back to obtain a differently worded candidate. The lecture does not specify a language pair, model, or round-trip settings, so none are assumed here. Translation errors can change meaning, entities, or labels; compare the returned text against the original before retaining it.

Contextual methods use a surrounding sentence to propose alternatives. In the lecture's masked-language-model example, “The government announced a new [MASK]” can be completed with “policy,” “regulation,” or “initiative.” Transformer or LLM-based methods can also generate paraphrases, new synthetic sentences, question-answer pairs, and synthetic labeled examples. Context can make a proposal more appropriate than a context-free word swap, but it does not validate factual content or labels. Inspect each generated example, including who acts, what happened, and whether negation or quantities changed.

Compare diversity with meaning risk

The lecture's table is a qualitative guide to the trade-off. Higher diversity is not a quality score; the practical choice depends on whether a task can tolerate the method's meaning risk.

Fixed, per-class split calculation

Teaching example — assignment quantities. Five classes, 80 originals per class, and a 60/20/20 split with seed 42.

1. Count the selected images

There are 5×80=400 originals. The class-block verification remains an instruction to the student before using a dataset.

2. Allocate each class with the fixed ratio

For each class, the 60/20/20 split gives 0.60×80=48 training, 0.20×80=16 validation, and 0.20×80=16 test images. Check: 48+16+16=80 for every class.

3. Check the overall totals

Training: 5×48=240. Validation: 5×16=80. Test: 5×16=80. Final check: 240+80+80=400, with the same 48/16/16 class counts in each split.

4. Freeze the split before augmentation

Use seed 42 to shuffle each class and save the selected file lists once. Reuse those lists in every experiment. Create augmented descendants only from the 240 original training images. Validation is for checkpoint selection; evaluate the untouched test set after selection.

Preprocessing and training-only normalization

The canonical notebook converts each image to RGB, directly resizes it to 96×96 with bilinear interpolation, and scales unsigned pixel values by 255 into [0,1]. It computes a separate mean and population standard deviation for each RGB channel from only the 240 original training images. Those same fixed values normalize training, validation, and test tensors. Statistics must not be refit on validation or test, because those sets are held out for model selection and final evaluation.

Labels are integer values 0–4 in alphabetical class order. The notebook uses cross-entropy loss, which consumes integer class targets; one-hot encoding is unnecessary for that loss. The assignment permits one-hot labels where a chosen loss requires them.

Supplemental calculation: fit channel statistics on training originals

Population mean and standard deviation

Givens. Two synthetic scaled RGB pixels: A = (0.2, 0.4, 0.6), B = (0.6, 0.8, 1.0). These toy numbers demonstrate the calculation only.

1. State a tiny synthetic training set

Supplemental calculation — synthetic values, not flower measurements. Use two one-pixel RGB training examples after scaling: A = (0.2, 0.4, 0.6), B = (0.6, 0.8, 1.0). Compute each channel independently over these N = 2 training pixels.

2. Compute the channel means

Red: (0.2+0.6)/2=0.4. Green: (0.4+0.8)/2=0.6. Blue: (0.6+1.0)/2=0.8. The population denominator is N=2.

3. Compute population standard deviations

For red, the deviations from 0.4 are −0.2 and +0.2, so variance=((−0.2)2+0.22)/2=0.04 and σ=0.2. Green and blue have the same deviations and standard deviation 0.2. This uses denominator N, not N−1.

4. Apply the same fixed statistics

Normalize A channel by channel: (0.2−0.4)/0.2=−1, (0.4−0.6)/0.2=−1, and (0.6−0.8)/0.2=−1. Thus A becomes (−1,−1,−1); B becomes (1,1,1). Apply these training-derived values unchanged to every split.

Check. The two normalized values in each channel have mean 0 and population standard deviation 1. In the assignment, the statistics instead use all pixels of the 240 original training images only.

Convolution and classifier parameter count

Givens. Six padded 3×3 convolutions in channel pairs 3→16→16, 16→32→32, 32→64→64; then 576→64→5 linear layers. Count one bias per output channel/unit.

1. Count one convolution

For a 3×3 convolution with Cin input channels and Cout output channels, each output filter has 3×3×Cin weights and one bias: (3×3×Cin+1)×Cout.

2. Count the first block

3→16:(9×3+1)×16=448. 16→16:(9×16+1)×16=2,320. Block total = 448+2,320=2,768.

3. Count the second and third blocks

16→32:(9×16+1)×32=4,640; 32→32:(9×32+1)×32=9,248; subtotal 4,640+9,248=13,888. 32→64:(9×32+1)×64=18,496; 64→64:(9×64+1)×64=36,928; subtotal 18,496+36,928=55,424.

4. Count the classifier and total

After pooling: 64×12×12 becomes 64×3×3=576 features. Dense 576→64:(576+1)×64=36,928. Dense 64→5:(64+1)×5=325. Sum all six convolutions and two dense layers: 2,768+13,888+55,424+36,928+325=109,333.

Final check. ReLU, max/average pooling, flattening, and dropout add no trainable parameters. The total matches the independently recounted layer counts.

The notebook trains with Adam, learning rate 0.001, weight decay 0.0001, batch size 32, 40 epochs, and cross-entropy. The lowest validation-loss checkpoint is retained. It reports accuracy and macro-F1; macro-F1 gives each class equal weight. The test set is evaluated only after checkpoint selection. These are notebook choices for an instructional baseline, not measured outcomes or compulsory assignment-wide settings.

Image transforms and controlled ablations

Every run keeps the 240 original training images. Transformed descendants are added only to training; validation and test remain fixed. The notebook uses a horizontal mirror, a rotation with angle magnitude from 8° to 30° whose sign alternates by variant, bilinear interpolation and black fill, and color jitter with brightness 0.2, contrast 0.2, saturation 0.15, and unchanged hue. The combined transform applies rotation, color jitter, and alternating mirror. The notebook preserves hue because it may distinguish flower categories.

The amount ablation varies how many combined variants are added per original. The separate type ablation compares flip, rotation, color, and combined methods with one added variant per original. Both use the same saved split, model initialization, epochs, batch size, and other training settings. This keeps those controls fixed, but same epochs do not mean the optimizer makes the same number of updates.

The runs share an epoch budget and batch size, not an optimizer-update budget. Larger sets receive more parameter updates over 40 epochs. What to record and how to interpret evidence

The notebook's comparison fields make the experiment auditable: experiment name and technique, original-to-augmented ratio, original/added/total training sizes, epochs, batch size, optimizer updates, best epoch, validation loss/accuracy/macro-F1, test loss/accuracy/macro-F1, run time, initial-weight hash, and split-manifest hash. The compact comparison focuses on experiment, ratio, sizes, updates, best epoch, validation loss/accuracy, and test accuracy/macro-F1. Supporting outputs include per-class reports, a confusion matrix, training/validation accuracy and loss curves, example transform views, split and augmented-class counts, and training-set pixel statistics.

For an amount comparison, look for a consistent change across the combined 1×, 2×, and 4× runs while remembering that update counts rise with set size. For a type comparison, hold the 1× ratio fixed and compare flip, rotation, color, and combined. Use validation results to select a checkpoint; use the untouched test set only for the final evaluation after that choice. A single score is not the whole explanation: inspect per-class metrics and the confusion matrix for uneven errors, and compare training with validation curves for signs of overfitting.

The assignment asks the student to justify the model and training settings, show curves and original-versus-augmented examples, compare augmentation amount and type, and submit their own notebook, image folder, and discussion. Common mistakes

  • Treating fluent text as proof that the meaning or label stayed the same; explicitly inspect entities, roles, negation, quantities, and relations.
  • Assuming more or more diverse examples must improve generalization; noisy or label-changing descendants can make training worse.
  • Inventing a back-translation language pair or settings that the lecture does not state.
  • Augmenting before splitting, or fitting channel statistics on validation/test data; both leak held-out information into training.
  • Re-splitting between experiments; this makes score differences harder to attribute to the augmentation change.
  • Confusing integer labels with one-hot labels; cross-entropy in the notebook uses integer targets.
  • Calling the notebook CNN a mandatory architecture; the assignment allows students to design and justify their own.
  • Comparing augmentation ratios as if equal epochs implied equal optimizer updates; record optimizer_steps.
  • Selecting a checkpoint with test performance or repeatedly consulting test results; validation selects, test evaluates after selection.
  • Treating planned comparison fields or code as evidence of measured scores; only actual student runs can fill the outcome columns.

Exam focus

  • Define augmentation and label preservation; explain why diversity can help while meaning drift can hurt.
  • Distinguish word, phrase, sentence, translation-based, and language-model-based strategies, and name the source examples.
  • State the qualitative diversity/meaning-risk ranking without describing it as measured data.
  • Reproduce the five class blocks, fixed 48/16/16 per-class split, and totals 240/80/80.
  • Explain why split files are saved/reused, why augmentation is training-only, and why validation selects the checkpoint before test evaluation.
  • Describe 96 × 96 RGB bilinear resize, scaling, training-only per-channel population statistics, and integer labels with cross-entropy.
  • Trace the canonical CNN shapes and independently check its 109,333 trainable parameters.
  • Distinguish amount ablation from type ablation; calculate updates with ceil(N/32)×40 and explain the same-epochs limitation.
  • Interpret result-table fields, curves, per-class reports, and a confusion matrix without inventing outcomes.

Practice questions

1. A paraphrase reads fluently but changes “The agency did not approve the request” to “The agency approved the request.” Should the original label be copied?

Check your answer

No. Removing “not” reverses the proposition. Fluency does not preserve meaning; reject the candidate or assign a label only after task-specific verification.

2. For five classes of 80 images each, how many examples go to each split under a 60/20/20 per-class allocation?

Check your answer

Each class contributes 48 training, 16 validation, and 16 test images. Across five classes the totals are 240,80,80, which sum to 400.

3. Why should channel means and standard deviations be estimated from the original training split only?

Check your answer

They are learned preprocessing values. Using validation or test pixels would let held-out data influence training. Estimate them from the 240 original training images, then reuse the fixed values on every split.

4. After the third max-pool layer, what is the canonical notebook’s spatial shape and how many values reach the dense layer?

Check your answer

The spatial tensor is 64×12×12. Average pooling by 4 gives 64×3×3, which flattens to 64×3×3=576 values.

5. How many optimizer updates does a 720-example training set receive over 40 epochs with batch size 32 and a retained final partial batch?

Check your answer

There are ⌈720/32⌉=⌈22.5⌉=23 batches each epoch. Over 40 epochs: 23×40=920 updates.

6. Which experiment answers an augmentation-amount question, and which answers a transform-type question?

Check your answer

Amount compares combined augmentation at 1×, 2×, and 4× (with baseline 1:0). Type compares flip, rotation, color, and combined at 1×. The split and other training controls are reused.

7. What may be inferred from the canonical notebook’s table schema before running it?

Check your answer

Only what fields and comparisons are planned, along with the specified dataset counts and settings. The schema does not establish accuracy, macro-F1, a winning transform, or an augmentation trend.
