GANs: Objective, Training, and Failure Modes

Understand how an implicit generator learns through an adversarial discriminator, why the objectives differ, and where training can fail.

Big picture

A discriminative model answers a conditional question such as “which label belongs to this image?” by learning a relationship like P(y|x). A generative model instead learns how observations are distributed so it can produce new samples. A GAN makes that learning problem adversarial: a generator proposes samples, while a discriminator learns to tell real data from generated data. Their alternating updates make the discriminator a training critic that supplies a differentiable learning signal to the generator.

From discrimination to probabilistic generation

The data distribution is the pattern of observations the model should reproduce. Explicit density models provide a formula or an approximation for the probability of a particular observation; implicit models provide a way to draw samples without an easy density calculation. The lecture distinguishes analytical explicit models such as a Gaussian, approximate explicit models such as Boltzmann machines with Monte Carlo probability estimates, and implicit models such as GANs. GAN sampling is a forward computation, but vanilla GAN training does not ordinarily return a tractable value of P(x) for a chosen image.

For an explicit model family PG(x;θ), maximum likelihood selects parameters that give observed training examples high probability. Taking logs converts the product of sample probabilities into a sum. In expectation, maximizing this log likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the forward KL divergence from the data distribution to the model. The second integral below does not depend on θ; it is the data entropy term.

L(θ)=∏i=1mPG(x(i);θ),θ*=argmaxθ∑i=1mlogPG(x(i);θ)
𝔼x~Pdata[logPG(x;θ)]=∫Pdata(x)logPG(x;θ)dx
DKL(Pdata‖PG)=∫Pdata(x)logPdata(x)dx−∫Pdata(x)logPG(x;θ)dx
argmaxθ𝔼x~Pdata[logPG(x;θ)]=argminθDKL(Pdata‖PG)

This likelihood derivation assumes an explicit density that can be evaluated. It is not a likelihood computation for a vanilla GAN. KL is directional: reversing its arguments changes the quantity. Jensen–Shannon divergence instead compares each distribution to their average M=(P+Q)/2, making the result symmetric.

DKL(P‖Q)=∫P(x)logP(x)Q(x)dx,DJS(P‖Q)=12DKL(P‖M)+12DKL(Q‖M)

The lecture motivates flexible generators by noting that changing only the parameters of a simple Gaussian-mixture family still restricts the kinds of densities it can represent. Treat that as an illustration about the chosen family, not a claim that maximum likelihood or Gaussian mixtures always fail.

Why the MLE derivation does not give a vanilla GAN likelihood

Teaching example. An explicit Gaussian model can evaluate its density at an observed x, so its log likelihood can be summed across data points. A vanilla generator can sample G(z) and compare its samples adversarially, but the ordinary network does not supply the normalized density pG(x) needed in that likelihood sum. Sampling ability and density evaluation are different capabilities.

Noise, generator, and discriminator

Draw a latent vector z from a simple noise prior, such as Gaussian or uniform noise. The differentiable generator maps it to an observation x̃=G(z;θg). It need not be invertible. The original slide says z is typically higher-dimensional than x, but that cannot be a general rule: the same lecture later shows a 100-value latent vector expanding to a 64×64×3 output, or 12,288 image values. Latent and output dimensions depend on the architecture; do not infer a universal inequality.

The discriminator receives either a sampled real observation or a generated one and returns a realness probability D(x). Real examples have target 1; generated examples have target 0 during discriminator training. The discriminator learns the conditional real/fake decision from these two classes. At generation time, the trained generator needs noise and G; the discriminator’s role is to shape training.

The minimax game and its generator variants

With equal weight for real and generated examples, the original value function rewards D for assigning high scores to real data and low scores to generated data. D maximizes this value; G minimizes it. Expectations over real data and latent noise are written separately. In the equations below, pz names the latent prior that the lecture also denotes by q(z):

V(D,G)=𝔼x~pdata[logD(x)]+𝔼z~pz[log(1−D(G(z)))]
minGmaxDV(D,G)

Under the literal minimax game, the generator minimizes only the fake term because the real-data term does not depend on G. When D is confident that generated data is fake, the derivative of log(1−D(G(z))) through a sigmoid logit is −D(G(z)), which approaches zero as the fake score approaches zero. The lecture therefore presents a non-saturating alternative: keep the same discriminator objective, but train G to maximize logD(G(z)), equivalently minimize its negative. These are different generator objectives; the non-saturating loss is not obtained by negating an unrelated discriminator loss.

LGminimax=𝔼z~pz[log(1−D(G(z)))]
LGNS=−𝔼z~pz[logD(G(z))]
Why the non-saturating objective can preserve an early signal

Let the discriminator output be D(G(z))=σ(a), where a is its logit. For literal minimax, the derivative with respect to that logit is:

∂∂alog(1−σ(a))=−σ(a)=−D(G(z))

As D(G(z))→0, this derivative tends to zero. For the non-saturating loss:

∂∂a[−logσ(a)]=σ(a)−1=D(G(z))−1

As the fake score approaches zero, this derivative approaches −1 rather than zero. This compares the output-logit factors; the complete parameter gradient also includes the derivatives through D, G, and the selected optimizer.

Optimal discriminator and ideal game value

For a fixed generator, D can be optimized separately at each input. The real and fake class probabilities at that input determine its best realness score. With balanced class priors, maximizing pdata(x)logD(x)+pG(x)log(1−D(x)) gives a density ratio. If the two class priors are not balanced, their weights must appear in that ratio.

D*(x)=pdata(x)pdata(x)+pG(x)

Derive and check the optimal discriminator

AssumptionsFixed generator distribution; balanced real/fake prior weighting.At one input x: a=p_data(x), b=p_G(x), and d=D(x).

1. Isolate the discriminator’s pointwise decision

For fixed distributions at a particular x, write a=pdata(x) and b=pG(x). With equally weighted real and generated classes, the terms depending on D(x)=d are f(d)=alogd+blog(1−d). The balanced-class assumption comes from the lecture’s equal real/fake batch weighting.

f(d)=alogd+blog(1−d),0<d<1

2. Differentiate and solve the interior optimum

Differentiate the pointwise objective and set the derivative to zero:

f′(d)=ad−b1−d=0⟹a(1−d)=bd

Rearrange a−ad=bd to obtain a=d(a+b). When a+b>0, the solution is:

D*(x)=d=pdata(x)pdata(x)+pG(x)

3. Check curvature and support edge cases

The second derivative is nonpositive in the interior, so the stationary point maximizes the concave objective. At the boundaries: if a>0,b=0, the optimum is d=1; if a=0,b>0, it is d=0. If both densities are zero, the ratio is undefined and the objective contributes no constraint on D(x). With unequal real/fake class priors, prior weights multiply the corresponding densities.

f′′(d)=−ad2−b(1−d)2≤0

4. Verify the balanced ideal match

If pG(x)=pdata(x)>0, the ratio gives D*(x)=1/2. Substitution into the game value yields V=−log4+2DJS(Pdata‖PG); at equality, Jensen–Shannon divergence is zero and the value is −log4. This is an ideal fixed-distribution/optimal-D result, not a guarantee that practical alternating SGD reaches it.

PG=Pdata⟹D*(x)=12 on the shared support,V=−log4

Substituting D* into the two expectations combines the data term weighted by pdata and the generated term weighted by pG. Add and subtract the normalizing factor log2 inside each logarithm; the two resulting KL terms are exactly the Jensen–Shannon average with M=(Pdata+PG)/2:

V(D*,G)=∫pdatalogpdatapdata+pG+pGlogpGpdata+pGdx=−log4+2DJS(Pdata‖PG)

The divergence is minimized when the distributions match; then D*(x)=1/2 on their shared support and the value is −log4. Where both densities are zero, the ratio is undefined and the objective does not constrain D there. The ideal equilibrium presumes the generator can represent the data distribution and the discriminator is optimized for the current generator. These statements do not prove that finite neural networks trained with alternating SGD will converge.

Alternating training: two separate parameter updates

The lecture trains with mini-batches and stochastic gradient ascent for the displayed value function. First sample real examples and noise, generate fake examples, and update D while holding G fixed. Then use fresh noise and update G while holding D’s parameters fixed. The loss still backpropagates through D’s input and through G(z) to the generator parameters. Freezing D weights is not the same as stopping the gradient at D’s input. The slide’s brief description that a generative model learns P(x,y) appears while introducing real/fake labels; in this binary GAN, y is the discriminator’s class and D estimates P(y|x), while G represents the observation distribution through its samples.

Two symbolic rounds of alternating GAN training

Teaching schematicInitial current parameters: θd(0),θg(0)Each D batch contains real x~pdata and detached generated x~=G(z;θg) examples.Targets: real=1, generated=0. D ascent; G descent. No numeric weights, losses, or performance are assumed.

1. Begin with current parameters and the first real/noise batches

Teaching schematic, symbolic only. The lecture specifies this order but supplies no numeric initial weights. Start with current parameters θd(0) and θg(0). Draw a real mini-batch x(1:m)~pdata and noise z(1:m)~pz. Generate x~i=G(zi;θg(0)). The fake batch is held fixed for the discriminator update (detached from G for that phase).

{xi}i=1m,{zi}i=1m,x~i=G(zi;θg(0))

2. Update D using real and generated examples; hold G fixed

Use real target 1 and fake target 0. Maximize the balanced batch value with respect to θd only; θg=θg(0) receives no optimizer step. The two batch means each have weight 1/m, matching the lecture algorithm.

V~D=1m∑i=1mlogD(xi;θd)+1m∑i=1mlog(1−D(x~i;θd))

Gradient ascent gives θd(1)=θd(0)+ηd∇θdV~D. Check: only D parameters changed; fake samples came from the old fixed G.

θd(1)=θd(0)+ηd∇θdV~D

3. Update G through the updated but fixed D

Draw a fresh latent batch. Evaluate it with D(·;θd(1)). For the lecture’s non-saturating step, minimize LGNS=−1m∑ilogD(G(zi;θg);θd(1)) with respect to θg. D’s weights remain fixed, but the derivative through D’s input is retained:

LGNS⟶D output⟶D input G(z)⟶θg

Thus θg(1)=θg(0)−ηg∇θgLGNS. Check: only G parameters change. Detaching G(z) or blocking D’s input gradient would cut the learning path.

θg(1)=θg(0)−ηg∇θgLGNS

4. Start round two from the updated state

Use new real and noise batches and the current pair (θd(1),θg(1)). Generate current fakes with G(z;θg(1)); update D alone by ascent while G remains fixed.

θd(2)=θd(1)+ηd∇θdV~D(θd;θg(1))

5. Update G again and audit the two rounds

Draw another fresh noise batch, freeze θd(2) as parameters, preserve the derivative through D’s input, and apply the same selected non-saturating loss:

θg(2)=θg(1)−ηg∇θgLGNS(θg;θd(2))

State check: two D-only ascent updates alternate with two G-only descent updates. Targets stay real=1/fake=0. This demonstrates dependency and update order; it is not a numeric run, a convergence claim, or a measured model result. The original minimax generator objective is a separate option described below.

The source algorithm shows D ascent and G ascent on its stated rewards; this is equivalent to D ascent and descent on its negative loss, while the practical generator expression displayed in that algorithm is the non-saturating logD(G(z)) reward. Some presentations use a half factor on each discriminator expectation; that rescales the value but leaves its optimizer unchanged. The number of D updates per G update is a training choice; either model can be updated without changing the other's parameters.

Failure modes and what they look like

GAN training has more than one player, each changing the objective seen by the other. A single-player non-convex optimization may settle at a local optimum under suitable assumptions; that does not imply that alternating game updates find a Nash equilibrium. The lecture’s minxmaxyxy example has rotating gradient dynamics, illustrating why even a small step size need not settle. This is a schematic game example, not a GAN performance measurement. The lecture also highlights the apparent difficulty that G receives no direct labeled “correct sample” target and may rarely fool D. Backpropagation through D supplies a local direction for changing G, so a useful gradient can be available even when D still recognizes generated samples.

minxmaxyxy,∂(xy)∂x=y,∂(xy)∂y=x

Non-convergence means the adversarial updates can oscillate or fail to settle at the equilibrium. Mode collapse means the generator produces only a small subset of the data variation, even if those few samples look plausible. The lecture describes a discriminator that becomes strong too quickly, a generator that exploits only its narrow weaknesses, and outputs that omit modes. The two failures are related to training quality but are not synonyms.

Distinguish coverage failure from game instability

Supplemental teaching scenario. A batch of sharp samples repeatedly contains only one kind of object while the real data has many kinds. This signals poor diversity or mode collapse. If instead the sample distribution and discriminator scores swing back and forth as updates proceed, the symptom is unstable non-convergence. A good-looking individual sample does not establish broad distribution coverage.

Diversity remedies and other proposals from the lecture

The lecture lists straightforward sampling and not requiring maximum-likelihood training among GAN advantages, and says empirical GANs can capture distribution modes while warning that P(x) is implicit and not straightforward to compute. These are the lecture’s practical claims, not universal guarantees about overfitting or coverage.

It proposes input normalization, modified losses, spherical latent noise, batch normalization, avoiding sparse gradients (it names ReLU and MaxPool in this context), soft or noisy labels, DCGAN or hybrid models, early failure monitoring, using labels when available, and adding input noise that decays over time. These are proposals from the slides, not guaranteed fixes. In particular, the slide flags discriminator loss going to zero as an early warning sign.

The diversity-specific methods give the discriminator information about a group of generated samples. Minibatch features can summarize pairwise differences (the lecture gives L2 distances as an example); feeding these features alongside an image lets D recognize when a fake batch is less varied than real batches. The generator then receives pressure to match the real batch’s diversity cues. The lecture also shows a latent-representation nearest-neighbor treatment for finding overly similar generated samples and supervision with class labels when those labels exist.

pair feature for i:fi=∑j≠i‖h(xi)−h(xj)‖2

The expression is a compact teaching notation for the slide’s pairwise-distance idea; the slide does not prescribe this exact feature formula or feature map h. Treat the named techniques as examples of how batch-level evidence can reveal collapse. The slide’s label-supervision examples include class-conditioned generation and a multiresolution LAPGAN family; they are presented as remedies or related approaches, not as guaranteed outcomes.

A GAN and OCR workflow

The GAN/OCR workflow combines several stages: configure MNIST input and a model, train a GAN, train a separate digit classifier, pass generated candidates through a human-review gate, evaluate the stated stages, and serialize/package the required artifacts. Its latent-dimension setting is z_dim=100.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing P(y|x) classification with modeling the data distribution P(x).
  • Assuming a GAN’s ability to sample means it can evaluate pG(x) for likelihood or maximum-likelihood training.
  • Reversing the forward KL direction or calling JSD a single KL divergence.
  • Treating the latent dimension as universally larger or smaller than the output dimension.
  • Mixing the original minimax generator loss with the non-saturating loss or their gradient signs.
  • Freezing D’s weights by cutting D’s input gradient; G then receives no adversarial signal.
  • Applying the balanced optimal-discriminator ratio without accounting for class priors.
  • Claiming ideal equilibrium theory guarantees practical alternating-SGD convergence.
  • Treating non-convergence and mode collapse as the same failure.
  • Calling a proposed diversity or stabilization method a guaranteed cure.

Exam focus

  • A GAN is an implicit generative model: noise passes through G to create a sample, while D estimates realness during training.
  • For explicit-density MLE, maximizing data log likelihood minimizes DKL(Pdata‖PG).
  • Original objective: minGmaxDV(D,G) with real logD(x) and fake log(1−D(G(z))) terms.
  • Literal minimax and non-saturating generator losses are distinct; non-saturating minimizes −ElogD(G(z)) to avoid the vanishing output-logit factor.
  • Under balanced real/fake weighting, D*(x)=pdata(x)/(pdata(x)+pG(x)); at the ideal match, D*=1/2 on shared support.
  • D phase: update D only, using real and detached fake samples. G phase: update G only through a fixed-parameter but differentiable D.
  • Non-convergence concerns game dynamics; mode collapse concerns missing output diversity.
  • Minibatch features, latent nearest-neighbor checks, and available labels are lecture examples for improving diversity feedback; none is a universal convergence guarantee.

Practice questions

1. What does a discriminative model estimate, and what does a generative model aim to reproduce?

Check your answer

A discriminative model estimates a conditional relationship such as P(y|x). A generative model aims to represent the observation distribution P(x) and produce samples from it.

2. Why is a vanilla GAN called an implicit model?

Check your answer

It can draw samples by evaluating G(z), but ordinarily does not provide a tractable explicit density value pG(x) for an arbitrary observation.

3. Which KL direction follows from maximum likelihood for an explicit density model?

Check your answer

The forward direction DKL(Pdata‖PG), because the expectation in the log likelihood is over data.

4. Write the original GAN value function and state which player maximizes it.

Check your answer

V=Edata[logD(x)]+Ez[log(1−D(G(z)))]. The discriminator maximizes V; the generator minimizes it in the literal minimax game.

5. Why can the non-saturating generator objective give a stronger early output-logit gradient?

Check your answer

When D(G(z))→0, the minimax derivative factor is −D(G(z))→0; for −logD(G(z)), it is D(G(z))−1→−1.

6. What changes, and what must remain differentiable, during the generator phase?

Check your answer

Update generator parameters only. Hold discriminator parameters fixed, but preserve the derivative through D’s input and through G(z) to the generator.

7. Derive the balanced optimal discriminator and name its undefined edge case.

Check your answer

At one x, maximize alogd+blog(1−d). Setting a/d−b/(1−d)=0 gives d=a/(a+b)=pdata/(pdata+pG). If both densities are zero, the ratio is undefined and the objective imposes no unique value.

8. Distinguish non-convergence from mode collapse and name one diversity remedy from the slides.

Check your answer

Non-convergence is unstable or oscillating game updates; mode collapse is poor coverage with too few kinds of generated samples. Minibatch features or latent nearest-neighbor checks give D batch-level diversity evidence.
