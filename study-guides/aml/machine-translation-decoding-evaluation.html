Translation Decoding, Multilingual Models, and Evaluation

Translation systems must search through possible outputs, share evidence across languages, and be evaluated for meaning as well as surface overlap.

Big picture

A translation model assigns probabilities to possible target sequences, but the decoder must choose one sequence from an enormous space. Greedy decoding commits to the best next token; beam search keeps several partial hypotheses and prunes them by cumulative score. Multilingual pre-training and prompting try to reuse evidence across languages and contexts, while translation evaluation asks whether the output preserves meaning and reads naturally.

These stages answer different questions: decoding chooses an output, a multilingual model provides learned representations, and evaluation estimates quality. A high sequence score or a fluent sentence alone does not prove that the source meaning survived.

Autoregressive decoding: build a completed hypothesis

An autoregressive decoder generates one token at a time. At each step, its distribution depends on the source and the prefix already generated. The end-of-sequence token, written EOS, is a prediction that the sentence is complete; it is not an ordinary visible word in the translation.

A partial hypothesis is not yet a translation. Its score accumulates the log probability of every token, including EOS when it terminates. Since probabilities are at most one, their logarithms are non-positive; a higher (less negative) cumulative log score is better.

S(y1T)=∑t=1TlogP(yt∣y<t,x)

Here x is the source, y₁ … y_T is the generated target sequence, and a completed sequence includes its final EOS decision. Review the neural prerequisite's BPE subword-tokenization example for the token representation; this page focuses on search and evaluation.

Greedy choice versus beam search

Greedy decoding chooses the highest-probability token at each step and discards every other prefix. It is fast, but a locally attractive token may lead to a weaker complete sentence. Beam search retains the k highest-scoring partial hypotheses after expansion at each step. It explores more alternatives, but pruning still makes it an approximation to the global sequence search.

The lecture's normalization divides cumulative log probability by length raised to α. At α=0, the score is the unnormalized sum. At α=1, it is the average log probability per token. The formula below follows the lecture's token count; a finished hypothesis counts its EOS token in T for the worked toy example.

Snorm(y1T)=1Tα∑t=1TlogP(yt∣y<t,x)

Supplemental calculation. This made-up model has first-token probabilities P(A)=0.6 and P(B)=0.4. Greedy keeps A. A width-two beam keeps both. Their cumulative probabilities are 0.6 and 0.4; log scores are ln(0.6)=−0.510826 and ln(0.4)=−0.916291. Higher log score ranks first. Next, expand both prefixes.

2. Expand A and B, then prune

From A, P(EOS∣A)=0.4 and P(x∣A)=0.6, giving A EOS with probability 0.6·0.4=0.24, log score ln(0.24)=−1.427116, and a completed sentence; Ax has probability 0.6·0.6=0.36, log score ln(0.36)=−1.021651, and remains partial. From B, P(EOS∣B)=0.99 and P(y∣B)=0.01, giving B EOS at 0.4·0.99=0.396, log score ln(0.396)=−0.926341, completed; By at 0.4·0.01=0.004, log score ln(0.004)=−5.521461, partial. Each listed conditional distribution sums to one. Ranking all four, retain completed B EOS and partial Ax. The other two are pruned.

3. Expand the retained unfinished prefix Ax

B EOS is already completed, so keep it as a final candidate rather than expanding it. From Ax, P(EOS∣Ax)=0.8 and P(z∣Ax)=0.2. Therefore Ax EOS has probability 0.36·0.8=0.288, log score ln(0.288)=−1.244795, completed; Axz has probability 0.36·0.2=0.072, log score ln(0.072)=−2.631090, partial. These conditional probabilities also sum to one. Keep both the completed candidate and the unfinished prefix until a stopping check justifies termination.

4. Stop with a valid bound and compare completed candidates

Under α=0, every future conditional probability is at most one, so extending Axz cannot raise its cumulative probability above 0.072. It cannot beat the completed candidate at 0.396; thus this toy search can stop without discarding a potentially better completion. Compare completed outputs: B EOS has probability 0.396 and score ln(0.396)=−0.926341; Ax EOS has probability 0.288 and score ln(0.288)=−1.244795. The beam winner is B EOS. Greedy chose A, then its best continuation x, then EOS: 0.6·0.6·0.8=0.288. Check: the beam's completed candidate is better than greedy's completion, but this toy example does not make beam search an exact global method in general.

Multilingual models: transfer, direct paths, and capacity

Multilingual pre-training exposes a shared model to text from many languages. When languages share useful patterns, representations learned from a higher-resource language may help a related lower-resource language. This benefit depends on the languages, data, model, and task; the lecture's “few tens of thousands” fine-tuning example is not a guarantee.

The model counts below describe specific paper-era systems, not present-day service recommendations.

SystemPaper-era descriptionTranslation path or role
mBART (2020)Denoising over 25 monolingual languages with a shared vocabulary and target-language marker; mBART-50 later extended coverage to 50Multilingual sequence-to-sequence pre-training
mT5 (2021)Text-to-text pre-training on mC4, covering 101 languages; tasks use a text-to-text formatGeneral multilingual text-to-text transfer
M2M-100 (2020 paper; 2021 publication)Direct many-to-many translation among 100 languagesAvoids requiring English as the intermediate pivot
NLLB-200 (2022 preprint; 2024 journal publication)200-language translation effort and FLORES-200 evaluationExplicit attention to low-resource language coverage

A Sinhala → English → Tamil pivot can compound errors across two translation steps. It can also lose a distinction that English does not express before the second step. Direct translation avoids that intermediate bottleneck, but it is not automatically better: the available data and model quality for that direction still matter.

Sharing a fixed parameter budget across more languages creates a capacity trade-off, sometimes called the curse of multilinguality. This is a risk, not a universal law that every added language degrades every other language. Multilingual transfer and capacity pressure can coexist; which dominates must be measured for the task.

LLM translation: instructions, examples, and context

Zero-shot translation supplies an instruction and source text. Few-shot (in-context) translation adds example input–output pairs to the prompt. Those examples condition the current generation; they do not update model weights. Similar examples can show the expected format, disambiguate the task, and carry domain terminology or register more clearly than a broad instruction.

Document context can help maintain pronouns, tense, and terms across sentences. Prompts may request formality, dialect, register, or target terminology. The lecture also names post-editing, quality estimation, grammar correction, and explanation of a translation choice as related tasks that can be requested with different instructions.

Teaching example: use context and a terminology constraint

Teaching example. A document uses “bank” to mean a financial institution and must use one consistent target term.

  1. Give the target language, audience, and output format.
  2. Include the surrounding sentence that establishes the intended sense and provide the chosen glossary term.
  3. If using few-shot examples, include a similar sentence with its approved translation.
  4. Check names, numbers, negation, pronouns, tense, and the required term against the source.
  5. Reject or revise an output that changes the meaning or answers in the wrong language. Natural phrasing alone is not evidence of faithfulness.

The lecture frames several limitations: low-resource language coverage may be weak; output can drift into the wrong language; and fluent hallucinations can diverge from the source. Prompt sensitivity means that changing the instruction's wording can change the output. This differs from nondeterminism, where the same input produces different outputs across runs. Latency, cost, and reproducibility also matter in production.

For a low-resource language pair, a dedicated NMT system trained on the available parallel data may still outperform an LLM. Model size or fluency alone does not settle that comparison; evaluate both on the language pair and task. These are qualifications from the lecture-era discussion, not measurements of a particular current system.

Human judgment: adequacy and fluency are separate

There is often more than one acceptable translation, so a single reference is not a complete definition of correctness. Human evaluation separates two questions: adequacy asks how much source meaning is preserved; fluency asks whether the target is well formed without consulting the source. A fluent but off-target sentence may score well on fluency and poorly on adequacy.

The lecture treats human judgment as the gold standard for these dimensions, while noting that it takes time, costs money, and varies between annotators. Automatic metrics are cheaper and repeatable, yet score proxies for quality. Their output must be interpreted with the language pair, references, tokenization, and evaluation setup in view.

BLEU: modified precision, geometric mean, and brevity penalty

BLEU compares candidate n-grams with reference n-grams. Modified precision clips each candidate count to the maximum count for that n-gram in any one reference. For one reference, that maximum is simply its count. Clipping prevents repetition such as the the the the from earning unlimited unigram credit. The lecture calculates orders one through four and combines them with a geometric mean; without smoothing, a zero precision at any included order makes the product zero.

The exact lecture example uses reference “The way to make people trustworthy is to trust them” and candidate “To make people trustworthy, you need to trust them.” For the displayed counts, lowercase, split on whitespace, and strip the comma after trustworthy. This gives reference length r=10 and candidate length c=9.

BP={1,c≥rexp(1−r/c),c<r
BLEU=BP·exp(14∑n=14logpn)

BLEU at corpus level and with multiple references

For multiple references, clip each candidate n-gram to the largest count found in any one reference, not the sum of counts across references. Different n-grams may take their maximum from different references. At corpus level, add clipped match counts and candidate n-gram totals over sentences first, then calculate each precision; do not average sentence BLEU scores. The effective reference length adds the closest reference length for each candidate sentence.

These aggregation rules matter because sentence BLEU can be brittle: a short candidate with no four-gram match gets zero under the lecture's unsmoothed four-order convention. Do not silently add smoothing or drop higher orders; those choices define a different evaluation setup.

Beyond BLEU: useful signals with boundaries

BLEU uses word n-grams, so it can penalize a correct synonym or legitimate reordering. Word-form overlap is also fragile across morphologically rich languages. chrF compares character n-grams, which can capture shared subword forms and morphology more flexibly. COMET and BLEURT use learned evaluators rather than only string overlap and may correlate better with human judgment in the settings where they were evaluated.

No metric proves that a translation is faithful. Learned metrics depend on their training coverage; low-resource language pairs may be exactly where their estimates are least dependable. Compare metrics only with a stated benchmark setup and retain human adequacy and fluency checks.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the locally best next token as the globally best complete sentence.
  • Counting a partial prefix as a completed candidate before its EOS decision.
  • Assuming beam search is exact, or that a larger beam must improve BLEU.
  • Reading old language counts as current product availability or as guaranteed quality for a language pair.
  • Treating multilingual capacity pressure as inevitable degradation, or direct translation as universally superior to pivoting.
  • Calling few-shot prompting fine-tuning: examples condition a prompt without updating weights.
  • Equating fluent wording with preserved meaning.
  • Summing reference counts for BLEU clipping, averaging sentence BLEU to obtain corpus BLEU, or hiding a zero precision with unstated smoothing.

Exam focus

  • Explain autoregressive next-token conditioning and why a complete candidate includes an EOS choice.
  • Compare greedy pruning with width-k beam search; rank cumulative log scores and apply the lecture's α=0 and α=1 rules.
  • State why beam search is approximate and why BLEU can behave unexpectedly as beam width grows.
  • Describe the paper-era roles/counts of mBART, mT5, M2M-100, and NLLB-200, plus conditional transfer and capacity trade-offs.
  • Distinguish zero-shot instructions from few-shot in-context examples and identify context, terminology, register, and failure limits.
  • Separate adequacy from fluency.
  • Compute BLEU's clipped counts, each p₁…p₄, geometric mean, brevity penalty, and completed score; state the tokenization and smoothing convention.
  • Explain corpus aggregation, maximum-count clipping across references, and why chrF, COMET, and BLEURT remain imperfect proxies.

Practice questions

1. A prefix has cumulative probability 0.07. Can any extension with ordinary conditional probabilities exceed 0.07 under α=0?

Check your answer

No. Each future conditional probability is at most one, so multiplying by more conditional probabilities cannot increase the prefix probability. The bound does not directly apply to a different length-normalized ranking objective.

2. In the lecture beam sketch, which two displayed candidates have the highest scores if all labels are same-step cumulative log scores?

Check your answer

the house (−0.5) and the home (−2.1). This is only a numeric ranking of the shown labels; the sketch lacks the conditionals and terminal policy needed to reconstruct a full run.

3. What changes when α moves from 0 to 1 in the lecture score?

Check your answer

At α=0, cumulative log probability is unchanged. At α=1, divide the sum by token count to get the average log probability per token. The preference over lengths may change.

4. How can Sinhala → English → Tamil lose information even if each stage is fluent?

Check your answer

An English intermediate can omit a distinction that English grammar does not express, leaving the second stage unable to recover it. Errors from the first translation can also propagate into the second.

5. Do few-shot examples update model weights? What can they contribute?

Check your answer

No. They condition generation in the prompt. Similar examples can clarify the task and format, and provide domain terminology or register.

6. A translation reads naturally but changes who performed the action. Which human evaluation dimension detects the problem?

Check your answer

Adequacy: it checks preservation of source meaning. Fluency asks whether the target is well formed, which can remain high despite the meaning change.

7. A candidate four-gram appears twice in the candidate and once in the only reference. How many clipped matches count?

Check your answer

One. Modified precision clips the candidate multiplicity at the maximum reference count, which here is one.

8. Why should corpus BLEU not be computed by averaging sentence BLEU values?

Check your answer

Corpus BLEU first aggregates clipped match counts and candidate n-gram totals across sentences, then computes the precisions and geometric mean. Averaging sentence scores is a different calculation and is especially distorted by sentence-level zeros.

9. If an unsmoothed four-order BLEU precision is zero, what is the score under the lecture convention?

Check your answer

Zero, because the geometric mean includes that zero precision. Adding smoothing or changing effective order would be a different stated setup.
