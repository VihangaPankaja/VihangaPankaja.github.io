Supervised Learning: Evaluation, kNN, and Decision Trees

How labeled examples support prediction, how to evaluate fairly, and how nearest neighbors and decision trees turn examples into classifications.

Big picture

Supervised learning starts with examples that pair an input with its desired output. A learner uses those labeled examples to form a rule that can predict outputs for new cases. The goal is not merely to reproduce the training set: the learned rule should generalize to representative unseen data.

This page follows that path: frame the task as classification or regression, protect a fair evaluation, choose measures that match the class balance and cost of errors, then see two contrasting classifiers. k-nearest neighbors (kNN) predicts locally from stored examples. A decision tree builds explicit tests and class leaves.

From labeled examples to a useful hypothesis

An example is a pair (x,y): features describe the case, while y is its known target. In pure induction, examples are written (x,f(x)) and the learner returns a hypothesis h that approximates the unknown target function f. A hypothesis is consistent when it agrees with every supplied training example. Consistency alone does not promise that it will work on new cases: memorizing every training row can be consistent and still generalize poorly.

Task What the target represents Typical question
Classification A discrete class label Is this application approved? Is this email spam?
Regression A continuous quantity What response value should be predicted from these features?

For binary classification, one common notation maps a feature vector x∈ℝn to one of two labels {−1,+1}. The label is the target; positive and negative are names for the two classes. Regression instead estimates a dependent response from one or more predictors. A simple linear model is Y=a+bX1+ε; a multiple linear model is Y=a+bX1+cX2+dX3+ε.

Here a is the intercept, b,c,d are slopes, and ε is the error term. Linearity concerns the modeled response relationship; it does not mean that each observed residual must be zero.

The lecture’s regression-assumption slide needs a correction. An individual residual is not assumed to equal zero, and residuals do not all have one constant value. In classical regression, the error term is commonly modeled as having conditional mean zero and constant variance, with errors independent across observations; normality is an additional assumption used for some inference. Residuals are observed estimates of errors and should vary around zero. Whether predictors are treated as fixed or random depends on the model and sampling design. NIST regression diagnostics describes residuals as unexplained variation and lists mean zero, independence, normality, and constant variance as classical assumptions.

Supervised learning is one learning setting: examples have predefined labels. Unsupervised learning begins without labels and looks for structure such as clusters; semi-supervised learning has only some labels; reinforcement learning learns from rewards and penalties while acting. These are different sources of feedback, not different names for classification.

The lecture motivates classification with applications such as medical triage: measurements about a newly admitted patient support a high-risk/low-risk prediction, while the downstream decision has unequal costs. A classifier predicts a label; the application still has to decide what action to take with that prediction.

Worked example: majority-class baseline

A useful model must be compared with a simple baseline. In the lecture’s 15-loan example, the majority class is “Yes” and it occurs 9 times.

  1. Predict Yes for every case, without learning a feature rule.
  2. Count correct predictions: 9.
  3. Divide by all examples: 9/15=3/5=0.60=60%.
  4. Check: this is the score of a no-learning majority predictor. A learned model should be compared with it; the baseline alone does not show that useful structure was learned.

Generalization depends on representative examples

The lecture assumes training and test examples come from the same distribution, including future cases. Real data can violate this assumption, and a strong shift can hurt performance. The practical requirement is that training examples represent the cases where the model will be used. A memorizing hypothesis can fit every known label but has no reason to behave well outside those examples. Occam’s razor recommends preferring the simplest hypothesis that remains consistent with the evidence; simplicity is a guide to generalization, not a guarantee.

The learning workflow is: decide what examples are appropriate; collect inputs and target outputs; select a representation and learning method; fit the model; then evaluate it on separate data. Classification has two related stages: model construction learns a rule from labeled training examples, and model usage applies it to unknown cases. Do not infer predictions for the loan test rows from the lecture’s diagram: it shows the workflow, but does not give a fitted model or resulting test labels.

Evaluation: preserve an untouched final check

Evaluation asks how well a model performs and whether it is useful for its setting. Besides predictive accuracy, the lecture names model-building time, prediction time, robustness to noise and missing values, scalability, interpretability, and compactness (for example, tree size or rule count). The best measure depends on the task; a high score under one criterion may hide a weakness under another.

The evaluation/generalization prerequisite already develops a synthetic confusion-matrix calculation. Review that page for that separate example. This page retains the distinctive highly imbalanced counts from this lecture and concentrates on the evaluation protocols and source-specific conclusions.

Holdout, cross-validation, and the roles of validation and test

With a holdout method, divide the labeled dataset into disjoint training and test subsets. Fit only on training data, then use the unseen test labels to estimate performance. This is most practical when the dataset is large enough for both parts to be useful.

With n-fold cross-validation, divide the data into n disjoint, roughly equal folds. Run n rounds: each fold is the test fold once, and the other n−1 folds train the model. Average the n scores. The lecture names 5-fold and 10-fold as common choices and recommends cross-validation when the available dataset is not large. Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) is the special case with one test example per round: for m examples, it is m-fold validation.

A validation set is used during development to choose settings such as hyperparameters. A test set is reserved for a final estimate after those choices are made. Repeatedly checking the test score and changing the model turns the test set into another validation set, so it no longer gives an untouched final check.

Worked example: 5-fold evaluation and a final test set

Suppose a development dataset has 100 labeled cases and you choose 5-fold cross-validation to compare values of k for kNN.

  1. Partition the development data into five disjoint folds of 20 cases.
  2. Round 1 trains on 80 cases and evaluates on fold 1; rounds 2–5 rotate the held-out fold.
  3. Average the five validation scores for each candidate k; select the value with the best average under the chosen criterion.
  4. After selecting the model and settings, evaluate once on a separate test set that was not used to choose k.
  5. Check the boundary: cross-validation supports model selection here; the separate test score is the final report. If there is no separate test set, do not describe the cross-validation score as an untouched test result.

Classification measures and class imbalance

The binary confusion matrix has actual classes in rows and predicted classes in columns. First decide which class is positive. Then identify each count relative to that choice.

Accuracy is the fraction of all cases classified correctly. Precision asks what fraction of predicted positives are truly positive. Recall asks what fraction of actual positives were found. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, so it remains low when either one is low.

The lecture explicitly prints precision and recall; accuracy and F1 above are arithmetic consequences of its confusion counts.

The lecture also gives a separate 1%-positive intrusion thought experiment: predicting every case as negative yields 99% accuracy and detects no positives. Keep it distinct from the confusion matrix above, whose counts contain 100 actual positives and 1,000 actual negatives (9.09% positives). Together, the examples show why accuracy can hide poor minority-class detection. Choose a measure that reflects the class of interest and the consequences of false positives and false negatives. The positive class is the class being sought; “positive” does not inherently mean a favorable outcome.

k-nearest neighbors: predict from nearby examples

kNN is an instance-based or “lazy” learner: it keeps the training examples instead of first constructing an explicit global model. For a new case, it measures distance to the stored cases, selects the k nearest, and predicts the most frequent class among those neighbors. Equivalently, the fraction of neighbors in class cj estimates Pr(cj∣d) for the query d.

The distance function must suit the application. Features on very different numeric scales can dominate a distance calculation, so decide on a scaling rule using training data and apply it consistently. Select k empirically with validation data or cross-validation. A small k is sensitive to individual examples; a large k smooths local differences and may wash out a small neighborhood. The source does not prescribe a tie rule: define one for an implementation, or choose a setting and data where the vote is unambiguous.

Prediction requires comparing a query with the stored training examples. With N examples and d features, direct distance calculation costs O(Nd) per query; selecting the nearest cases adds work (sorting all distances costs O(NlogN)). The lecture summarizes prediction as linear in training-set size, treating feature count and neighbor selection as fixed/implementation details. This can be slow for large training sets, and the method does not produce an easily interpreted global model. Its local votes can create flexible, complex decision boundaries.

Worked example: source k = 6 figure, kept qualitative

The source figure marks a new query and labels the neighborhood “k = 6.” Its legend has Government (green), Science (red), and Arts (black). The oval is a visual neighborhood cue, but the slide supplies no coordinates, distance formula, or list of six neighbors. Therefore the source does not support exact distance arithmetic or a reproducible class count from the drawing alone. The correct source-grounded procedure is: calculate distances using the chosen application metric, rank the stored examples, retain the six smallest distances, then count their labels. To predict the query’s Science probability, divide the number of Science cases among those six by 6. Do not assign a numerical probability from this picture.

Decision trees: explicit tests and class leaves

A decision tree represents a classifier as attribute tests. Each internal node asks a question; an outgoing branch corresponds to a possible value; a leaf predicts a class. A case follows one path from root to leaf. Trees can express any function of their input attributes, but a tree that memorizes training labels may not generalize. Prefer compact trees when they explain the training data adequately.

The lecture lists easy construction, fast classification, interpretability for small trees, useful accuracy on many simple datasets, no distributional assumption (nonparametric behavior), and built-in feature selection as advantages. It also warns about expensive training, over-complex trees that overfit, and limited expressivity. The restaurant example describes 12 “Wait” rows using attributes including Patrons, Type, Price, and others. Its class counts show why a good test creates purer child groups. Keep it separate from the seven-row buys_computer example below: they are distinct source datasets.

Greedy recursive induction and stopping cases

The lecture’s top-down procedure is greedy: choose a locally useful attribute at the current node, partition the examples by its values, and recurse. It is divide and conquer because each branch solves a smaller subproblem. The selected attribute is not reconsidered by global search; the split heuristic (such as information gain) affects the resulting tree.

At each call DTL(examples, attributes, default):

  1. If the subset is empty, return the default class supplied by the parent, commonly the parent subset’s majority class.
  2. If all examples have the same class, return that pure class leaf.
  3. If no attributes remain, return the majority class of the current examples.
  4. Otherwise choose the best attribute, make a test node, partition examples for each value, and recursively build each child with the remaining attributes and the current subset’s majority class as its default.

These stopping cases prevent pointless splits. The empty-child case is distinct from a nonempty but mixed subset with no attributes left: the former inherits a parent default; the latter uses its own majority. When majority counts tie, the lecture does not specify a tie rule, so make the policy explicit rather than quietly assuming one.

Node impurity and information gain

An attribute is useful when its branches make the class labels more homogeneous. The lecture names entropy, Gini index, and misclassification error as node-impurity measures and says to seek low impurity; its ID3/C4.5 criterion selects the attribute with the highest information gain. Entropy is measured in bits here. When a class probability is zero, use the convention 0log20=0 so a pure child has zero entropy.

For class proportions pi, entropy is −∑ipilog2pi. Supplemental formalizations of the other named node-impurity measures are Gini impurity, 1−∑ipi2, and misclassification error, 1−maxipi, the fraction that would be wrong if the node predicted its most frequent class. Unlike the entropy split arithmetic below, the lecture does not work numerical Gini or classification-error splits. For Gini or classification error, compare the weighted post-split impurities and choose the lower value; for entropy, information gain measures the reduction and the larger value is preferred. Do not confuse weighted entropy with weighted classification error: they are different criteria.

I(D)=−∑i=1mpilog2(pi),0log20:=0
Gini(D)=1−∑ipi2,Err(D)=1−maxipi
InfoA(D)=∑j=1v|Dj||D|I(Dj),Gain(A)=I(D)−InfoA(D)

Common mistakes

  • Calling a hypothesis useful because it is consistent with the training rows; generalization is about unseen cases.
  • Treating a test set as a tuning set. Use validation or cross-validation for choices, then reserve the test for a final check.
  • Reporting accuracy alone on an imbalanced dataset; inspect positive-class precision and recall as well.
  • Reversing the confusion-matrix axes or using the wrong denominator: precision uses predicted positives, recall uses actual positives.
  • Treating kNN’s source plot as if it gave exact coordinates or a specified distance function; it does not.
  • Comparing raw feature distances when units differ, or forgetting to choose how ties are handled.
  • Confusing the two tree datasets: restaurant has 12 rows; buys_computer has 7.
  • Treating tree induction as globally optimal. The source procedure greedily chooses a split at each node.
  • Returning a majority of an empty child. The empty case uses the inherited default; majority voting applies to a nonempty subset.
  • Copying the printed Patrons gain 0.0541 without checking its inputs; the shown counts yield about 0.541 bits.
  • Using information gain, Gini impurity, and misclassification error as interchangeable names. They are distinct split criteria.

Practice questions

1. A test result has TP = 8, FP = 2, FN = 12, TN = 78. Find precision, recall, accuracy, and F1 for the stated positive class.

Check your answer

Precision = 8/(8+2)=0.8. Recall = 8/(8+12)=0.4. Accuracy = (8+78)/100=0.86. F1 = 2(0.8)(0.4)/(0.8+0.4)=0.5333…. Check the denominators: predicted positives are 10; actual positives are 20; total cases are 100.

2. A dataset has 30 cases: 18 positive and 12 negative. What is the accuracy of the majority-class baseline?

Check your answer

Predict positive for all 30. Correct cases = 18, so accuracy = 18/30=0.60=60%. This is a baseline, not evidence of learned feature relationships.

3. In a dataset of 240 examples, how many model fits and test-fold evaluations are made by 10-fold cross-validation?

Check your answer

There are 10 rounds, so 10 model fits and 10 test-fold evaluations. Each round trains on 9 folds (216 examples) and evaluates on the remaining fold (24 examples).

4. A classifier labels 40 cases positive; 30 are truly positive. Of 50 actual positives, it detects those 30. Find precision and recall.

Check your answer

Precision = 30/40=0.75. Recall = 30/50=0.60. The precision denominator is the 40 predicted positives; recall’s denominator is the 50 actual positives.

5. A parent node has 8 yes and 2 no examples. One branch receives all 8 yes examples; the other receives both no examples. What are the two child entropies and the weighted child entropy?

Check your answer

Both children are pure, so each child entropy is 0 bits (using 0log20=0). Their weighted sum is (8/10)(0)+(2/10)(0)=0 bits. The split has removed all parent label uncertainty.

6. Why does the source’s seven-row tree predict RID 3 incorrectly, and what happens to RIDs 6 and 7?

Check your answer

RID 3 is in the youth/student=yes leaf, where two of three training labels are yes; majority voting predicts yes although RID 3 is labeled no. Senior RID 6 (fair) follows the no branch and RID 7 (excellent) follows the yes branch, matching the original rows and tree branches.

7. A decision-tree call receives a nonempty subset containing both classes but has no remaining attributes. What should it return, and how is this different from an empty subset?

Check your answer

With no attributes left, return the current subset’s majority class. An empty subset has no local class counts, so it returns the default class inherited from its parent.
