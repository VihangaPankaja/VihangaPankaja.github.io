Amortized analysis

Bound total cost across a sequence, using earlier cheap operations to pay for later expensive ones without assuming random inputs.

Big picture

Three questions are easy to mix up. Actual cost is the work this operation really does. Amortized cost is the amount we charge it in a proof; a cheap operation may be charged extra so a later expensive one is already paid for. Expected cost is an average over a probability distribution or randomized choices. Amortized analysis needs no probability and does not promise that every operation is fast.

For an operation that changes state D to D′, choose a potential Φ that measures saved credit in the state. Then charge actual work plus the change in potential:

c^i=ci+ΔΦi=ci+Φ(Di)−Φ(Di−1)

Adding the charges makes the intermediate potentials cancel (the telescoping sum):

∑i=1mci=∑i=1mc^i+Φ(D0)−Φ(Dm)

If the initial potential is zero and every reachable final potential is nonnegative, total actual work is at most the total charge. More generally, it is enough that Φ(Dm)≥Φ(D0) . This is why the starting state and the end of the sequence matter: an expensive initialization must be charged or explicitly excluded, and resetting to an empty state is another operation with a cost and a potential change. A claim fails if it silently spends more credit than the state contains, chooses a potential that can end below its initial value, or omits an expensive reset.

Three accounts for the same four increments

We will account for the exact same sequence three ways. A four-bit binary counter starts at 0000 and increments to 0100. One unit of actual cost is one bit flip. For this source-supported example, each increment is charged 2. “Credit” and “potential” below are two descriptions of the same saved units: one credit sits on each 1 bit, so Φ=number of 1 bits.

1. Aggregate: count the work in the whole sequence

The counter visits 0000 → 0001 → 0010 → 0011 → 0100. The bit flips per increment are 1, 2, 1, and 3. The aggregate method first bounds the total, then gives each operation an equal share of that bound.

For m increments from zero, bit j flips at most ⌊m/2ʲ⌋ times, so the total is less than m+m/2+m/4+…<2m. The aggregate method proves the total bound first; 2 is then a convenient amortized charge per increment. It does not claim each increment costs at most 2.

For a single increment with q trailing 1s, q bits turn off and one turns on. The potential counts 1s, so:

ci=1+q,ΔΦi=1−q,c^i=(1+q)+(1−q)=2

2. Accounting: leave coins on bits

Now interpret the charge as two coins collected for each increment. Pay for actual flips; keep one coin on every bit that is 1. A 1 bit will eventually need one flip to become 0, so its coin is reserved for that future flip.

3. Potential: calculate the same balance from the state

The potential method does not track physical coins one by one. It uses the number of 1 bits as a mathematical balance, Φ = number of 1s. For the same four transitions, actual costs stay 1, 2, 1, 3, and each charge is actual cost plus the change in Φ.

c^i=ci+(Φafter−Φbefore)

All three accounts describe one fact: the sequence costs 7 flips, so charging 2 per increment covers its total of 8 units. Aggregate counts the flips; accounting assigns saved coins; potential computes their balance from the counter. The accounting/potential rows match exactly here because Φ was chosen to equal the number of saved coins.

Expression stack: why one closing symbol is not quadratic work

Lecture 5 rewrites a = x+((a+b)*c+d)+y; into z1 = a+b;, z2 = z1*c+d;, then a = x+z2+y;. Ordinary symbols are pushed. A ) pops through the matching (, emits a temporary assignment, and pushes the temporary name. A ; empties the remaining stack. One ) may pop many symbols, so charging each call only its actual cost misses the sequence pattern: each symbol is pushed once and popped at most once.

The lecture's unit cost is one stack push or pop. Its example has 18 input symbols, counting the assignment target and equals sign as separate symbols. Below, consecutive ordinary symbols are grouped only where each still costs exactly one push. The stack is shown bottom-to-top.

The two closing symbols cost 5 and 7 actual units, but each is charged only 2 because it removes symbols whose earlier pushes increased the potential. Across all 18 calls, actual work is 34 stack operations and charge is 36, matching the lecture's terminal potential of 2.

Keep the terminal convention straight. The lecture explicitly sets Φ(n)=2 on the final ; call so that even this call has amortized cost 2. It is a bookkeeping exception; after the semicolon, the physical stack is empty. Under the natural potential “stack size after every operation,” the last call instead has Φ: 7→0, charge 7−7=0, and the sequence total is still 34. Both conventions prove the same total; never use the lecture's terminal value 2 in the natural-potential calculation (or vice versa).

Lazy heaps: what the course slides establish

Lecture 6's binomial heap adds a new one-node tree on insert, leaving the root list uncombined. A later remove-min finds and removes the minimum root, puts its children back on the root list, then combines equal-degree trees. The basic version scans all roots. The enhanced version uses a degree table, with cost O(MaxDegree+s), where s is the number of roots examined; MaxDegree is O(logn). The lecture's summary table gives binomial insert O(1) actual/amortized and remove-min O(n) actual, O(logn) amortized. Lecture 7 gives Fibonacci-heap remove-min O(n) actual and O(logn) amortized, and decrease-key O(n) actual and O(1) amortized.

The course example makes the deferred work visible: after removing the minimum and promoting its children, seven roots await consolidation. Equal-degree pairs are then linked, leaving fewer roots. Each insert is cheap partly because it leaves consolidation for later. That explains the amortized idea, but the slides do not derive the heap's potential function or show a full numeric credit ledger; do not read the table as a worst-case latency promise for one remove-min. The following smaller example is supplemental and uses seven singleton roots so the saved-work calculation is easy to check. Its exact action count charges one unit per initial-root inspection, exposed child and link; degree-table setup/reset adds the separate logarithmic term in the proof below.

Supplemental binomial-heap potential proof (not shown in the lectures). To make the earlier-work story precise, use Φ=2R for a simplified model, where R is the number of roots. Each O(1) insert adds one root and therefore only O(1) amortized cost. Suppose remove-min starts with s roots, removes a root of degree d, exposes its d children, links L equal-degree pairs, and ends with R′ roots. Then:

R′=s−1+d−L,c=s+d+L+O(logn)
c^=c+ΔΦ=s+d+L+2(d−1−L)+O(logn)=2d+R′−1+O(logn)=O(logn)

After consolidation, both d and R′ are O(logn); the potential decrease pays for the many root inspections and links. This is an illustrative proof under the stated action-count model, not a cost derivation presented by the course slides. A proof must include initialization or start from the empty heap. For Fibonacci heaps, a standard textbook choice is Φ=t+2m, where t is the number of roots and m the number of marked nonroot nodes. The lecture introduces the child-cut mark and reports amortized bounds, but does not teach this formula; treat it as supplemental context.

Common mistakes

An amortized bound is not an average over inputs and does not cap each operation. “This four-bit increment costs at most four” proves nothing about counters of arbitrary width. The chosen potential must cover the operations actually allowed, including initialization and reset. Keep the cost model fixed: the counter example counts bit flips; the stack example counts pushes and pops; the heap slide's asymptotic table should not be turned into invented exact costs.

Exam focus

For a potential proof: state the initial state and Φ; compute each operation's actual cost and ΔΦ; simplify the amortized charge; sum and telescope; verify Φ(Dₘ)≥Φ(D₀). For the stack, explain that each symbol is pushed at most once and popped at most once, even if a close triggers many pops. For a counter with q trailing 1s, actual flips are q+1 and the number of 1s changes by 1−q, so the amortized charge is (q+1)+(1−q)=2. For lazy heaps, separate actual work on a particular remove-min from the amortized guarantee across operations.

Practice questions

A counter has state `0111` before increment. Under the bit-flip cost model and Φ = number of 1s, find actual cost, ΔΦ, and amortized cost.

Check your answer

The three trailing 1s turn to 0 and the next bit turns to 1, so actual cost is 4. Potential changes from 3 to 1, so ΔΦ=−2. Amortized cost is 4−2=2.

A sequence has total charge 50, initial potential 3, and final potential 8. What upper bound does this give on actual cost?

Check your answer

By telescoping, actual cost = total charge + initial potential − final potential = 50+3−8=45.

Why can the lecture charge the final semicolon of its stack example 2 even though its natural stack-size potential would charge 0?

Check your answer

The lecture defines the terminal potential as 2, not the physical final stack size 0. From stack size 7, its ΔΦ is 2−7=−5; actual cost 7 plus ΔΦ gives charge 2. With natural potential ending at zero, ΔΦ=−7 and the final charge is 0. Either convention works only when used consistently.

The lecture reports O(n) actual and O(logn) amortized binomial remove-min. Does that mean every remove-min scans only O(logn) roots?

Check your answer

No. One remove-min may scan O(n) roots. The amortized bound concerns a sequence of operations; it is not a per-operation worst-case latency guarantee.
