LAN, Ethernet and switching

From shared-medium access rules to Ethernet frames, switch learning, VLAN tags, and the two MAC-address labs.

Big picture

A local area network joins nearby devices over a shared or switched link. Before switches gave each Ethernet device a point-to-point path, stations had to coordinate who could use a shared medium. This page follows that problem from scheduled access and token passing through contention-based Ethernet, then shows how a switch learns where MAC addresses live and how VLAN tags carry logical separation across switches.

The older access methods explain the ideas behind MAC. The practical focus is Ethernet frames, collision/backoff behavior on a shared half-duplex segment, and the source-learning and destination-lookup decisions used by the two-switch labs.

LAN scope and IEEE 802 context

A LAN covers a limited local area, from a few metres to several hundred metres in the lecture's examples. The two Lecture 4 sources describe different technology generations: the PDF gives a historical range of 1–100 Mb/s and the PowerPoint gives 100 Mb/s–1 Gb/s. Treat those as period-specific snapshots, not a disagreement about one fixed LAN speed. The PDF contrasts a typical LAN error ratio of about 1 in 10⁸ with WAN examples around 1 in 10³ to 1 in 10⁵; these are also historical lecture figures.

The lecture distinguishes two signaling approaches. A baseband LAN carries digital signaling over the medium; stations share it through an access rule when the link is shared. A broadband LAN uses analog signaling and modems, with frequency-division multiplexing (FDM) separating subchannels. On shared IEEE 802 links, MAC (Media Access Control) frames and addresses medium use; LLC (Logical Link Control) provides the logical-link interface above it. The OSI layer map belongs in the OSI notes; the one relevant distinction here is that MAC procedures decide when a station may send.

The PDF describes IEEE 802 as splitting the data-link functions into LLC and MAC; it identifies LLC as a subset of HDLC and associates MAC with the 802.3/4/5 families. The second Lecture 4 source names Token Ring, Token Bus, and Ethernet as CSMA/CD examples. These labels are useful historical context; the following sections explain their access rules once, then follow Ethernet into its switched form.

Three ways to decide who sends

Medium-access methods solve the same question in different ways: who gets to transmit now? The slides group methods into primary/secondary control and peer-to-peer access. Polling and TDMA assign turns; ALOHA and CSMA let stations contend; token methods circulate permission. The table preserves the source families without treating ARQ reliability methods as access protocols.

Family Rule for taking a turn What the source emphasizes
Poll/select A primary asks a secondary whether it has data or selects a secondary to receive. The primary controls when secondaries may send or receive.
TDMA A reference station announces which station owns each time slot. Satellites; secondaries indicate demand to the reference station.
TDM Each station has a pre-assigned slot and transmits only in that slot. The simplest peer-to-peer schedule in the slide.
ALOHA A station transmits when it has data; overlapping frames can collide. Arbitrary start times make collision risk rise under heavy traffic.
CSMA A station listens and transmits when the channel sounds idle. Near-simultaneous starts can still collide because signals take time to propagate.
CSMA/CD A wired station listens while sending, stops on collision, then retries after a random wait. Historical shared-medium Ethernet; the LAN slides supply the jam and backoff details below.
Token passing Only the station holding a free token may send. Token Ring and Token Bus provide turns without Ethernet collision contention.

Polling and ARQ are separate dimensions even though the lecture's slide 29 lists “stop and wait” and “continuous ARQ” under polling. Polling chooses who may access the medium; ARQ handles loss or corruption after a frame is sent. A system can combine a poll schedule with an ARQ recovery method. The data-link guide covers ARQ itself.

Poll/select, TDMA and TDM

In the lecture's poll/select dialogue, a primary polls with “Do you have data for me?” or selects a secondary with “I have data for you and I am selecting you. Can you receive?” The key is centralized turn control, not a numeric efficiency calculation; none is supplied.

For satellite TDMA, one site is the master/reference station. Secondary stations indicate that they need the channel; the reference station periodically announces which station may use each time slot. The separate TDM slide describes pre-assigned slots: a station sends only in its own slot. Keep those source descriptions distinct: TDMA's slide includes a reference station and requests, whereas the TDM slide only states fixed slots.

ALOHA and carrier sensing

Pure ALOHA allows transmission at arbitrary times. If two frames overlap, the lecture says stations detect the collision and retransmit; as traffic grows, overlaps become more likely and performance degrades. Its A–E timeline shows arbitrary frame placement and overlaps only; it has no measured outcomes or time values. On radio links, senders generally cannot detect a collision while transmitting; successful reception is commonly inferred from an acknowledgement. That wireless qualification is supplemental and is explained below.

CSMA improves on ALOHA by listening first. A station transmits only if it senses the channel free, but two stations can both sense idle before either signal reaches the other. They may therefore start nearly together and collide. CSMA/CD adds collision detection while transmitting: abort the damaged attempt and back off. The source's conceptual timeline marks three states—contention, transmission, and idle—without assigning numeric slot lengths.

The Pure ALOHA slide also has five separate user lanes (A–E) with frames placed at arbitrary times. The exact horizontal positions are illustrative only; the slide does not mark which frame succeeded or provide throughput data. Its teaching point is that there is no common start slot, so frame overlap is possible. CSMA/CD's source timeline above differs because stations first sense the medium and detect collisions while sending.

Worked conceptual trace: why carrier sensing can still collide

Two stations both observe idle before either signal arrives

Given: shared wired medium; two stations have frames ready; propagation delay means each may sense the other only after it starts. This is a conceptual trace, not a timed source example.

1. The shared channel is idle

Stations A and B are separated along a shared cable. Neither has yet received a signal from the other.

2. Both sense before propagation completes

A and B each observe an idle channel and begin transmitting within the propagation delay between them. Carrier sensing is local evidence, not a simultaneous global reservation.

3. Their signals meet

The transmissions overlap on the medium. CSMA alone cannot prevent this collision because both decisions were made from an idle observation.

4. CSMA/CD reacts

On a shared wired half-duplex Ethernet segment, each sender detects the collision while transmitting, sends the collision-enforcement jam, aborts the frame, and waits a random backoff before retrying.

Collision-avoidance scheduling and two RTS/CTS contexts

The lecture also names “carrier-sense systems (collision free)” and describes two broad ideas: a timer or arbiter chooses a safe time, or stations use pre-determined timing thresholds that may reflect priority. No specific protocol, synchronization rule, or failure behavior is shown, so treat these as scheduling goals rather than a guarantee that every real deployment has zero collisions.

The slide 31 RTS/CTS sketch is a ready/clear handshake: one side sends RTS, the other replies CTS, data follows, and a later CTS-Off / interrupted data line indicates that the receiver is no longer ready. The slide compares this to RS-232-C control signaling. That example is about gating a data exchange; it does not show the wireless medium reservation procedure.

Wireless distinction: IEEE 802.11 WLAN uses CSMA/CA rather than wired Ethernet's CSMA/CD. Its medium-access behavior differs from the serial-style RTS/CTS control signals shown here; see RFC 8325 §6.1.

Token access: a turn circulates instead of competing

In Token Ring, a logical ring carries a free token while no station owns the turn. A station with data waits for the free token, removes it, and sends a busy token plus its frame. Each station repeats the circulating traffic; the destination copies its data. When the busy token and data return to the sender, the sender removes them and releases a free token. The recovered speaker notes emphasize that the basic scheme has one token; a slotted-ring scheme is the later multi-token exception mentioned in the notes. If the sender wants to send again, the next node gets first chance, so it waits for another turn.

The original Token Ring slides identify it as IBM-promoted and IEEE 802.5, with historical 4→16 Mb/s performance and priority-based access. The lecture's compact frame view has an 8-bit start delimiter, 8-bit access-control field, and 8-bit end delimiter for a 3-byte token; the data/command form adds frame control, 6-byte destination and source addresses, payload, CRC, and frame status. That structure is context for the token procedure, not another Ethernet frame format.

The PowerPoint shows a star-like physical layout through a Token Ring MAU, while frames follow a logical ring. Token Bus uses a physical coaxial bus but a logical/virtual ring: stations pass permission in a sequence even though transmissions travel on the bus. The LAN PDF says the sequence can be based on numeric address; after passing the token to a successor, a station watches for evidence such as that successor's next transmission, and may choose a new successor if needed. The separate slide 46 only shows the bus/virtual-ring distinction, so that successor detail is a lecture-PDF supplement.

Token access is orderly under a functioning ring: the lecture says every station gets a chance within a bounded time and that token methods can be fairer and more efficient than CSMA/CD under heavy load. Waiting for the token and maintaining the ring add delay and complexity. Some rings use a special monitor node to restore a lost token; others share recovery responsibility and must avoid creating multiple replacement tokens. Centralized maintenance creates a single point of failure, while distributed maintenance adds coordination and complexity.

Lecture sequence: a free token becomes a busy frame and returns

Follow the six-station Token Ring lifecycle

Given: stations 1–6 in logical order, one free token, and station 2 has data for station 5. Receiver copying and the lifecycle follow the Lecture 4 slides 42–43 and recovered speaker notes; the sender/receiver labels illustrate one path.

1. Free token circulates

Stations 1→2→3→4→5→6→1 pass the single free token. A waiting station may take the turn only when the free token reaches it.

2. Station 2 captures the turn

Station 2 removes the free token and sends a busy token with its message toward station 3. No other station can take that same token while the frame is circulating.

3. Intermediate stations repeat; destination copies

Stations 3, 4, 5, and 6 forward the circulating frame. If station 5 is the destination in this teaching example, it copies the data and continues forwarding the frame around the ring.

4. Sender removes returned data and busy token

The busy frame returns through station 1 to station 2. The sender removes the frame and its busy token, rather than leaving a duplicate circulating indefinitely.

5. Sender releases a free token

Station 2 transmits a new free token. The following station in ring order gets the next chance; station 2 cannot immediately capture the same turn ahead of others.

Wireless LAN signpost

The last slide of the LAN PDF lists Wireless LANs (WLANs), IEEE 802.11a and 802.11b, as related topics; it supplies no WLAN procedure. Those are historical labels in this lecture, not a current standards inventory. Wireless uses collision avoidance (CSMA/CA), while old shared-cable Ethernet used collision detection (CSMA/CD).

Ethernet frames and medium access

Ethernet's frame fields carry destination and source MAC addresses, a Type/Length value, payload/padding, and the frame check sequence. The preamble and start-frame delimiter (SFD) help synchronize and identify the frame start. The sizes below preserve Lecture 4's frame illustration. The maximum basic untagged frame is 1518 bytes, excluding the 8-byte preamble/SFD.

The lecture describes the preamble as 56 alternating bits and the SFD as 8 bits; the SFD ends in 10101011. A frame has 6-byte destination and source addresses, a 2-byte Type/Length field, 46–1500 bytes of data/padding, and a 4-byte FCS. If the payload is shorter than 46 bytes, padding makes the data/pad field reach its minimum. The FCS carries a CRC check; error detection and recovery are covered in the data-link notes.

The source slide's Type/Length cutoff is incorrect: it says “1518 or less” means length, but 1518 is the total frame-size limit. In Ethernet's conventional field interpretation, values up to 1500 indicate payload length; EtherType values start at 1536 (0x0600), leaving 1501–1535 as a reserved gap. This correction follows RFC 7042 and the IEEE maintenance contribution on the Type/Length field.

Carrier sense, collisions and retry

On a shared half-duplex segment, a station listens while the carrier is present. When the channel becomes idle, it waits one interframe gap (IFG) and transmits. The lecture sets IFG to 96 bit times: at 10 Mb/s, 96 bits take 9.6 μs; at 100 Mb/s, 960 ns. If two stations begin close enough together that both sensed idle, they detect the collision while transmitting. Each sends a 32-bit collision-enforcement (jam) signal—even if the collision was detected during the preamble—then stops the failed frame and waits a random number of slot times.

For 10/100 Mb/s Ethernet, a slot is 512 bit times. If 512 bits go out without collision, the lecture calls the channel acquired. Its propagation examples say this slot allowed about 2800 m on coax at 10 Mb/s and about 205 m at 100 Mb/s; twisted-pair segment length is stated as 100 m for other physical reasons. The lecture reports a maximum of 16 retries before reporting failure upward; treat that as the source's historical rule, not a universal current Ethernet claim.

As a timing check, 512 bits/10 Mb/s=51.2 μs and 512 bits/100 Mb/s=5.12 μs. At the faster rate, the same 512-bit collision window is much shorter, which is why the historical Gigabit half-duplex case needed a longer slot.

Worked source-rule example: choose an Ethernet backoff delay

Calculate a hypothetical backoff draw at 10 and 100 Mb/s

Lecture rule: k=min(n,10); choose integer 0≤r<2k; at 10/100 Mb/s wait r×512 bit times. For this worked example, n = 3 and the hypothetical random draw is r = 5.

1. Set the attempt-count convention

Use the lecture’s variable n as the attempt number supplied to its formula. This teaching example chooses n = 3; the slide does not specify whether to count the initial attempt or collisions only, so do not translate this into a retry count.

2. Compute the exponent cap

k=min(n,10)=min(3,10)=3. The random integer must satisfy 0≤r<23, so the possible draws are 0 through 7.

3. Apply the hypothetical draw

Suppose the random choice is r = 5. The wait is r×512=5×512=2560 bit times. The draw is illustrative, not a measured or fixed outcome.

4. Convert bit times to seconds

At 10 Mb/s, one bit time is 100 ns: 2560 × 100 ns = 256,000 ns = 256 μs. At 100 Mb/s, one bit time is 10 ns: 2560 × 10 ns = 25,600 ns = 25.6 μs.

5. Check the cap case

When k = 10, there are 2¹⁰ = 1024 possible integer draws: r = 0…1023. This checks the strict upper bound; 1024 itself is not a valid draw.

After a successful transmission the lecture resets the backoff counter. If collisions continue, the selection range grows up to the cap; choosing independent random waits makes repeat collisions less likely than if every station retried at the same instant.

Why half-duplex Gigabit Ethernet extended the slot

At 1 Gb/s, a 512-bit slot would correspond to only about a 20 m collision diameter under the lecture's timing assumptions. The historical half-duplex solution extended the slot to 4096 bits (512 bytes) with carrier extension, filling short-frame transmission time so collision detection still had the required window. Short frames then carried considerable extension overhead.

Frame bursting lets a station contend normally for a first frame; if it succeeds, it can send additional frames in a burst. Extension symbols fill the inter-frame gaps so another station does not seize the medium between the burst frames. The lecture gives a 65,536-bit burst limit and claims efficiency above 90% for small data frames; these are source-era figures, not a promise for every link.

Full duplex removes shared-medium contention

Full-duplex Ethernet is point-to-point: transmit and receive use independent paths at the same time. With only two endpoints on the link, there is no shared-medium contention, collision detection, or collision backoff. The IFG still applies between frames. A 100 Mb/s full-duplex link can carry up to 100 Mb/s in each direction simultaneously; the old slide's “200 Mb/s” is the sum of both directions, not a 200 Mb/s one-way rate. Thus collision/backoff discussion applies to a shared half-duplex link, not ordinary full-duplex switched Ethernet.

Switch learning and forwarding

A switch builds a table by reading each arriving frame's source MAC and associating it with the local ingress port and VLAN. It then looks up the destination MAC in that VLAN. The two lookups answer different questions: where did this source arrive, and where should this destination go? The switch buffers and forwards the frame; the lecture slide's “connection” means a temporary forwarding action, not a persistent circuit.

Destination lookup in the ingress VLAN Forwarding decision
Known unicast on another port Send the frame only through the mapped port.
Known unicast mapped to the ingress port Filter it; sending it back through the same port is unnecessary.
Unknown unicast Flood copies through eligible forwarding ports in the same VLAN, except the ingress port. The destination field remains the original unicast MAC.
Broadcast (FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF) Flood within the VLAN, except the ingress port.

Unknown-unicast flooding is not the same as changing the destination to broadcast. The lab worksheet calls an unknown destination “broadcasted”; the precise term is unknown-unicast flooding. Cisco's switching explanation confirms that a frame with no destination entry is sent to forwarding ports in the VLAN except the ingress port (Cisco, Unicast Flooding in Switched Campus Networks). Broadcast frames also flood, but their destination MAC is already all ones.

Teaching trace: an ARP request and reply teach both switches

Follow source learning and destination lookup across Lab 1.2 topology

Topology: PC-A—S1 F0/6—S1 F0/1—S2 F0/1—S2 F0/18—PC-B. Addresses: PC-A 192.168.1.3/24, PC-B 192.168.1.2/24, S1 VLAN 1 .11/24, S2 VLAN 1 .12/24; no default gateways. The trace uses symbolic MACs and assumes an empty dynamic table only to teach the transition.

1. Start with a declared illustrative state

Assume the dynamic entries are empty and call PC-A’s MAC A and PC-B’s MAC B. This is a teaching assumption, not a claim about the lab’s observed startup table. PC-B sends an ARP request with source B and broadcast destination.

2. S2 learns B locally and floods the broadcast

The request enters S2 on F0/18. S2 records B → F0/18. Because the destination is broadcast, S2 floods within the VLAN except F0/18, including the inter-switch port F0/1.

3. S1 learns B behind its uplink

The same request enters S1 on F0/1. S1 records B → F0/1. It floods the broadcast to other eligible VLAN 1 ports, including PC-A on F0/6.

4. PC-A replies; S1 learns A on its access port

PC-A’s reply has source A and destination B. It enters S1 on F0/6, so S1 records A → F0/6. S1 already knows B → F0/1, so it forwards the unicast reply only to F0/1.

5. S2 learns A behind its uplink and forwards to B

The reply enters S2 on F0/1, so S2 records A → F0/1. S2 already knows B → F0/18 and sends the reply only to PC-B through F0/18. The tables now contain local source-to-ingress-port mappings at both switches.

The two-switch topology is the actual Lab 1.2 layout. A MAC table entry identifies the port from which the frame arrived, not necessarily the endpoint's own physical location: multiple downstream hosts can be learned behind one switch-to-switch uplink. The lab asks students to record actual table entries rather than assume the table is empty. Clearing dynamic entries on S2 does not clear a PC's ARP cache, and background traffic can relearn a MAC before the next command is entered.

VLANs and the 802.1Q tag

A VLAN groups switch ports and their frames into separate logical LANs. The lecture describes each group as acting like an independent hub and notes that filtering rules can restrict traffic between VLANs. A VLAN can span multiple switches when frames carry a tag, so the physical switch topology and the logical LAN membership are not the same thing.

IEEE 802.1Q adds four bytes between the source MAC and the original Type/Length field. Its two-byte TPID is 0x8100, which identifies a tagged frame; the two-byte TCI carries 3 priority bits (802.1p) and a 12-bit VLAN identifier. Because the tag adds four bytes, the basic untagged 1518-byte maximum becomes 1522 bytes. The lecture warns that configuration and traffic rules become harder to track as VLANs grow.

Lab 1.2 observation sequence: clear, wait, generate traffic, compare

Use observations instead of assuming a clean MAC table

Lab ports: PC-A→S1 F0/6; S1 F0/1↔S2 F0/1; PC-B→S2 F0/18. Addresses: PC-A .3, PC-B .2, S1 SVI .11, S2 SVI .12, all /24; gateways are N/A. Use actual device outputs; the worksheet leaves observations blank.

1. Record identifiers before interpreting the table

On each PC run ipconfig /all; on S1 and S2 run show interface F0/1 and record the hardware address/BIA. The MAC table alone may not tell you which named PC is behind a learned entry if you have not recorded these identifiers.

2. Inspect S2 before pings

Run show mac address-table on S2. There may already be dynamic entries from attached devices, the other switch, or background traffic. Ignore CPU entries when matching host addresses to ports.

3. Clear only dynamic entries, then sample twice

Run clear mac address-table dynamic; immediately run show mac address-table, then wait 10 seconds and run it again. Clearing the switch table does not flush PC-B’s ARP cache. New frames can repopulate the table during the interval.

4. Observe PC-B ARP and generate the requested pings

On PC-B run arp -a; count unicast IP/MAC pairs and exclude broadcast/multicast entries. Ping PC-A, S1, and S2; record actual success or failure and check cabling/addressing if necessary.

5. Compare both caches

Run show mac address-table on S2 and arp -a on PC-B again. The switch learns source MACs to local ports as frames arrive; PC-B learns IP-to-MAC mappings for local peers. The counts need not match because the tables map different identifiers and may contain different kinds of entries.

Common mistakes

  • Carrier sensing lowers collision risk; it cannot prevent two distant stations from starting before either signal arrives.
  • Polling selects who may use the medium. Stop-and-wait and continuous ARQ recover delivery problems; they are not MAC access families.
  • The lecture's serial-style RTS/CTS sketch, slide 25's flow-control label, and optional 802.11 RTS/CTS reservation have different contexts.
  • A token-ring physical star through a MAU can still carry a logical ring. Token Bus has physical bus wiring and a separate logical token order.
  • In an Ethernet backoff calculation, r is a random integer and 0 ≤ r < 2ᵏ; the upper endpoint is excluded. The lecture's attempt-count wording does not define whether n counts collisions or all attempts.
  • The 1518-byte untagged Ethernet maximum is a total frame limit excluding preamble/SFD; it is not the Type/Length threshold. Length is at most 1500; EtherType starts at 1536.
  • A switch learns from the source MAC and looks up the destination MAC. Unknown unicast is flooded as copies of the original unicast frame; broadcast has an all-ones destination.
  • A MAC table maps MAC/VLAN to a local port. Behind another switch, many device MACs can map to the same uplink.
  • show arp maps network-layer IP addresses to MAC addresses. show mac address-table maps VLAN/MAC addresses to switch ports. show interfaces vlan 1 reports the management SVI, not Fa0/6.
  • The lab worksheets ask for actual measurements. Do not copy sample addresses or treat the illustrative empty-table trace as measured output.

Exam focus

  • IEEE 802.3 is the lecture's Ethernet/CSMA-CD family; 802.4 is Token Bus; 802.5 is Token Ring; 802.2 is LLC.
  • Token Ring gives a controlled turn by passing a free token. A transmitter turns it busy, lets the frame circulate and be copied, removes it when it returns, then releases a free token.
  • CSMA listens first; CSMA/CD detects collision while sending on a shared wired half-duplex link, jams, aborts, and applies a random slot backoff.
  • Ethernet frame fields: 64-bit preamble/SFD, 48-bit destination, 48-bit source, 16-bit Type/Length, 46–1500-byte data/pad, 32-bit FCS. Basic maximum frame is 1518 bytes without preamble/SFD.
  • IFG is 96 bit times; the 10/100 Mb/s slot time is 512 bit times. In the lecture's backoff formula, k=min(n,10) and r ranges from zero through 2ᵏ−1.
  • Half-duplex Gigabit used a 4096-bit extended slot and carrier extension; frame bursting shares the contention win across a bounded burst. Full duplex has independent simultaneous transmit/receive paths, no collision backoff, and retains IFG.
  • Switches learn source MAC → ingress port within a VLAN, then look up the destination. Known unicast goes to its mapped port; unknown unicast and broadcast are flooded in that VLAN, except ingress.
  • VLAN tagging uses a 4-byte 802.1Q tag: 2-byte TPID 0x8100 and 2-byte TCI with 3 priority bits and 12 VLAN-ID bits; the lecture does not expand the remaining TCI bit. Basic maximum grows from 1518 to 1522 bytes.

Practice questions

Quiz 1, Q2. Why does Token Ring give predictable access under heavy traffic?

Check your answer

Stations take turns as the free token circulates, so each station has a bounded opportunity to capture it under the lecture’s functioning-ring assumption. This differs from contention, where repeated collisions can delay a station.

In Token Ring, what happens after the busy frame returns to the transmitter?

Check your answer

The transmitting station removes the busy token and its data, then transmits a free token. The next node gets first opportunity; the sender does not keep the same turn indefinitely.

For the worked Ethernet backoff rule, what integer values may r take when n=3?

Check your answer

k=min(3,10)=3, so 0 ≤ r < 8: the possible values are 0 through 7. At r=5, the wait is 5 × 512 = 2560 bit times, which is 256 μs at 10 Mb/s and 25.6 μs at 100 Mb/s.

Quiz 1, Q8. After detecting an Ethernet collision, what does CSMA/CD do?

Check your answer

It sends the collision-enforcement jam, aborts that frame, waits a random backoff period, then retries. The quiz’s short answer is abort and wait a random backoff before retransmission.

Quiz 1, Q10. Which shared-medium data-link sublayer controls access?

Check your answer

MAC (Media Access Control). LLC provides the logical-link interface; MAC handles medium access.

A frame arrives on S2 F0/18 with source MAC B and an unknown unicast destination MAC A. What does S2 learn, and where can the frame go?

Check your answer

S2 first learns B → F0/18. It then floods a copy of the original unicast frame to eligible forwarding ports in that VLAN except F0/18. The destination field remains A; it is not rewritten to the broadcast address.

How do you distinguish the three Lab 1.1 commands show interfaces vlan 1, show arp, and show mac address-table?

Check your answer

The first reports the VLAN 1 SVI and its MAC/BIA; ARP maps IP addresses to MAC addresses; the switch MAC table maps VLAN/MAC addresses to local switch ports.
