WAN and MAN technologies

Follow the lecture’s historical path from dedicated lines to shared packet services, ATM cells, MPLS labels, and Ethernet First Mile access.

Big picture

A WAN connects distant sites through a service-provider network; a MAN serves a metropolitan area. The lecture follows a historical design question: reserve a whole line, or share provider links with other traffic? Packet WANs evolved from X.25 toward lighter Frame Relay service and fixed-size ATM cells. MPLS adds label-based forwarding to a carrier core, while Ethernet First Mile extends Ethernet over provider access links.

The shared theme is where capacity and forwarding state live. A leased line reserves a physical link. Packet networks share transmission capacity; virtual circuits add logical per-flow state without dedicating a wire. These are historical lecture topics, not a survey of current carrier products.

WAN, MAN, and the provider network

In the lecture’s approximate boundaries, a LAN is generally under 1 km in diameter, a WAN typically exceeds 30 km, and a MAN covers a metropolitan area up to about 50 km. The Colombo Metro example runs from Moratuwa to Ja-Ela. The lecturer’s organizing premise is that formerly distinct LAN/MAN/WAN technologies were converging, with Ethernet used at all three scales; this does not mean geography or provider roles disappear.

A provider network has customer-facing access links, switches, and higher-capacity transmission links between switches. Its core can have alternate paths. Access brings subscribers into the network; transmission carries traffic among provider switches.

Dedicated line or shared packet network?

A leased line is a full-time dedicated point-to-point connection, continuously available at a specified capacity. The lecture’s examples are 64 kb/s, 2 Mb/s, and 155 Mb/s. Its period-specific common range is 64 kb/s–2 Mb/s, with 64 kb/s for a few computers and 2 Mb/s for a large office. One slide prints “2MB”; the rate context suggests a unit typo for 2 Mb/s, not megabytes per second.

The slides distinguish analog and digital leased lines, noting most had become digital by the lecture’s period. Customers pay a fixed monthly cost regardless of use. Uses include voice and tie lines, corporate data networks, Internet access, and combined voice/data. Dedicated capacity is straightforward and continuously available, but idle capacity is still paid for.

Packet switching divides a stream into packets. Packets from different connections take turns on shared links, so one flow’s idle periods can be used by another. This statistical sharing helps with bursty traffic; it does not mean every flow can transmit at peak simultaneously or that capacity is unlimited.

With one leased line per customer, all three capacities remain reserved while two customers are idle.

Share a provider link

With packet switching, packets from whichever customer is active can use the shared link. Capacity is statistically shared across flows.

Check the conclusion

Sharing avoids reserving three separate physical routes for this pattern. If bursts overlap or exceed shared capacity, queues or loss may occur; no guaranteed rate follows from this example.

Datagram forwarding and virtual circuits

A datagram is routed independently, packet by packet. Packets in one conversation can encounter different routes and may arrive in a different order. A virtual circuit (VC) selects a route for a connection or flow; packets carry a connection identifier and follow that logical route. The network keeps per-connection state.

A VC is not a dedicated physical circuit: it is a logical path over shared links. Route selection once per flow and physical reservation are separate properties.

X.25 and Frame Relay

X.25: early virtual-circuit packet service

The lecture describes X.25 as the ITU standard for a user interface to a packet-data network, used by banks and airlines. It characterizes X.25 as expensive and slow, at about 64 kb/s. The slide also says “Introduced in the 1960s”; distinguish packet-network research history from the standard date: ITU-T’s history says the first X.25 Recommendation was approved in 1976 (ITU-T history).

In the comparison, X.25 is connection-oriented and uses virtual circuits. The lecture presents substantial internal error/flow-control work as a reason it was slower than later alternatives.

Frame Relay: lighter frame service over provider VCs

Frame Relay was presented as an X.25 replacement: it relays frames, is virtual-circuit oriented, and can carry IP datagrams, so IP can use it as a link-layer service. The lecture dates its design to the late 1980s and wide deployment to the 1990s. Its contrast with X.25 is reduced hop-by-hop sequencing and recovery work under an assumption of more reliable links.

The slide shorthand “no error control” is too broad. Frame Relay reduces link-by-link recovery overhead; it still detects frame errors. Congestion notification is also distinct from end-to-end congestion control. The simpler source point is that more recovery responsibility sits with endpoints. See the Frame Relay operational model in RFC 1315 and reduced sequencing/flow-control scope in RFC 1973.

A corporate customer could lease service from a public provider and use permanent VCs as logical pipes between routers. Multiple VCs can share one physical access link. CIR means Committed Information Rate: a negotiated per-VC service commitment that affects the charge. The slide calls it “guaranteed”; read that as a commitment within service terms, not an unconditional end-to-end throughput, delay, or availability promise.

ATM: fixed cells and virtual paths

Asynchronous Transfer Mode (ATM) switches fixed-size 53-byte cells: a 5-byte header and a 48-byte information field. These sizes are specified in ITU-T Recommendation I.361. The lecturer’s motivation was integrated data, voice, and video: uniformly small cells and a simple header were expected to process quickly and reduce variation in switching time. That is a design rationale, not a guarantee of low end-to-end latency.

Cell part Size Role
Header 5 bytes Carries forwarding and control fields
Information field (payload) 48 bytes Carries the cell data
Total cell 53 bytes Fixed-size unit switched by ATM

The lecture describes ATM as scalable and flexible, with possible charging by cell count rather than a fixed rate such as 64 kb/s or 2 Mb/s. It says ATM generally used optical fibre; treat that as a typical medium in the lecture’s period, not a defining requirement.

Cells follow a VC and carry a VC identifier rather than a full destination ID. Switches keep state for active connections; bandwidth or buffers may be allocated for circuit-like behavior. In the slide’s simplified hierarchy, VPI groups a virtual path and VCI identifies a virtual channel in that forwarding context. Values are forwarding identifiers, not global IP destinations; switches may translate them. The slide also labels GFC, PT, and HEC fields.

The source's header sketch occupies five rows, each representing a header octet. Its boxes are schematic rather than a complete bit-width specification:

Header octet Fields shown, left to right
1 GFC, VPI

A virtual path groups virtual channels; that grouping describes the identifiers, not two successive routing decisions. At a virtual-channel switch, use the incoming interface and VPI/VCI context together to find the outgoing mapping. The ATM cell specification, I.361, defines the routing-field interpretation. The drawing above shows the forwarding lookup rather than branching from a path into several simultaneous destinations.

The original vision was ATM from desktop to desktop as a network technology. In practice, the lecture says ATM was used as a switched link layer connecting IP backbone routers; layer role depends on context.

Worked example: derive ATM cell payload efficiency

Calculate the payload share

Lecture values: header 5 bytes; payload 48 bytes; total 53 bytes.

Identify sizes

Header = 5 bytes and payload = 48 bytes, so total = 5 + 48 = 53 bytes.

Set the fraction

Payload efficiency = payload / total = 4853.

Convert to percent

Payload share: 4853×100%≈90.566% ≈ 90.6%. Header share: 553×100%≈9.4%.

Check

90.6%+9.4%=100.0% after rounding. This is a size ratio, not application throughput.

MPLS: labels through a carrier core

Multiprotocol Label Switching (MPLS) forwards packets using labels. The lecture’s simplified stack places it between IP or another multi-service layer and Layer 2 technologies such as PPP, ATM, or Frame Relay. It emphasizes service-provider and carrier networks.

At the MPLS edge, traffic is classified into a forwarding-equivalence class and a label is added. Core nodes use the label to choose the next forwarding action rather than repeating the same network-header analysis at every hop. “Multiprotocol” describes support across network-layer protocols and link technologies. The slide says “IP is used to signal MPLS connections”; more precisely, IP routing supplies reachability while label bindings are distributed by label-distribution mechanisms. The slide names BGP-4, OSPF, and IS-IS in its IP-routing context, without teaching their operation here. RFC 3031 defines labels as locally significant forwarding identifiers and separates label distribution from IP reachability. The lecture says MPLS is typically used in service-provider/carrier networks rather than private networks.

The lecturer’s before/after figure shows separate PSTN, IP, and Frame Relay/ATM/TDM networks consolidated onto an IP/MPLS core to support varied service needs. Named capabilities include RSVP, IP multicast, IP CoS, network scalability, traffic engineering, and VPNs. “Route at the edge, switch at the core” is a conceptual summary, not a complete protocol specification.

The “best of both worlds” slide attributes scalability, flexibility, ease of deployment, low cost, and dynamic routing to IP packet forwarding; it contrasts these with connection orientation, performance, traffic engineering, security, and QoS support associated with ATM/Frame Relay. A VPN is logical service separation; MPLS alone does not encrypt traffic.

Ethernet First Mile (EFM): the lecture’s Metro Ethernet section

Ethernet First Mile (EFM), standardized in IEEE 802.3ah, is the lecture's Metro Ethernet access technology. EFM connects homes and offices using Ethernet over copper or fibre.

EFM form Medium and method Exact lecture values or examples
EFMC (copper) DSL modulation carries Ethernet frames Short reach: 10 Mb/s at 750 m; long reach: 2 Mb/s at 2,700 m. The lecturer says products may exceed these objectives over longer distances.
EFMF (fibre) Point-to-point fibre or Passive Optical Networks (PON) Dual fibre: 100BASE-LX10, 1000BASE-LX10. Single fibre: 100BASE-BX10, 1000BASE-BX10.

These are the lecture’s historical objectives and standard labels. Do not infer current product performance from them.

Quick revision

  • LAN: generally under 1 km; MAN: up to about 50 km in this lecture; WAN: typically over 30 km. Access connects subscribers; transmission links connect provider switches.
  • Leased line: dedicated, always available, fixed monthly charge. Packet switching: statistical sharing, useful for bursty traffic.
  • Datagram: independent packet routing. VC: one logical route and per-flow state; not a dedicated wire.
  • X.25: about 64 kb/s on its dedicated slide; the “1960s” date and 0.64 Mb/s comparison entry need caveats.
  • Frame Relay: virtual-circuit frame service with reduced hop-by-hop recovery overhead; CIR is a per-VC commitment within service terms.
  • ATM: 53-byte cell = 5-byte header + 48-byte payload; VPI/VCI identify path/channel forwarding context.
  • MPLS: labels identify forwarding classes inside a provider domain; IP reachability and label distribution are related but distinct.
  • EFM: the copper objectives and fibre standards above are the actual scope of the Metro Ethernet slide section.
