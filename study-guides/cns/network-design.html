Network design

Network design starts with the services an organization needs, then fits the network to its business goals, access rules, existing assets, growth, and traffic.

Big pictureDesign considerationsFour complementary design strategiesApply the design ideasCommon mistakesExam focusPractice questions

Big picture

A network is successful when it supports the organization’s work at an appropriate cost and level of service. Begin with business outcomes and constraints, then decide where applications and data belong and what paths users need. The lecture presents six design considerations and four complementary design strategies. They are useful questions to revisit as the design takes shape.

Design considerations

1. Match business needs

State what the organization is trying to accomplish. The lecture uses better customer service as an example of a business goal. Decide which services to provide or withhold and where their applications and data should be placed. A proposed network feature is justified when its expected business value—including competitive advantage and return on investment—makes sense against infrastructure cost.

2. Plan for future growth

Ask what may expand before fixing the design. Consider applications, services, servers, office networks, wireless hotspots, geographic coverage, technology adoption, changes to the business, and connections to outside organizations. The lecture also names extranet links, subsidiaries, and other parties. Growth may affect capacity, the locations a network must reach, and how easily later changes can be made.

3. Inventory existing infrastructure and physical constraints

Existing equipment is already a capital investment. Assess whether it can be reused in a different network role instead of assuming that every redesign requires disposal. Its performance and suitability still need to fit the proposed role. Building locations, physical security, and staff access also constrain placement.

The lecturer’s example is a large campus with a star-wired multimode-fibre backbone: a central location where the backbone fibres meet is a natural candidate for the core switch. The physical cable layout helps determine where the logical core can sensibly sit.

Worked decision: place a campus core switch

Given: a large campus has a star-wired multimode-fibre backbone.

  1. Locate where the backbone fibres converge and identify the buildings they serve.
  2. Check whether that central location is physically secure and accessible for installation and maintenance.
  3. Use it as the candidate core-switch location because it matches the existing cable arrangement.
  4. Before committing, check the actual equipment, capacity, and building constraints; the slide offers a placement cue, not a universal rule that every campus core must be central.

Check: the proposed location is supported by the given fibre topology and remains subject to security and access constraints.

4. Decide Internet and intranet requirements

Branches can use their own Internet connections or send Internet traffic through head office. Neither arrangement is always correct. Compare the control the organization needs with users’ need for access and freedom; consider confidentiality, openness, reliability, availability, and dependence on one provider or site. Decide what policing the intranet needs and which services are public versus internal.

5. Balance security and accessibility

Ask who should access which services. Organizational policy, computer-use policy, and IT-security policy shape those paths. Include mobile employees and decide whether single sign-on or distributed authentication better fits the organization. Identify servers that need stronger protection. The design should make required access possible while limiting other paths; this is a network-placement and reachability question, not a reason to repeat the separate security foundations material.

6. Choose an appropriate architecture

Avoid sophistication that does not answer a real need. Estimate current requirements from traffic loads, server workloads, and application types. The lecturer’s slide says most equipment will be outdated in three years while cabling may last longer. Treat the three-year statement as the lecturer’s planning heuristic, not a guaranteed lifecycle for every device. The lecture presents structured cabling as easier and more cost-effective to manage and enhance over time.

Four complementary design strategies

The strategies are different ways to examine the same requirements. A design can begin from an application, then be checked against security, topology, and traffic concerns. Use each lens to uncover a different constraint.

Design lens Start by asking What it helps determine
Function or application Which business applications and data must be supported? The network, servers, storage, data-centre needs, and supporting IT organization
Security Who may communicate with what, under which policies and threats? Protected nodes, segmentation, and permitted paths
Topology, technology, or protocol Which LAN, WLAN, WAN, or tunnel fits the location and constraints? Suitable connectivity given speed, technology, budget, and environment
Traffic Which traffic is delay-sensitive, where can congestion or failure matter? Queueing, priority, switching, load distribution, redundancy, and server availability

Function or application based design

Also called business–IT alignment, this lens starts with organization-specific applications and the data they use. Those needs guide which network forms and infrastructure should be designed. In the lecture figure, business drivers lead into an IT environment that contains business applications and data alongside network, server, and storage infrastructure. Security, systems management, and disaster recovery span that infrastructure. The data centre and IT organization and processes are part of the wider setting too.

The redrawn figure keeps the cross-cutting concerns visible: they apply across infrastructure rather than forming a single network hop.

Security based design

This lens considers physical and network threats and the policies applying to offices, branches, and third parties. The slides distinguish limited access (they name proxies and network address translation, or NAT) from privileged access for people such as directors, managers, and collaborators (they name VPNs and user- or role-based access). NAT can also be used to address IPv4 address depletion, as the slide notes. Supplemental clarification: address translation by itself does not define authorization or guarantee protection; access still depends on policy and enforcement.

The lecture also names server farms and VLANs for critical-node protection, firewalls, intrusion detection systems (IDSs), and intrusion detection and prevention (IDP). Consider how a multi-tier application communicates and whether supporting services—mail relays, DNS servers, and authentication, authorization, and accounting (AAA) servers—need different reachability. If an application lacks its own cryptographic support, the lecture suggests considering isolation; it notes that this can require additional subnets and firewall interfaces.

The slide uses “SSL” as an example of application cryptography. That is historical terminology: SSLv3 is prohibited by IETF RFC 7568. For current protocol terminology, refer to Transport Layer Security (TLS), specified for version 1.3 in IETF RFC 9846. This note does not expand into the separate secure-protocol topic.

Topology, technology, or protocol based design

Compare LAN, WLAN, and WAN options against their speed and technology differences, budget, and environmental constraints. For extended networks, the slides identify VPNs and tunnels as ways to support WAN security or extend a LAN. They name PPTP and L2TP as examples to explore, not as a deployment recommendation. PPTP is a legacy example; its original specification describes security limitations (IETF RFC 2637).

Traffic based design

This lens asks whether applications tolerate delay and where traffic could queue or congest. The lecture names priority traffic, congestion avoidance, wire-speed switching, load balancing, reliability and availability, redundant paths, and server availability. Quality of service (QoS) is considered through observable properties such as latency and throughput. The lecturer contrasts satellite and fibre-optic media as having different performance characteristics but supplies no figures; do not infer a numerical comparison from this slide. Priority can influence which traffic waits during congestion; it does not create link capacity, and a redundant path does not by itself make an unavailable server available.

Supplemental worked decision: apply all four lenses to a branch service

Branch service design review

Given (supplemental scenario): branch staff need an internal record service at head office and public Internet access. The organization is concerned about protected records, head-office path availability, and competing traffic. No traffic measurements or cost figures are supplied.

Name the function

Identify the internal record service and public Internet access as two separate needs with different destinations and data.

Set access boundaries

State which branch users may reach the records service. Public Internet access must not imply access to internal records; protect the permitted path under organizational policy.

Compare feasible connectivity

Compare a branch-local Internet exit with sending public traffic through head office. Check budget, available technologies, environment, control, and provider dependence while preserving the required internal path.

Examine traffic and availability

Separate traffic that can tolerate delay from traffic that cannot. Estimate actual loads before choosing priority, capacity, switching, or redundancy measures.

Check the result

Verify that chosen paths serve both applications, respect access policy, and meet availability needs. The facts given do not support a universal local-egress, centralized-egress, or capacity recommendation.

Apply the design ideas

For a useful design discussion, connect each proposed feature to a stated need: “this path or placement supports this service, policy, growth expectation, or traffic requirement.” The lecture’s campus example shows physical topology guiding equipment placement; its branch question shows how access, control, confidentiality, and availability change the Internet path decision.

The Lab 3.1 walkthrough applies these ideas to a switch-and-router topology, addressing plan, configuration, and connectivity checks.

Common mistakes

  • Selecting devices before identifying the services, users, and constraints they need to support.
  • Treating every existing device as either automatically reusable or automatically disposable; check each proposed role.
  • Assuming every branch should use the same Internet exit without comparing control, availability, provider dependence, and policy.
  • Treating NAT alone as an access-control policy.
  • Equating priority or QoS with extra capacity, or assuming redundant paths solve server failures.
  • Treating the four design strategies as competing architectures instead of complementary viewpoints.
  • Repeating the slide’s three-year equipment estimate as a universal lifecycle guarantee.
  • Treating named historical protocols such as PPTP as present-day recommendations.

Exam focus

  • Recall the six considerations: business needs; future growth; existing infrastructure; Internet/intranet requirements; security versus accessibility; and appropriate architecture.
  • Distinguish the four lenses by their first question: application and data, access and threats, topology and constraints, or traffic and performance.
  • In a scenario, state the requirement before naming the design consequence. Explain how physical routes can constrain logical placement.
  • Discuss branch egress as a trade-off among control, confidentiality, availability, and provider dependence; do not claim that one pattern is always correct.
  • Relate traffic concerns to delay, throughput, queueing, congestion, load balancing, redundancy, and server availability without inventing thresholds absent from the lecture.

Practice questions

1. Why can existing fibre layout affect where a campus places its core switch?

Check your answer

A star-wired backbone brings links together at a physical location. A central candidate can fit that layout, subject to building security and accessibility constraints.

2. Give two growth areas to consider before choosing network placement or capacity.

Check your answer

Examples include applications/services/servers, office networks, wireless hotspots, geographic coverage, technology adoption, business diversification, extranet links, subsidiaries, and other parties.

3. Name three criteria for comparing branch-local Internet access with head-office egress.

Check your answer

Any three of controllability, confidentiality, openness, reliability, availability, provider dependence, intranet policing, or the distinction between public and internal services. Explain how the chosen requirement affects the paths.

4. A network proposal adds priority queues for an application but leaves its server unavailable. Has the service requirement been met?

Check your answer

Not necessarily. Priority can affect queueing under congestion, but it does not make an unavailable server available. The traffic lens explicitly includes server availability as well as links and paths.

5. How do the function-based and topology-based lenses differ?

Check your answer

Function-based design starts with organization-specific applications and data and derives infrastructure needs. Topology/technology/protocol-based design compares connectivity choices such as LAN, WLAN, WAN, or tunnels against speed, technology, budget, and environment.
