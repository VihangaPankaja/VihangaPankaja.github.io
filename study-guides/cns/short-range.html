Bluetooth, NFC and ZigBee

Choose a short-range network by matching its topology, protocol support, energy use and application role to what devices actually need to exchange.

Big picture

TCP/IP is useful for general-purpose communication, but small nearby devices may need less data, less energy, smaller hardware, or a different topology. Bluetooth, NFC and ZigBee show how the network should fit the application. Start by deciding what the devices must exchange and how they are organised, then compare range, rate, power, cost, and interoperability. A gateway can connect a specialised local network to an IP service when needed.

Choosing communication for a room-light control task

The lecture asks groups of three to design communication for several lights in one room: choose connectivity topology, define a simple command protocol, estimate its payload and compare TCP and UDP overhead across transport, IP and Ethernet. An explicit SET light-id ON or SET light-id OFF command is easier to retry safely than TOGGLE: if a command is duplicated, “set ON” leaves the same state while a second toggle undoes the first. The byte calculation below uses the sizes in the lecturer’s speaker note and declares the otherwise-unspecified payload.

A small direct-control network does not automatically need a full IP stack. If the application later needs remote monitoring or integration, keep that as an explicit gateway or interoperability decision. Detailed TCP/UDP behavior belongs in Transport services.

Bluetooth: personal-device links

The lecture presents Bluetooth as cable replacement, multiparty exchange and communication among personal trusted devices. It notes Ericsson’s role in development and the Bluetooth SIG’s management. Its version-specific illustration gives Classic Bluetooth 2.0 a shared 2.1 Mbps rate and about 10 m range in the 2.4–2.48 GHz ISM band. These are historical lecture figures, not universal current limits. Example devices include phones, laptops, peripherals, printers, headsets, speakers, fitness/healthcare devices, car kits and navigation equipment.

Stack responsibilities and profiles

The stack divides radio-link control, adaptation and application services. Baseband manages physical channels and links, including frequency hopping/time division, error correction, whitening, hop selection and security. LMP sets up and controls a radio link, including link authentication and power management in the lecture’s table. HCI is the host/controller software interface. L2CAP multiplexes logical connections and segments/reassembles packets. SDP discovers another device’s services and their parameters. RFCOMM emulates a serial line; TCS-BIN supports telephone control; OBEX exchanges structured objects. The slide’s audio path is drawn directly over lower layers, so do not assume it always follows the same stack path as data protocols.

Usage examples in the slide place Sync/IrMC, file transfer/OBEX, dial-up/AT commands, fax/TCS-BIN, headset/audio, LAN access/TCP-IP/PPP and cordless phone over shared lower layers. Profiles define possible applications and their expected behavior on top of the core specification and, where needed, extra protocols. The named profiles are HFP (Hands-Free), BPP (Basic Printing), AVRCP (Audio/Video Remote Control), FTP (File Transfer), HID (Human Interface Device), PAN (Personal Area Networking) and GOEP (Generic Object Exchange, using OBEX).

Classic piconets and scatternets

In the lecture’s Classic piconet, one master coordinates active slaves. Slaves do not exchange data directly; a slave-to-slave message goes through the master. The source gives up to seven active slaves and 255 parked slaves. Preserve “Classic”: these are not general Bluetooth Low Energy limits. Connecting two or more piconets through a device that participates across them forms a scatternet.

Version note. The lecture and Quiz 2 associate Classic, High Speed and Low Energy with Bluetooth 4.0, and describes High Speed as Wi-Fi based. Preserve that as the course’s version-era summary, but distinguish lineage: Bluetooth SIG describes LE as introduced in Core 4.0, while High Speed appeared with Core 3.0+HS. The 4.0 wording should not imply High Speed originated there. Bluetooth Core change history. The slide 37 “3 channels” figure is a simplified comparison: LE has 40 channels, of which 3 are primary advertising channels and 37 are general-purpose channels (Bluetooth SIG overview).

NFC: intentional close-proximity exchange

The lecture’s historical timeline says NFC began in 2004 through Nokia, Philips and Sony, followed by a Nokia phone in 2006 and an Android phone in 2010. Its application collage includes transport/check-in, mobile identification, marketing, payments and social interaction.

Near Field Communication couples devices through nearby antenna coils and a magnetic field, as the lecture’s reader/tag figure shows. The lecture gives 13.56 MHz, at most 10 cm, and 106–424 kbps; retain these as course figures rather than current universal guarantees. NFC is convenient, low-cost, low-energy and avoids a separate search-and-pair procedure, but has low range and data rate. The lecture calls proximity “better security”; treat that as a limited advantage, not a security guarantee. Short distance can reduce exposure, but does not itself authenticate a peer or secure an application. NFC Forum describes authentication, access control and encryption as additional measures (NFC security FAQ). Its current overview gives different typical range and rate figures, illustrating why values are version/context dependent (NFC technical overview).

Two independent ways to describe NFC operation

Active/passive answers which device produces the field. In active mode, both devices generate a field (the lecture’s example: two phones). In passive mode, one active reader supplies the field and the other device responds using it (phone reading an RFID-tagged poster). Separately, the application can use card emulation, peer-to-peer or reader/writer mode. Card emulation lets a phone act like a contactless smart card for payment or ticketing through existing readers. Peer-to-peer exchanges data such as business cards, photos, or Bluetooth/Wi-Fi setup information; the slide shows LLCP and protocol bindings such as IP/OBEX. Reader/writer reads or writes NFC Forum tag types using RTD/NDEF. The stack figures label Type 1–4 tags and a separate Type 3/FeliCa path alongside NFC-A, NFC-B and NFC-F; named examples include Topaz, MIFARE and FeliCa, not universal product requirements. A mode switch connects these application paths to radio support such as ISO 18092, ISO 14443 A/B and FeliCa. Touch & Go, Touch & Confirm and Touch & Connect are application interaction patterns, not synonyms for active/passive.

Worked classification: phone reads an RFID poster

Separate NFC application role from field mode

Scenario from the lecture and Quiz 2: a phone reads an RFID-tagged poster.

1 · Phone approaches a tagged poster

The phone is the reader/initiator and generates the electromagnetic field.

2 · Tag responds using that field

The poster’s passive tag uses the reader’s field to exchange information; it does not generate its own field. This is passive mode.

3 · Name both dimensions

Application role: reader/writer. Field generation: active phone with passive tag. These labels answer different questions, so “reader/writer” does not mean “active mode.”

ZigBee: low-rate sensing and control

The lecture associates ZigBee with low power, long battery life, low data rate, small/simple circuits and lower cost. The application collage covers patient and fitness monitoring; building security, HVAC (heating, ventilation and air conditioning), lighting, access control and irrigation; asset, environmental and energy management; industrial/process control; telecom/m-commerce; and consumer peripherals such as remotes. These are use examples, not a promise that every ZigBee deployment shares one range or lifetime.

IEEE 802.15.4 supplies the PHY and MAC layers for a low-rate WPAN. ZigBee adds network construction, application support, application/device objects and higher-layer services. ZigBee profiles specify offered services and a common data language; profile/device descriptions have unique identifiers licensed by the ZigBee Alliance to support interoperability across manufacturers. This layer distinction is supported by the Zigbee specification.

Device capabilities and network shapes

An IEEE 802.15.4 FFD can act as PAN coordinator, coordinator or simple device and communicate with FFDs or RFDs. An RFD is intended for simple, low-volume applications that communicate with a particular FFD. Capability is not the same thing as a network topology role: “FFD/RFD” describes device function, while “coordinator/router/end device” describes participation in a network.

TopologyHow traffic is organisedTrade-off in the lecture
StarEach device communicates through the coordinator.Easy to synchronise and low latency; limited scale.
Mesh / peer-to-peerRouting-capable devices can forward along alternate paths.Robust multihop, multipath, flexible and lower latency; route discovery and routing-table storage cost resources.
Cluster treeDevices form parent-child branches from a root.Low routing cost, multihop and scalable; route reconstruction can be costly and latency long. A failed parent can isolate its branch.

The cluster-tree slide’s last disadvantage is malformed (“Root not becomes a single point of failure”), while Quiz 2 explicitly says the root is a single point of failure. A tree has no alternate parent path around a failed link, so root/parent dependence is the safe interpretation; do not describe the tree as multipath. Mesh’s extra discovery and stored routing state buy alternate paths.

Beacon timing, sleeping and polling

Beacon-enabled operation periodically announces a superframe. A device listens, synchronises, learns when the contention access period is available, then competes with peers to transmit. Coordinator and end devices can sleep between active periods, reducing energy, but precise timing is required; the slide gives beacon periods from milliseconds to minutes. Without beacons, the coordinator/router stays awake while end devices can follow different power schedules. The lecture calls this a heterogeneous network with asymmetric power use rather than synchronised sleep.

Route discovery and maintenance

The lecture describes a 16-bit network address space. A sender first checks its routing table. If no active route exists, it broadcasts RREQ; routers establish entries toward the source as the request propagates. An endpoint router replies with RREP, allowing entries toward the destination and a source-side next hop to be recorded. The lecture’s diagram illustrates this exchange but does not define the link labels as a metric or specify a general shortest-path algorithm.

B responds with RREP along the depicted reverse path B→C→U→A. This is the route reply path shown by the lecture figure.

4 · Record a usable next hop

A records U as its next hop toward B. Independent check: distinguish the broader request exploration from the return reply path. Do not infer a shortest-path algorithm from the edge labels.

Routing checks for an existing entry, tries discovery when possible and can fall back to hierarchical routing. Maintenance tracks failed neighbor deliveries and repairs after a threshold; too much repair traffic can itself load the network. If connectivity is entirely lost, the device uses orphaning and reassociates with the network.

Comparing the lecture’s examples

Teaching example: choose a network for a sensor building

Given: thousands of battery-powered sensors and light controllers; messages are small and infrequent; batteries should last months to years.

Reasoning: prioritize low data rate, low power, and a topology that can cover a larger installation. The lecture presents ZigBee as low-rate/low-power, with star, mesh and cluster-tree choices and a months/years illustrative power profile. NFC is too close-range for building coverage; the lecture’s Wi-Fi profile uses more power; Bluetooth LE’s comparison profile is shorter. The quiz therefore selects ZigBee for this scenario.

Check: this is a lecture-based fit for the stated requirements, not a guarantee of a node count, range or battery life in an actual deployment.

The slide’s comparison is useful for exam recall, but its range, rate, channels, node count and power profile are illustrative historical figures, not modern guarantees or a product-selection specification. In particular, its “7” belongs to the Classic Bluetooth piconet model; “100+ m” for ZigBee depends on conditions and topology; “months/years” is a workload-dependent power profile. The slide’s 65,000 ZigBee node figure is a comparison-table claim, not a general capacity guarantee.

Common confusions

  • Do not mix Bluetooth Classic piconet limits with general Bluetooth Low Energy connection behavior.
  • Do not let an NFC application role (reader/writer, peer-to-peer, card emulation) stand in for active/passive field generation.
  • IEEE 802.15.4 is not the complete ZigBee stack; it contributes PHY and MAC.
  • A mesh has routing discovery/state and can offer alternate paths; a pure tree does not.
  • Beacon mode trades synchronization requirements for coordinated sleep; non-beacon mode keeps coordinator/router infrastructure awake.
  • “Close range” or “better security” does not replace authentication and application protection.

Quick revision

  • Bluetooth Classic: personal links; master-coordinated piconet; up to seven active slaves in the lecture model; no direct slave-to-slave traffic. LMP controls links, L2CAP multiplexes/adapts, SDP discovers services, and profiles define application behavior.
  • NFC: 13.56 MHz in the lecture; active/passive describes field generation. Card emulation, peer-to-peer and reader/writer describe application role.
  • ZigBee: IEEE 802.15.4 PHY/MAC plus ZigBee networking/application services; FFD/RFD capabilities; star, mesh and tree have different routing costs; beacon and polling sequences support low-power devices.
  • Route discovery: check route → broadcast RREQ → endpoint returns RREP → record route/next hop; repair and reassociate when needed.
