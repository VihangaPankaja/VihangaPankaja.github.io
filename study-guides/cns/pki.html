Public key infrastructure

PKI helps people decide whether a public key really belongs to the identity it names. Certificates, trusted issuers, registration checks, directories, and revocation information work together to support that decision.

Big picture

Public-key encryption only helps if the sender has the right recipient's public key. If an attacker substitutes their own key, a sender can encrypt a secret for the attacker while believing it is protected for the intended person. Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) is the framework of certificates, organizations, directories, procedures, protocols, and policies used to distribute public keys with an identity binding that others can evaluate.

The central credential is a digital certificate: it associates a subject's identity with a public key, and a Certificate Authority (CA) signs that certificate. A Registration Authority (RA) checks the applicant's identity and passes the request to a CA. A directory helps people retrieve certificate information. A CA can revoke a certificate, and records revoked certificates in a Certificate Revocation List (CRL). These pieces provide a basis for trust; a certificate by itself does not encrypt a session or make every application decision for you.

The cryptography context

The lecture opens by contrasting symmetric and asymmetric systems. These are only the background needed for the PKI problem; for how keys are used in encryption, signatures, hashes, and message authentication, follow the Security foundations and cryptography page.

Lecture comparison Symmetric system Asymmetric system
Keys The same shared secret is used at both ends. A public/private key pair is mathematically related; the public key may be distributed, while the owner controls the private key.
Main trade-off in the slides Fast, but every communicating pair needs its own secret and secure key delivery/storage. Easier key distribution and scaling, but slower operation.
Source's security claim Symmetric encryption is described as providing confidentiality, not authenticity or nonrepudiation. This statement is about encryption alone; a separate message authentication code can provide authentication. The slides say asymmetric systems can provide authentication and nonrepudiation, depending on how the keys are used.

For suitable algorithms and parameters, deriving a private key from its public key is computationally infeasible. This is the precise form of the lecture's absolute wording; see the NIST public-key cryptography definition. If a private key is disclosed, the owner's security is compromised. The lecture names RSA, ECC, Diffie–Hellman, and ElGamal as asymmetric examples. This is a broad historical list, not a claim that each system supports the same encryption/decryption operation or is current deployment advice. It also names DES, 3DES, RC4, RC5, RC6, and AES as symmetric examples; the list is preserved as lecture context, not a recommendation.

Why a public key needs an identity binding

The lecture's Alice, Bob, and Charles example asks how Alice can check the authenticity of a public key she receives. If Alice encrypts a session key with a substituted key, Charles may be able to receive it. The cryptography can operate as designed while the identity assumption is wrong. PKI addresses the identity-to-key question by relying on certificates issued by a CA that the verifier trusts.

The original slide names the attack but does not show each intercepted message. The diagram summarizes the risk rather than claiming a complete protocol trace. For the key-use and signature fundamentals behind this example, see Security foundations and cryptography.

PKI is a framework

The lecture defines PKI as a system of programs, data formats, procedures, communication protocols, security policies, and public-key mechanisms working together. It lists confidentiality, access control, integrity, authentication, and nonrepudiation as services supported by the framework. Treat these as framework capabilities: a certificate alone does not enforce application authorization, encrypt all traffic, or guarantee nonrepudiation in every context. The surrounding cryptographic protocol and application determine what a particular exchange actually provides.

Certificates bind a subject to a public key

A digital certificate is a credential containing a subject's public key and identifying information. The CA's signature endorses the certificate's binding. People who have not met can evaluate that binding through a CA they both accept as a trust source; this is indirect reliance on the CA, not personal knowledge of one another.

The lecture's X.509 diagram names these fields. Read them by asking: which certificate is this, who issued it, who or what is its subject, what public key is bound to that subject, and during what validity interval does the certificate claim to apply?

Standards clarification: the lecture's field picture compresses the signature information. RFC 5280 represents a certificate with a tbsCertificate, a signatureAlgorithm, and a signatureValue; the signature is computed over the DER-encoded tbsCertificate. See RFC 5280 §4.1 and §4.1.1.3. For signature mechanics, use the Security Foundations material rather than treating this note as a signing tutorial.

The CA is the trusted organization or server that maintains and issues certificates. It may be internal to an organization or a dedicated external service. The exact certificate fields and policies determine what identity claim is being endorsed; the general word “trusted” does not mean that every CA is accepted for every purpose.

Registration, issuance, and retrieval

The lecture separates identity registration from certificate issuance. The RA establishes and confirms an individual's identity, initiates the certification process with a CA on the end user's behalf, and performs certificate life-cycle management functions. It can broker between the user and CA, but cannot issue certificates. The CA constructs and signs the certificate, sends it to the requester, and maintains it over its lifetime. A directory provides a place from which a person can request certificate or public-key information.

For the lifecycle issues actually named, the CA may revoke a certificate when it was issued using false credentials or when the holder's private key has been compromised. It records revoked certificates in the CRL and updates the list periodically. An RA's listed life-cycle duties are not expanded into renewal, rekey, or detailed enrollment procedures in this lecture.

Revocation, configured trust, and terminology

The CA's CRL matters because an issued certificate may later cease to be trustworthy—for example, false credentials are discovered or the subject's private key is compromised. The source says the CA maintains and updates the list periodically. It does not describe how a verifier obtains a fresh list, handles an unavailable list, or checks revocation in a particular protocol.

The browser slide illustrates a configured CA list: browsers accept certificates signed by CAs present in a configured list. Its Internet Explorer certificate-store screenshot is historical UI; the lasting concept is that trust begins from configured CA certificates, not that today's browsers use that same screen.

Term Meaning in the lecture
Issuer The organization, server, or person that issued a certificate.
Subject The person who obtained the certificate to prove identity.
Target The person whose certificate another party seeks.
Principal Anything or anyone that has a public key.
Verifier The person evaluating a certificate chain.

The “certificate chain” term signals that a verifier may evaluate a linked set of certificates, not only the target certificate. The lecture defines the role but does not teach chain-building or validation rules. For lower-level encryption, hashing, and signature distinctions, see Security foundations and cryptography.

Quick revision

  • A certificate associates identifying information with a subject public key; a CA signs the certificate binding.
  • The RA checks identity and forwards/initiates a request; the CA issues and maintains the certificate.
  • A directory distributes certificate information. A CRL records revoked certificates and is periodically updated.
  • PKI is a framework whose surrounding protocols and policies provide services; a certificate alone is not the whole service.
  • In the John–Diane drawing, John uses Diane's key before the figure shows him validate it.
  • The X.509 picture's “Signature” gloss is algorithm identification; the actual signature value is distinct in the RFC structure.

Practice

Why can encrypting with a public key still fail to protect the intended recipient's secret?

Check your answer

Because a sender may have received an attacker's key while believing it belongs to the recipient. Encryption then protects the secret for the holder of the substituted private key, so identity binding must be checked.

Which role checks the applicant's identity, and which role issues the certificate?

Check your answer

The RA establishes/confirms identity and forwards or initiates the request. The CA constructs, signs, sends, and maintains the certificate; the RA cannot issue one.

Name the two revocation reasons given in the lecture and the record used for revoked certificates.

Check your answer

False credentials and compromise of the holder's private key are listed reasons. The CA records revoked certificates in a periodically updated Certificate Revocation List (CRL).

What authentication gap is visible in the John–Diane diagram?

Check your answer

John obtains Diane's public key and encrypts a session key with it before the figure shows him validating that key. Diane's validation of John's key is shown afterward.
