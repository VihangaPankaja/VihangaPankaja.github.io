Values, Culture, and Person-Environment Fit

Separate values, job fit and organizational fit without turning national culture into an individual label.

What matters, and where it fits

  • Values rank what is desirable. Watch everyday choices as well as stated principles.
  • Fit with job tasks and fit with organizational culture can differ.
  • National-culture dimensions describe broad patterns, with variation inside every country.

What values mean at work

A value is a basic belief that a way of behaving or an end-state is personally or socially preferable to its opposite. Values carry a sense of what ought or ought not to happen. People bring these standards into an organization. They can help someone notice what matters in a decision, but they can also cloud objectivity.

The lecture says values influence workplace attitudes and behaviour.

Supplemental explanation. Keep three ideas separate. A belief describes what someone thinks is true. An attitude evaluates a person, object, or situation. A value says what outcome or conduct is desirable.

For example, “this report may contain an error” is a belief about the report; “I dislike the review process” is an attitude; “we ought to correct safety information before release” is a value. An attitude or action alone does not reveal a person’s complete value system.

Personal values and work values

Personal values concern what matters across life; work values concern what matters at work. Their priorities can differ by situation. Compare an organization’s stated principles with its routine decisions to understand the values people experience.

Teaching example: distinguish a value from a belief and an attitude

An analyst thinks a safety report contains an uncertain measurement. That is a belief about what the evidence may show. The analyst feels frustrated by the approval process. That is an attitude toward the process. The analyst believes the team ought to check a plausible safety risk before closing the report. That is a value about responsible conduct.

The analyst’s next action may reflect all three, along with deadlines and workplace rules.

Supplemental explanation. A value has a content, meaning which end or mode of conduct matters, and an intensity, meaning how strongly it matters compared with other values. Priorities across values form a value system. This distinction helps explain why two people can endorse fairness yet differ when fairness conflicts with speed. The lecture itself classifies values by their ends and means below.

Terminal and instrumental values

Rokeach’s distinction separates the desired end from the preferred means. A terminal value is a desirable end-state or lifetime goal. The lecture’s example is economic success and prosperity. An instrumental value is a preferred mode of behaviour used to reach an end. The lecture gives personal discipline and being goal-oriented as examples.

People can value both what they want to achieve and how they pursue it, so the slides emphasize balancing ends and means.

Teaching example: connect a desired end with its means

A person wants economic success and prosperity. That desired outcome is the terminal value. The person uses personal discipline and goal-oriented effort as preferred ways to pursue it. The same end could be pursued through other behaviours, so a goal alone does not tell us which means someone values. A conflict may arise if a chosen means undermines another important value.

Person–environment fit

The lecture considers how well people match work settings. Personality describes characteristic tendencies; values describe preferred ends and standards. They can both matter when a person weighs a job or organization, but they are not interchangeable. Holland’s person–job fit theory focuses on matching personality characteristics to job requirements.

Person–organization fit focuses on compatibility between a person’s values/personality and an organization’s culture. These are related questions with different comparison targets.

The lecture emphasizes applicants’ flexibility across changing situations and commitment to the organization over their ability to perform one specific job. This is its stated hiring emphasis, not a universal rule for every role.

Holland’s person–job fit and RIASEC

Holland’s theory presents six personality and occupational orientations. The lecture says satisfaction should be highest and the tendency to leave lowest when a person and occupation agree. Its table links preferred activities, described characteristics, and congruent occupations. These are broad theory categories, not fixed identities or hiring rules.

The source hexagon places Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, and Conventional in that order around six sides, returning to Realistic. Adjacent categories are described as similar and more compatible. Diagonally opposite categories are highly dissimilar. The hexagon represents similarity, not a sequence of development or a causal path.

Source example: compare three Realistic job matches

A Realistic orientation prefers practical physical activities involving skill, strength, or coordination. A mechanic is listed as a congruent occupation, so this is the clearest match of the three examples. An Investigative occupation such as biologist is adjacent to Realistic on the lecture’s hexagon and is less congruent in the slide’s comparison.

Social is opposite Realistic, and the lecture calls a Realistic person in a Social job the most incongruent case. This is Holland’s prediction about fit, not a guarantee about an individual’s satisfaction or ability.

Person–organization fit

Person–organization fit asks whether a person’s values and personality are compatible with an organization’s culture. The lecture’s theory says people are attracted to and selected by organizations that match their values, and may leave organizations that do not fit their personalities. It proposes that stronger cultural fit may support satisfaction and lower turnover.

Fit should be assessed with evidence about the person and the actual workplace. It does not mean that every employee should think alike or that job competence can be ignored.

Teaching example: separate job fit from organization fit

A person enjoys helping and developing others but applies for a role dominated by solitary data validation. The daily task mix raises a person–job fit question. Whether the employer’s culture values careful service, collaboration, or rapid independent completion raises a separate person–organization fit question.

Before judging either match, learn more about the actual tasks, the person’s preferences, and how the organization works. One interest alone cannot decide the outcome.

National culture and Hofstede’s framework

Hofstede’s framework compares national-culture patterns. The deck is inconsistent: slide 72 says five dimensions; slide 73 shows six. The table follows the six-dimension graphic and preserves its endpoint wording.

Source context for the six dimensions

The slide describes more than 116,000 IBM employees in 40 countries. The framework developed over time; the final two dimensions came from later work. These are national comparisons with substantial within-country variation. Hofstede’s six-dimension model.

DimensionLower end in the graphicHigher end in the graphic

Femininity vs. masculinityNurture is importantPower is important
Long-term orientationTraditional and short-termFuturistic and long-term
Restraint vs. indulgenceRestraint is goodSatisfaction is good

These dimensions compare cultural patterns at a broad level. They are not personality categories, individual employee scores, or reliable explanations of one person’s choices. A request for written decision rationale, for example, may reflect role needs, past team experience, or individual preference. Nationality alone does not establish which value a colleague holds.

Reasoning from a country-level pattern to an individual is an ecological fallacy. A related research discussion warns against carrying national-culture associations down to individuals or organizations: Brewer and Venaik, “The Ecological Fallacy in National Culture Research” (2014).

Teaching example: respond to a request for written decisions

An international teammate asks the group to document why it chose one proposal. Do not infer a national-culture dimension from that request. Ask what information the role requires, whether the teammate has had trouble tracing past decisions, and what format would help the team.

Hofstede’s framework can prompt a careful comparison of group-level patterns, but it cannot tell you this colleague’s motive or preference by itself.

Job purposing: put values into everyday work

Bea Boccalandro calls small changes to ordinary work that contribute to a social purpose job purposing. A worker can put values into daily interactions and decisions without changing employers or waiting for a company-wide program.

Examples in the article include inviting quieter colleagues into discussion, addressing bias in property sales and buying from minority-run small businesses. These illustrate possible actions, not universal instructions.

What the assigned article reports about outcomes

The author reports 13% higher average job satisfaction and higher likelihoods of raises and promotion in research on social purpose. An image-labeling experiment and another study are separate examples. The latter reports 24% faster work and 43% less downtime without lower quality; those figures should not be assigned to the image-labeling experiment.

These are the HBR author’s research summaries. The underlying studies were not independently replicated here, and the numbers are not guaranteed individual outcomes.

Source case: Tom supports a colleague during a work interaction

Situation: An injured patient objects to a woman patroller handling the sled. Value at stake: respectful and fair treatment at work. Job-purposing action: Tom says the patient’s objection has no basis and points to the patroller’s competence. He uses an ordinary work interaction to support a broader social purpose while continuing the task.

Reported outcome: the author says the patient apologized about a year later. That ending belongs to the author’s story; it does not show that every challenge will change someone’s views or be safe in every context.

Teaching example: find a values-based action within a routine task

An analyst values accuracy and public service, while the team’s written process emphasizes closing reports quickly. The analyst notices an uncertain safety result and proposes a short escalation check before closure. Accuracy and public service are values; the uncertainty about the measurement is a belief; frustration with delay would be an attitude.

The analyst has adapted a routine task toward a social purpose. The team still needs to check the evidence, authority, and applicable procedure before acting.

Common mistakes

  • Treating values, beliefs, attitudes, and personality as interchangeable. They answer different questions.
  • Reversing Rokeach’s categories: terminal values are desired ends; instrumental values are preferred means.
  • Treating a single action as proof of a person’s values, or assuming a stated organizational principle matches daily practice.
  • Confusing person–job fit with person–organization fit. One concerns job demands; the other concerns organizational values and culture.
  • Reading the RIASEC hexagon as a causal sequence or assuming a type predicts ability, satisfaction, or career destiny.
  • Memorizing five Hofstede dimensions from slide 72 while ignoring the six shown in slides 73–74, or silently pretending the conflict is resolved.
  • Using national-culture patterns to assign motives or values to an individual employee.
  • Treating the Adam Grant social-media quote on character and personality as a validated definition or empirical result.
  • Treating HBR’s anecdotes and reported study figures as universal effects, or confusing job purposing with leaving a job to volunteer.

Practice questions

1. Classify each statement: “The report may contain an error”; “I dislike the review process”; “We ought to correct safety information before release.”

Check your answer

The first is a belief about what may be true. The second is an attitude toward the process. The third is a value about desirable conduct.

2. In Rokeach’s classification, which is the end and which is the means: economic prosperity, personal discipline?

Check your answer

Economic prosperity is the terminal value, or desired end-state. Personal discipline is an instrumental value, or preferred means.

3. What is the RIASEC order around the lecture’s hexagon, and what does adjacency mean?

Check your answer

Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional, then back to Realistic. Adjacent types are described as more similar and compatible. Opposite types are highly dissimilar. The relationships do not imply causation.

4. A role’s tasks suit an employee, but the organization’s routine decisions conflict with values the employee considers important. Which fit lens addresses each issue?

Check your answer

Person–job fit addresses the match between the person and job requirements or tasks. Person–organization fit addresses compatibility with organizational values and culture.

5. Why should a manager avoid concluding that an employee holds a particular value because of their nationality?

Check your answer

Hofstede’s framework describes broad national-culture patterns. Applying an aggregate pattern directly to an individual is an ecological fallacy. The manager needs evidence about that employee and the situation.

6. Slide 72 says five dimensions, but slides 73–74 display six. How should you report the framework from this deck?

Check your answer

State that slide 72’s historical note says five, while the later diagram shows six. Name the six visible dimensions and acknowledge that the deck does not explain the discrepancy.

7. What does job purposing mean, and how does Tom’s response in the HBR story illustrate it?

Check your answer

Job purposing means adapting ordinary work actions so they contribute to a social purpose during the workweek. Tom used a work interaction to challenge a patient’s sexist objection and support his colleague’s competence. It is the author’s anecdote, not proof of a universal effect.

8. How should a student describe the HBR article’s percentage claims in an exam answer?

Check your answer

Attribute them to the HBR author’s summary of studies, distinguish them from the article’s practice examples, and avoid presenting the figures as guaranteed outcomes. The underlying studies were not reviewed for these notes.
