Managing Emotions at Work

Recognize emotions, ask what support is wanted, and choose a response that fits the person and the task.

Notice first. Respond with context.

  • Notice, Name, Need and Normalize are a leader’s reflection practices, not a script to impose on someone.
  • Acknowledge emotion without assuming you know its cause or demanding disclosure.
  • Support can mean listening, helping with the task or arranging a later conversation.

This topic connects the previous lesson's emotion and emotional-intelligence foundations to practical leadership. The readings offer three related but distinct contributions: Dina Denham Smith's four practices for emotional awareness and a leader's climate; an HBR interview's context-sensitive guidance for responding to another person; and Sound Community's broader recommendations for workplace culture. They are not one combined research model.

Why emotions matter at work

Denham Smith argues that dismissing emotions as distractions or signs of weakness can encourage suppression. Her article associates prolonged ignoring or suppression with costs to wellbeing, morale, teamwork, and organizational results. It also treats emotions as information about a person's environment and as part of connection, influence, and motivation.

Sound Community's article describes stress, excitement, frustration, fear, and joy as common at work. It associates positive emotional experiences with creativity, engagement, communication, and collaboration, and unmanaged negative experiences with disengagement, conflict, or lower productivity. This broad framing is a reminder to attend to team climate, not a rule that positive feelings are always useful or negative feelings should disappear.

Examples of how workplace emotions may show up

Teaching example: frustration as a cue: A project handoff is missed twice, and two coworkers become short with each other. Frustration may signal an unmet expectation or a process problem, but the manager should ask what happened before deciding that either person is careless. The emotion is a reason to inquire, not proof of a diagnosis or a complete explanation.

A leader's four practices: Notice, Name, Need, Normalize

Denham Smith proposes a sequence for developing emotional awareness and making emotion discussable. It begins with the leader's own experience and extends to curiosity about others. The steps are a practical mnemonic, not a substitute for the separate interpersonal response choices described later.

Notice: observe cues

Emotions can show up in bodily sensations, behaviour, and energy. A leader might notice their own jaw tightening, unusually rapid speech, withdrawal, procrastination, or an unexpected drop in energy. With another person, observable cues include tone, facial expression, body language, mismatch between words and expression, or a change from that person's usual participation.

These observations do not reveal an exact emotion. They create a reason to pay attention and, if appropriate, ask. A colleague who becomes quiet might be disappointed, concentrating, tired, or simply choosing not to speak.

Teaching example: notice without inference: In weekly planning, an analyst who is usually talkative says little after a schedule change. "I noticed you have been quieter since we changed the deadline" reports an observation. "You are angry about the deadline" turns that observation into an unsupported conclusion.

Name: build precision, especially about your own feeling

Emotional concepts are learned through social experience and culture. Denham Smith recommends going beyond broad words such as "mad," "sad," and "glad." More specific words, such as frustrated, resentful, provoked, joyful, relieved, or confident, can help a person describe their own experience with greater precision. A careful question can invite someone else to be more specific, but should not impose a label on them.

Teaching example: name your own feeling: A manager catches herself thinking "I am angry" after a tense budget meeting. On reflection, she identifies disappointment about a decision and anxiety about how to explain it to the team. That distinction helps her choose what to address, without claiming that the same vocabulary will fit every person's experience.

Need: ask what the feeling may be signaling

After noticing and naming an emotion in oneself, ask what message it might carry. It may point toward something important in one's needs, values, boundaries, relationships, or environment. For another person, the recommended move is gentle, open-ended inquiry rather than guessing what they need.

Teaching example: inquire before solving: An employee has stopped contributing to design reviews. The manager says, "I noticed you have not been joining the discussion as much lately. How have you been feeling about the work?" If the employee says the new review format feels rushed, the manager has learned something actionable.

If the employee does not want to discuss it, that boundary should be respected.

Normalize: make room without oversharing

Denham Smith recommends that leaders model grounded, intentional honesty about both difficult and positive emotions. A brief, relevant acknowledgement can signal that emotion is allowed and can support trust and psychological safety. Normalizing does not mean disclosing everything: the article warns that oversharing can undermine credibility and destabilize a team.

Teaching example: normalize rather than suppress: During a demanding release week, a manager says, "This is a hectic week. I am feeling the pressure too, and we will take it one step at a time." The manager names a shared strain while staying focused on the work.

If instead the manager unloads private distress and expects employees to provide comfort, the disclosure asks the team to manage the leader.

Respond to another person: acknowledge, then choose the timing

The HBR interview with Christina Bradley and Lindy Greer adds a separate decision lens: ask whether the work is time-sensitive and whether the person is coping well enough to continue. These questions help choose timing and support; they are not a diagnostic test. The person's preference for privacy also matters.

Managers may hesitate because of boundaries, discomfort with emotion or concern about mental-health topics. The interviewees also advise engaging with positive emotion, such as a colleague’s excitement.

Who does the reported “80%” describe?

The interviewees say responders default to giving advice or problem-solving about 80% of the time in the research they discuss. This is about the response people give, not a finding that employees prefer receiving advice. They also report about 90% avoidance of emotion in conflict. These are their reported figures, not universal rates.

Two context checks for choosing when and how to respond
Work contextIf the person is copingIf the person is not coping
Time-sensitive workAvoid unnecessary interruption; check in privately when there is a suitable pause.Intervene enough to protect the person and the task. Follow up with acknowledgement when there is time.
Time permitsAcknowledge the situation, ask how they are experiencing it, and offer help without pressure.Pause or move the conversation somewhere private; listen, validate, and ask what support would help.

The interview's examples show why context matters. Surgeons described situations where a visibly rattled resident could put a critical task at risk, making immediate intervention necessary. A Navy SEAL described first regulating his own response, waiting when a teammate could cope during a high-pressure moment, and checking in during a lull.

These examples support the distinction between urgent task protection and later validation; they are not a universal script for every workplace.

Case: a difficult call before a client presentation

An employee becomes quiet after a difficult call. Ignoring it assumes they will recover; announcing “You are angry” assumes you know their feelings.

Check whether they can proceed with the urgent task. Offer immediate help if needed. Otherwise, ask privately afterward: “That call sounded difficult. How are you doing, and would any support be useful?” The employee can correct your impression or decline to discuss it.

Validate the experience; do not diagnose the emotion

The interview cautions that confidently naming another person's precise emotion can backfire: someone described as "frustrated" may actually feel confused. Describing a shared situation, "That meeting felt tense", or asking how it landed leaves room for the person to explain. In a public meeting, asking someone to account for a subtle emotional cue can expose them.

When they are coping, a private follow-up is usually less pressuring. If they cannot participate, or safety or task performance is affected, pause and respond promptly.

Acknowledgement does not require immediate advice. The interviewees describe the common impulse to solve, reframe, or make a person look on the bright side. A more useful order is to recognize what is happening, listen, and then ask whether the person wants help or advice.

If they do, offer coaching questions, such as what another perspective might reveal or what a trusted mentor might suggest, rather than dictating the "correct" interpretation.

Teaching example: acknowledgement before advice: A teammate says they feel overlooked after a decision was made without their input. "You should just be glad the project moved forward" dismisses the experience. "It sounds like being left out of that decision mattered to you.

Would you like to talk through what happened, or would you prefer help planning what to raise next time?" acknowledges first and lets the teammate choose the next step.

The interview applies to peers and clients as well as direct reports. In a digital conversation, acknowledge the message and offer an appropriate way to talk. A leader can give brief context for their own stress without making others responsible for managing it.

Build a supportive emotional culture

Sound Community Services offers broad practitioner recommendations for the everyday conditions around emotional wellbeing: open and thoughtful communication, recognition of achievements, regular check-ins, work-life balance, boundaries, breaks, and emotional-intelligence development. It also mentions personal awareness and brief mindfulness or breathing practices as ways to respond rather than react.

These suggestions focus on routines and working conditions, not on replacing a one-to-one acknowledgement when someone is distressed.

The HBR article and interview add that leaders can acknowledge positive emotions as well as difficult ones, anticipate emotionally significant events, and learn from examples of constructive responses. This does not mean a manager must make employees disclose. A supportive culture leaves room for people to speak and for people to keep private matters private.

Teaching example: culture practice with a boundary: After a difficult reorganization announcement, a manager schedules a check-in, explains what is known, invites questions, and recognizes the uncertainty people may be experiencing. Employees can share concerns or pass. The check-in creates a channel for communication; it does not promise that everyone will feel positive or that a single meeting resolves the underlying disruption.

Common mistakes

  • Treating emotion as unprofessional and responding with silence or suppression.
  • Assuming that facial expression, tone, or quietness proves a particular emotion or need.
  • Equating normalization with forced disclosure or unrestricted leader self-disclosure.
  • Calling out a person's subtle emotion publicly when a private conversation would preserve their choice.
  • Rushing to advice or positivity before acknowledging what the person has said.
  • Applying one response regardless of urgency, task risk, coping, or the person's preference.
  • Treating broad culture suggestions as a substitute for addressing workload, conflict, or other workplace conditions.
  • Blending the three practitioner readings into one formal model or presenting their recommendations as one empirical study.

Practice questions

1. A normally engaged employee becomes quiet in a meeting. What can a manager conclude from this cue?

Check your answer

The manager can conclude that there is a change worth noticing, but not which emotion caused it. A respectful, preferably private question gives the employee room to explain or decline.

2. Name the four practices in Denham Smith's leadership framework and state what "Need" asks the person to do.

Check your answer

Notice, Name, Need, Normalize. "Need" asks the person to consider what their own emotion may signal about needs, values, boundaries, relationships, or context. With someone else, ask rather than infer their need.

3. A person seems upset during a time-critical task but is still performing safely. What response does the interview support?

Check your answer

Avoid unnecessary interruption, then check in privately at a suitable pause. If the person is not coping or safety is at risk, intervene enough to protect the task and person, then acknowledge and follow up when possible.

4. Why can "You look frustrated" be less helpful than "That meeting felt tense to me" followed by an open question?

Check your answer

The first assigns a specific internal state that may be wrong. The second describes the situation and invites the other person to explain their own experience.

5. A colleague shares disappointment about being passed over for a project. What should come before advice?

Check your answer

Acknowledge and listen to the experience, then ask whether the colleague wants to talk, wants advice, or wants another kind of support. Reframing should be offered as coaching, not imposed.

6. Give one Sound Community culture recommendation and explain why it does not replace the response framework in the HBR interview.

Check your answer

For example, regular check-ins can support open communication and help surface concerns. They are a broad team routine; a specific response still depends on urgency, whether the person is coping, privacy, and what help they want.

7. A manager wants to normalize stress during a busy period. What makes the disclosure grounded rather than oversharing?

Check your answer

Keep it brief, relevant to the shared work, and paired with a constructive next step. Do not unload private distress or make employees responsible for reassuring the manager.
