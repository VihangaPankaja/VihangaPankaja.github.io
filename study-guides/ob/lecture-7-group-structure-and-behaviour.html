Shared expectations shape group behaviour

  • A role concerns expected conduct; a norm is a shared standard; status is a socially assigned rank.
  • Make contributions identifiable to reduce social loafing.
  • Cohesion strengthens shared performance norms. A close group can reinforce low output.

Two development models help explain different patterns. Tuckman’s stages describe a possible path for groups working together; punctuated equilibrium describes temporary groups working toward a deadline. The remaining ideas explain how group expectations and relationships affect everyday behaviour and results.

What counts as a group?

A group is two or more people who interact, depend on one another, and come together to achieve particular objectives. Groups may be formal or informal. A formal group is defined by the organization: assigned work, established tasks, and behaviour directed toward organizational goals. An informal group is not created by the organization’s formal structure.

It develops through social contact, yet its interactions can still affect work behaviour and performance.

Teaching example: a formal project group and an informal lunch group

A manager assigns employees from finance, operations, and sales to deliver a forecast. The assigned task and responsibilities make this a formal group. Those employees also meet for lunch because they enjoy one another’s company. That social group is informal. The informal connection may help them communicate at work, but its existence does not replace the formal group’s task or authority.

How groups develop

Tuckman’s five stages

The lecture’s sequence is Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing → Adjourning.

  1. Forming: clarify purpose and tasks; members may rely on a leader.
  2. Storming: differences about direction and power become visible.
  3. Norming: work through conflict and establish expectations, roles and trust.
  4. Performing: coordinate work and use members’ strengths.
  5. Adjourning: recognize contributions, reflect and move on.

The lecture slide labels Adjourning “Stage 4,” even though the same deck labels Performing “Stage 4.” Here it is correctly presented as Stage 5: the five-stage sequence added adjourning after the original four stages in Tuckman and Jensen’s review. Primary reference: Tuckman & Jensen (1977).

The model is a guide, not a promise that every group moves neatly forward. Groups progress at different rates; some remain in storming. Storming and performing can coexist, and a group can return to an earlier difficulty. A clear purpose and strategy, and a positive social focus, may help a group reach productive work sooner.

Moving to a later stage alone does not prove the group is effective.

Teaching example: diagnosing a project team’s stage without treating it as a score

A newly assembled project team is polite, asks who owns each task, and waits for the lead to settle basic questions. These clues fit forming. Two weeks later, members openly challenge the schedule and compete over priorities: that is consistent with storming. If they agree on handoffs and resolve disagreements constructively, norming is emerging.

A later return to conflict after a new member joins is possible; it does not mean the earlier progress was false.

Temporary groups and punctuated equilibrium

Temporary groups with deadlines may follow a different pattern. In the lecture’s semester-presentation example, the first meeting sets a direction. A period of inertia or slow progress follows. At the exact midpoint of the allotted time, a transition prompts major change. A second period of inertia follows, then activity accelerates at the last meeting as the deadline approaches.

The model emphasizes long periods of relative stability interrupted by a brief change triggered mainly by awareness of time and deadlines.

Teaching example: a semester presentation at its midpoint

A group agrees on a topic at its first meeting, then makes little visible progress during the first half of the semester. At the midpoint, members recognize that half the available time is gone, divide the remaining work, and change their plan. Some slow progress may follow while those new arrangements settle.

The last meeting is intensely active as members combine the presentation before the deadline. The midpoint transition is a feature of the deadline model; it is not a sixth Tuckman stage.

Roles, norms, and sanctions

Roles: position and expectations

A role is the set of behaviour patterns expected of someone in a position within a social unit. People hold several roles at work and outside it. Role perception is how a person understands the tasks, goals, and problems attached to a role. Role expectations are what the group or society expects that person to do.

A role conflict occurs when simultaneous expectations are incompatible, or when responsibilities overlap with another worker or group. Greater role conflict is associated with greater work-related stress.

Teaching example: overlapping responsibility creates role conflict

A project coordinator is told by one manager to release a report immediately and by another to withhold it until confidential figures are checked. The problem is not simply a large workload: the two role expectations are incompatible. Clarifying decision authority and the release procedure addresses the conflict more directly than asking the coordinator to work faster.

Group norms and their domains

Norms are shared standards about what members ought or ought not to do in particular situations. They are different from formal rules, policies, and laws, which are enforced through an organization’s legitimate hierarchy. Norms emerge more naturally and often influence behaviour with little external control. They can cover nearly any aspect of group behaviour.

Norm domain What members may expect
Performance How hard to work, how to do a task, and what level of lateness is acceptable
Appearance Dress, presentation, or unspoken expectations about looking busy
Social arrangements Whom to eat with, off-job friendships, or when to offer a community elder tobacco before asking advice
Resource allocation How difficult jobs, pay, equipment, or other resources are assigned
Communication Turn-taking and respectful language during discussion
Decision-making Whether to seek consensus or use majority rule, and how members follow the outcome
Teaching example: identify the norm and the formal rule

A department has a written safety policy requiring eye protection in the workshop. That is a formal rule. Team members also tend to wait until the shift lead finishes before raising a concern. That turn-taking expectation is a communication norm. A group may have both at once; the norm is not a substitute for the written safety requirement.

Social sanctions are group responses that reinforce norms. Positive sanctions reward conformity, for example through praise, recognition, increased status, or access to group benefits. Negative sanctions express disapproval, such as ostracism, criticism, or loss of trust and support. Sanctions can promote conformity, reinforce a group’s sense of cohesion, and help members maintain order.

But conformity by itself does not show that a norm is fair, useful, or consistent with formal policy.

Teaching example: a sanction reinforces a harmful norm

If coworkers praise members who stay silent about a recurring safety problem and exclude a member who raises it, praise and exclusion are positive and negative sanctions, respectively. They reinforce the group’s norm of silence, but the norm can still be harmful and conflict with formal safety expectations.

Deviant workplace behaviour

Deviant workplace behaviour—also called antisocial behaviour or workplace incivility in the lecture—is voluntary behaviour that violates significant organizational norms and threatens the well-being of the organization or its members. The lecture sorts examples along two separate dimensions: who or what is targeted, and how serious the act is.

Its image-only diagram on slide 34 places interpersonal behaviour on the vertical axis and minor-to-serious behaviour on the horizontal axis.

Target ↓ / seriousness → Minor Serious
Interpersonally directed Political deviance: spreading rumours, favouritism, backstabbing Personal aggression: sexual harassment, verbal abuse, physical assault
Organizationally directed Production deviance: absenteeism, lateness, withholding effort Property deviance: theft, sabotage, vandalism

The lecture’s examples also include leaving early, intentionally working slowly, and wasting resources (production); misusing equipment and stealing from the organization (property); blaming coworkers (political); and stealing from coworkers (personal aggression). Do not sort an act by its label alone: classify its target and seriousness. The slide’s four categories summarize common patterns; a specific example may need context to judge severity.

Causes described in the lecture include perceived unfairness in pay, promotion, or workload; job dissatisfaction; an unsupportive or disrespectful culture; stress or burnout; ineffective leadership; peer norms; opportunity and rationalization; and individual differences such as low conscientiousness or high impulsivity. These are possible contributors, not a diagnostic checklist. One behaviour alone does not identify its cause or establish a trait.

The lecture proposes a possible group-level chain: shirking, undermining, or noncooperation can contribute to negative group moods; those moods can impair coordination and lower group performance.

Prevention therefore includes respectful and inclusive culture, clear policies and expectations, open communication and feedback, employee involvement, constructive conflict-resolution processes, support systems such as counselling or employee assistance, recognition and training, monitoring the work environment, and leaders modelling appropriate conduct.

Teaching example: classify and respond to two different acts

A team member repeatedly arrives late and withholds effort. The lecture places lateness and withholding effort in production deviance: the target is organizational work, and the examples are on the minor side of the source’s seriousness axis. A supervisor discovers deliberate equipment sabotage: that is property deviance, directed at organizational property and on the serious side.

For either case, first establish what happened and apply clear policy consistently; do not assume the same cause or response fits both people.

Status, voice, and group size

Status is a socially defined position or rank assigned to a group or member by others. A perceived mismatch between the status a person believes they have and the status others give them can motivate behaviour. The lecture identifies three sources: power over people or resources; contribution that is critical to group goals; and personal characteristics valued by that group.

What a group values varies by context; examples in the lecture are illustrations of local valuation, not universal traits or predictions about individuals.

Status interacts with norms. Higher-status members may receive more freedom to depart from norms and may resist conformity, especially when they are valued but do not depend on group social rewards. Adding high-status members may improve performance only up to a point; they may also introduce counterproductive norms.

Status also affects who speaks: high-status members tend to speak, criticize, command, and interrupt more, while lower-status members may participate less. When quieter members have relevant expertise, failing to hear them can reduce creativity and performance.

Teaching example: a lower-status expert is not heard

In a planning meeting, a junior technician notices that the proposed schedule conflicts with a maintenance window. Senior members talk over the warning. The group should distinguish speaking time and rank from the quality of the information: invite the technician to explain the constraint and check the schedule before deciding.

The scenario illustrates how status can shape voice; it does not imply that lower-status members are always correct.

Group size depends on the task. The lecture suggests that a dozen or more members can be useful when the aim is to gather diverse input or facts. A smaller group of about seven may be better when members must produce something concrete within a deadline. These are task-dependent lecture guidelines, not universal cutoffs.

Effort, cohesion, and performance

Social loafing

Social loafing, or free riding, is the tendency to exert less effort in a group than when working alone. When responsibility is shared, a person may feel less accountable for the overall result.

The lecture lists diffusion of responsibility, reduced accountability, unidentifiable contributions, low perceived task importance, the presence of free riders, larger group size, and negative group culture or low cohesion as possible contributors.

The lecture qualifies the pattern by culture: the studies it reports found social loafing more consistently in individualist US and Canadian settings, while the Chinese and Israeli comparison groups showed no propensity to engage in social loafing and performed better in a group than alone. Treat this as a reported group-level finding, not a rule for predicting any individual or country.

The suggested remedies are to set shared group goals; use intergroup competition focused on shared outcomes; use peer evaluation; select members who are motivated and prefer group work; and, where possible, base part of group rewards on each member’s identifiable contribution. These approaches make purpose, effort, and contribution more visible.

Teaching example: select a remedy that addresses the cause

A large team’s report is late, and members cannot tell who owns which section. Because contributions are hard to identify and responsibility is diffuse, assigning named sections with peer review and a shared deadline directly improves visibility and accountability. Adding a reward without clarifying contributions may not address the problem.

If low task importance is the issue, the manager should also explain how the report will be used.

Cohesion depends on performance norms

Cohesion (also called cohesiveness or social cohesion) is the strength of the bonds linking members and their sense of unity. Cohesion affects productivity through the group’s performance-related norms. It does not guarantee high output by itself: a cohesive group reinforces the standards it shares.

Performance-related norms High cohesion Low cohesion
High High productivity Productivity rises, but less than when both are high
Low Low productivity Low-to-moderate productivity

The lecture lists ways to encourage cohesion: make the group smaller, agree on group goals, spend more time together, raise group status and the perceived difficulty of membership, stimulate competition with other groups, give rewards to the group, and physically isolate it. These are possible levers, not unconditional recommendations; choose them only when they support the group’s task and healthy norms.

Teaching example: cohesion amplifies the norm a group already has

Two teams feel equally close to one another. Team A has strong norms for quality and cooperation, so its high cohesion supports coordinated, high productivity. Team B treats low output as acceptable; its cohesion can reinforce that low standard, leaving productivity low. To improve Team B, changing the performance norm matters as well as strengthening bonds.

Diversity and faultlines

Group diversity means including members with varied backgrounds and perspectives, including differences such as age, gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic position, and culture. Variety can add creativity, insight, and problem-solving capacity, while also creating challenges for group dynamics.

The lecture says diverse groups may experience more conflict early in their time together, with lower morale and greater dropout, and may perform better over time if initial conflict is managed. This is a qualification about group processes, not an individual prediction based on demographic identity.

A group faultline is a hypothetical dividing line that can form when member attributes align and divide a group into subgroups. Slides 68–69 call the topic “Faultiness,” but slide 68’s examples use “group faultline”; the correct term is faultline.

The concept was developed as “faultlines” in Lau and Murnighan’s primary article on how member characteristics align into potential subgroups (Lau & Murnighan, 1998).

Source illustration Attribute alignment What makes the alignment strong or weak
Strong faultline Two young female Asian members and two senior male Caucasian members Age, gender, and ethnicity align into two internally similar subgroups
Weak faultline A young female Asian, a young male Caucasian, a senior female Caucasian, and a senior male Asian member The source describes the possible subgroups as diverse rather than split into two homogeneous sets

These are hypothetical examples of attribute alignment, not claims about the abilities, preferences, or behaviour of people in any demographic category. Faultline effects arise from subgroup division and interaction. The lecture associates splits with competition that takes time from core work, slower learning, riskier decisions, lower creativity, greater conflict, and less trust between subgroups.

Overall group satisfaction may be lower even while subgroup satisfaction is high.

Teaching example: reduce a subgroup split by changing the work structure

A four-person team notices that two members always consult one another while the other two are left out of decisions. Rather than infer why from anyone’s identity, the manager can rotate mixed pairs, make information visible to the full team, and give the group a shared task requiring cross-pair coordination.

The aim is to reduce separation and improve trust; it does not assume that mixing people automatically removes every disagreement.

Common mistakes

  • Treating Tuckman’s stages as mandatory, perfectly sequential, or as a score of group effectiveness. Groups can stall, overlap stages, or regress.
  • Calling the deadline midpoint a Tuckman stage. Punctuated equilibrium is a separate model for temporary groups.
  • Confusing role perception (how a person understands a role) with role expectations (what others expect), or calling any busy workload role conflict.
  • Treating norms as formal policies, or assuming that a sanctioned norm is beneficial.
  • Classifying deviance by just one axis. Use both target and seriousness; the categories are not a single mild-to-severe list.
  • Assuming one cause explains deviance or that an observed act diagnoses an individual trait.
  • Assuming status equals expertise, or overlooking useful contributions because the speaker has less status.
  • Treating the lecture’s approximate group sizes as fixed rules, or assuming social loafing occurs equally in every cultural context.
  • Assuming cohesion alone raises output, or that diversity automatically guarantees either conflict or success.
  • Reading the source’s demographic faultline illustration as a prediction about an individual. It demonstrates aligned attributes and hypothetical subgroup structure.
