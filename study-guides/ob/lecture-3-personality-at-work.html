Personality at Work

Compare personality frameworks and see why workplace rules and cues change what traits predict.

A pattern, expressed in a situation

  • Traits describe relatively stable patterns, not an explanation for every isolated action.
  • MBTI uses preference categories. The Big Five describes trait dimensions.
  • Strong rules constrain behaviour; trait-relevant cues can bring particular tendencies into play.

Personality, traits, and development

Personality consists of relatively stable characteristics and traits that help explain behaviour. Independence, conscientiousness, agreeableness, and self-control are examples. A single action is not enough to establish a trait: a characteristic is more useful as a trait description when it appears consistently over time and frequently across diverse situations. Stability is a pattern, not a promise that behaviour never changes.

Heredity influences personality, while development continues throughout life. The lecture says personality is more changeable in adolescence than adulthood. Neither inherited influence nor relative stability means that genes determine conduct or adults cannot change.

The work environment is also relevant to how personality is expressed. Rules, coworkers, incentives, and situational demands can constrain behaviour or make some tendencies more relevant. The lecture develops this person–situation interaction later; it does not provide a separate environmental-development model that would justify assigning a person's traits from their background.

Teaching example: distinguish a trait pattern from one response

A coordinator speaks up in one tense meeting. That event alone does not establish that the person is generally assertive. If the person regularly offers views across different teams and over time, assertiveness may be a useful trait description. Even then, a quiet response in a formal meeting does not disprove the pattern: the meeting's expectations may constrain what is expressed.

Two frameworks for describing personality

The lecture names the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) and the Big Five as dominant frameworks. The MBTI is presented as a 100-question assessment asking how people usually feel or act in situations. It organizes preferences into four pairs. Combining one preference from each pair yields 16 labels.

The 16-code poster illustrates combinations, but its short type descriptions should not be treated as complete portraits of people. The lecture reports that some psychologists find theoretical inconsistencies, including the assumption of a static personality that overlooks development and change. One slide asks “Why the Myers-Briggs test is totally meaningless?” but supplies no argument or evidence beneath the headline.

That headline alone cannot establish the claim.

MBTI assigns preference categories. The Big Five places people along dimensions. A category does not tell you how close someone is to its boundary, and neither framework guarantees job performance.

Additional evidence on MBTI measurement

Supplemental explanation. MBTI categories select one preference from each pair; they do not show how strongly someone leans toward that preference. The Big Five instead describes traits along dimensions.

A 2025 synthesis of MBTI Form M studies found acceptable reliability and validity for scores, while also noting gaps in the evidence base, including a lack of structural-validity studies and limited test–retest evidence. These findings concern Form M and do not turn four-letter categories into fixed identities or work-performance guarantees. 2025 psychometric synthesis; MBTI type and trait measurement overview.

Teaching example: read an MBTI code as a set of preferences

Suppose a questionnaire response yields E, S, T, and J. The four letters mean that the framework has selected one pole from each pair: Extraversion, Sensing, Thinking, and Judging. The result is one of 16 combinations. It does not prove that the employee is always sociable, attends only to details, ignores feelings, or needs every task tightly structured.

Ask about the actual work and observed behaviour rather than treating the letters as a prediction.

The Big Five: dimensions and workplace connections

The Big Five describes personality through five dimensions. The lecture calls these traits useful for understanding organizational behaviour and presents associations with workplace outcomes. The associations are tendencies and framework-level summaries, not universal rules for hiring or predicting an individual. Scores describe positions on dimensions; they should not be read as moral rankings.

The trait labels summarize broader clusters, not single behaviours. Examples in the lecture include extraversion descriptors such as friendliness and excitement-seeking; agreeableness descriptors such as trust and sympathy; conscientiousness descriptors such as orderliness and self-discipline; neuroticism descriptors such as anxiety and vulnerability; and openness descriptors such as imagination and adventurousness.

OCEAN is a memory aid for the five dimensions, not a score or assessment by itself.

The lecture's outcome map uses arrows from each trait to proposed reasons and outcomes. Its performance entries for extraversion and agreeableness carry an asterisk, but the footnote is not visible in the supplied figure; no explanation should be inferred. More broadly, the arrows summarize reported links, not guaranteed causal effects.

The slide says the Big Five have the most verifiable links to important organizational outcomes, while also emphasizing that they are not the only relevant traits.

Teaching example: interpret a Big Five pattern with limits

A project team member scores relatively high on conscientiousness and openness. The lecture's framework suggests that persistence and planning may help with follow-through, while curiosity may support learning and adapting to changes. The score alone cannot establish how the person will perform: task demands, resources, experience, team norms, and the measurement context also matter.

Use it as one prompt for a work-related conversation, not as a verdict about capability.

Other personality frameworks: the Dark Triad

The lecture introduces Machiavellianism, narcissism, and psychopathy as socially undesirable characteristics that can occur to varying degrees and matter for organizational behaviour. These are trait concepts in this discussion, not clinical diagnoses. Behavioural concerns should be addressed through specific evidence and workplace processes, not by diagnosing a colleague.

They also say high-Mach employees “encourage others” more, without defining that phrase. Short-term gains may carry social costs because others may not like the manipulator.
NarcissismGrandiose self-importance, a strong need for admiration, entitlement, and arrogance.The lecture mentions possible charisma and reports associations with leadership, adaptability, and complex business decisions. These are not rules that self-centredness is necessary for success. Trait narcissism is not the same as Narcissistic Personality Disorder.

Measures discussed attempt to assess norm compliance, deceit and its effectiveness, impulsivity, and empathic concern.The lecture portrays workplace risks such as charm, opportunism, manipulation, taking credit for others' work, bullying, and hard influence tactics. These are reported descriptions and associations, not a manager's diagnostic checklist.

The lecture distinguishes predatory bullying, where tormenting a vulnerable person is described as an end in itself, from instrumental bullying, where bullying is used as a means to reach a goal. This describes a distinction between motives; it does not excuse either behaviour. A person can be held accountable for documented conduct without assigning a personality label.

Some of the lecture's illustrations need special caution. Slides about selfies, social-media use, and mental-health effects are popular-media graphics and do not establish diagnoses or causal claims. A later graphic contrasts “psychopath” with “sociopath” and lists career prevalence, but the lecture gives no support for treating sociopath as a distinct diagnosis or for the job rankings.

Do not infer narcissism or psychopathy from phone use, a single conflict, job title, or an informal profile.

Teaching example: describe harmful conduct without diagnosing

A team member repeatedly presents a colleague's work as their own and threatens coworkers who question the credit. A manager can document the incidents, hear from those involved, apply the organization's conduct process, and protect affected staff. The behaviour may resemble risks discussed in the lecture, but it does not allow the manager to diagnose psychopathy or infer a stable personality from an incomplete account.

Personality and situations

Personality's effect on organizational behaviour depends on the situation. Two frameworks explain different parts of that relationship: situation strength describes how much the setting constrains appropriate conduct, while trait activation theory (TAT) describes how particular situations, events, or interventions make some traits more relevant or expressive.

Situation strength

Situation strength is how clearly norms, cues and standards prescribe conduct. Strong situations constrain alternatives; weak situations leave more discretion, so trait differences may predict behaviour better.

More control is not always better. Tight rules can limit initiative and creativity, and their effects depend on the people and work.

The lecture's example is swearing. Attention-seeking, conservatism, and risk tolerance may relate to how likely someone is to swear; casual time with friends permits more than a classroom or meeting with a boss. The same person's tendency is therefore more visible in the weaker setting and constrained in the stronger one.

The slides also report that employees were more likely to behave proactively in line with their values in weaker organizational situations, where there is more room to act.

Source example: compare swearing with friends and in a meeting

Setting A: casually spending time with friends. The norms are relatively permissive, so a person's attention-seeking, conservatism, or risk tolerance may be more visible in whether they swear. Setting B: a classroom or meeting with a boss. Expectations are clearer and the social cost of swearing is higher, so the same person is less likely to express that tendency.

The comparison concerns the behaviour's context; it does not establish a trait from one utterance.

Trait activation theory

Trait activation theory says that some situations, events, or interventions activate one trait more than another. A commission-based compensation plan may make differences in extraversion more relevant because the slide describes extraversion as more reward-sensitive than openness to new experiences.

A study summarized in the lecture found that individual differences in prosocial tendencies mattered more when coworkers were unsupportive: when support was common, most people behaved prosocially; in a less supportive environment, individual differences became more visible.

Situation strength and trait activation are related but answer different questions. Strength concerns how strongly norms and standards constrain behaviour overall. Activation concerns whether a cue is relevant to a particular trait. A cue can activate a trait while the wider setting still limits how it is expressed.

Teaching example: one employee across two work demands

An employee who enjoys social contact may find a commission-heavy sales role especially relevant to extraversion: the incentive can activate differences in approach to social interaction and reward. During a high-stakes compliance check, however, clear procedures and review standards constrain how work is done. The first case illustrates trait activation; the second illustrates situation strength.

Neither setting reveals the employee's whole personality, and neither guarantees a particular outcome.

Common mistakes

  • Treating “stable” as fixed, or heredity as destiny. The lecture explicitly allows development and lifetime change.
  • Inferring a trait from one action instead of looking for consistency across time and varied situations.
  • Treating an MBTI code as a diagnosis, an immutable identity, or a work-performance forecast. The headline-only “meaningless” slide is not evidence either.
  • Reading Big Five workplace links as promises about every person, or interpreting one dimension as morally good or bad.
  • Confusing trait narcissism or psychopathy with a clinical disorder; treating social-media habits, job titles, or one conflict as diagnosis.
  • Assuming workplaces are always strong situations, that strong control is always helpful, or that situation strength and trait activation mean the same thing.
  • Assuming a trait produces identical visible conduct in every context. Norms, incentives, cues, and discretion shape its expression.

Practice questions

1. What makes a characteristic more useful to describe as a personality trait?

Check your answer

A characteristic is more trait-like when it appears consistently over time and frequently across diverse situations. One isolated action is not enough.

2. Name the four MBTI pairs and state one limitation highlighted in the lecture.

Check your answer

Extraversion–Introversion, Sensing–Intuition, Thinking–Feeling, and Judging–Perceiving. The lecture reports that critics see theoretical inconsistencies, including an assumption of static personality that overlooks development. The headline-only slide does not provide evidence for calling the instrument meaningless.

3. A colleague follows a careful checklist in a regulated meeting but improvises during an informal brainstorming session. How can situation strength help explain this difference?

Check your answer

The regulated meeting has clearer standards and stronger constraints, leaving less discretion for traits to predict visible behaviour. The informal brainstorming session is weaker, so individual tendencies may be more visible. This does not mean the person's underlying personality changed between settings.

4. How does a commission plan illustrate trait activation theory?

Check your answer

The incentive can make differences in extraversion more relevant or expressive because the lecture describes extraversion as more reward-sensitive than openness. It is an example of a cue activating a trait, not a guarantee that every extravert will succeed in sales.

5. Why should a manager address documented bullying without diagnosing the person involved?

Check your answer

The Dark Triad concepts describe traits, not a manager's clinical diagnosis. Specific conduct can be documented and addressed through workplace procedures without inferring a disorder or a fixed personality.
