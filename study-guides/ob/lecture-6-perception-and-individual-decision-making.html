Perception and Individual Decision Making

See how attention, attribution and decision shortcuts shape the conclusions people reach.

An interpretation is not the whole evidence

  • Perception selects and interprets information. People can read the same scene differently.
  • Attribution needs three comparisons: other people, other situations and other times.
  • The rational model optimizes under demanding assumptions. Bounded rationality searches for a satisfactory choice under limits.

The situation, the target, and the perceiver each influence perception. They are separate inputs, not steps in a chain. A loud voice may stand out because of a target feature; an urgent meeting may make it more noticeable; and a listener’s expectation may shape what the voice means to them.

Perceiver factors include attitudes, personality, motives, interests, past experiences, and expectations.

Perception shapes behaviour

The lecture defines perception as the process by which people organize and interpret sensory impressions to give meaning to their environment. What someone perceives can differ substantially from objective reality. In organizational behaviour, the perceived world matters because people generally respond to what they think is happening, not to an observer’s account of conditions alone.

The opening visual illustrates this with two people who call the same glass “almost full” or “almost empty,” and a ground marking that appears as a 6 to one viewer and a 9 to another. The shared stimulus is interpreted from different viewpoints; the illustration does not make either observer's reading a fact about the object.

People may form different impressions of similar jobs and working conditions. The lecture also notes that people often evaluate those they see as similar to themselves more positively, and those they see as different more negatively. Similarity is therefore not neutral evidence of competence or fit.

From stimuli to action

The process begins as sensory information arrives and ends in action. The lecture groups selection, organization, and interpretation as the “throughputs” between stimulus input and action output. People receive more information than they can process, so they select some signals and screen out others.

External features such as intensity, size, movement, and repetition, as well as internal qualities such as age, learning, and interests, can affect selection. They then organize selected information to form meaning.

The lecture names three ways people organize information:

Organization mechanisms
MechanismWhat the perceiver doesLecture illustration
GroupingGroups stimuli by similarity or proximity.Workers from the same place may be perceived as similar because they are near one another.
ClosureFills gaps in incomplete information to make it meaningful.Uses past experience, data, or a hunch to supply what is missing.
SimplificationIdentifies main features and simplifies the interpretation.Reduces a complicated stimulus situation to a few salient features.

Closure and simplification help people make sense of complexity, but an interpretation that feels complete may still rest on missing evidence. A hunch can fill a gap; it does not establish that the guess is true.

Teaching example: silence in a meeting. A manager notices that an employee says little (selection). The manager groups this with the employee’s quiet manner, fills in the missing reason as “not interested” (closure), and reduces several observations to the label “unengaged” (simplification). The employee’s silence is observed; disinterest is inferred.

Checking the employee’s written contribution and asking about the meeting can change the interpretation before the manager assigns work.

Perceptual selectivity: what gets attention

Perceptual selectivity is attending to particular stimuli according to their perceived relevance, importance, and personal interests. External properties can make a signal stand out; personal influences help explain why people in the same setting notice different things.

Influences that direct selective attention
Influence groupFactorsHow they can shape attention
External stimulus propertiesSize, intensity, repetition, novelty or familiarity, contrast, motionLarge, loud, repeated, new or familiar, contrasting, and moving stimuli can draw attention.
Personal influencesSelf-concept, beliefs, expectations, inner needs, response disposition, response salience, perceptual defenceWhat a person believes, expects, needs, recognizes, or is disposed to notice can make one interpretation more prominent.

External properties include size, intensity, repetition, novelty or familiarity, contrast, and motion. The slide illustrates how loud sounds and bright lights stand out, repeated messages attract attention, and movement can be more noticeable than stillness. Novelty and familiarity both matter: unusual stimuli attract attention, and familiar stimuli may be readily recognized.

Self-concept, beliefs, expectations and needs influence attention. A manager who expects non-technical staff to lack product knowledge may overlook what their answers actually show.

Familiar stimuli are easier to perceive through response disposition. Response salience reflects cognitive predispositions: marketing, accounting and personnel staff may notice different sides of the same business problem.

Perceptual defence is screening information that creates conflict or feels threatening. It can involve denying the information, distorting it to fit an older view, or accepting it only as an unrepresentative exception. Recognizing the pattern is a reason to look again at the evidence that challenges a current interpretation.

Social perception and attribution

Social perception is gathering and interpreting information about people through appearance, verbal communication, and nonverbal signals. These cues help people judge characteristics, roles, and relationships, but they do not directly reveal another person's motives.

Attribution theory describes how observers decide whether behaviour was caused internally or externally. An internal cause is believed to be under the person's control; an external cause is something the situation is thought to have forced. The lecture's lateness example contrasts oversleeping after partying (internal) with a traffic accident blocking the route (external).

Ask what is being compared before interpreting the cue
CueQuestionHigh cueLow cue
DistinctivenessDoes this person behave differently in other situations?Behaviour differs from this person’s behaviour in other situations; points toward an external attribution.Similar behaviour across situations; points toward an internal attribution.
ConsensusDo other people respond similarly to the same situation?Others respond the same way; points toward an external attribution.Others in the same situation do not; points toward an internal attribution.
ConsistencyDoes this person respond this way over time?Repeated behaviour; the lecture points toward an internal attribution.Not repeated over time; points toward an external attribution.

Keep the comparisons distinct: “other situations” is distinctiveness, “other people” is consensus, and “other times” is consistency. The source gives directional cues, not a deterministic test. Consider the combined pattern and investigate context before deciding what caused a behaviour.

Teaching example: compare two lateness cases

Two employees arrive late today. Case A is normally punctual and is punctual for other kinds of commitments, and several coworkers using the same route are also late after a traffic accident. Case B is late several times a week, is late to other commitments too, and coworkers on the same route arrive on time.

Use the lecture's three questions; do not treat the result as proof of cause.

1. Distinctiveness: compare other situations

Case A: lateness is unusual for this person, so distinctiveness is high and the cue points outward to the situation. Case B: lateness occurs across settings, so distinctiveness is low and the cue points inward to the person.

2. Consensus: compare other people

Case A: coworkers using the route are late too, so consensus is high and the cue points outward. Case B: other commuters arrive on time, so consensus is low and the cue points inward.

3. Consistency: compare other times

Case A: this is not a repeated pattern, so consistency is low and the cue points outward. Case B: lateness repeats several times a week, so consistency is high and the lecture's cue points inward.

4. Combine the cues and check

Case A's high distinctiveness, high consensus, and low consistency point toward a temporary external explanation such as the accident. Case B's low distinctiveness, low consensus, and high consistency point toward an internal explanation. These are provisional attributions: ask what happened and check the relevant circumstances before acting.

Change the attribution evidence

Patterns suggest explanations to investigate. An unknown comparison stays unknown; a cue is not proof of cause.

Shortcuts when judging other people

Shortcuts reduce the effort of interpreting people, but they can replace individual evidence with an impression or reference point. Keep their mechanisms separate.

Recognize the shortcut by the source of the judgment
ShortcutRecognition cueWorkplace example
Selective perceptionSome information is attended to while other information is screened out.Teaching example: a manager notices only behaviour that fits an existing expectation.
Halo effectAn overall impression spills over into judgments of separate attributes.Lecture example: someone seen as attractive or likeable is also rated more favourably on skills or characteristics, regardless of actual performance.
Contrast effectA comparison point makes a person's qualities seem more different than they are.Lecture example: a candidate is seen as better than they are after less suitable applicants, or worse after top-tier applicants.
Social identity effectGroup identification shapes self-definition and attitudes toward in-group and out-group members.Teaching example: a team member feels belonging and self-esteem through group membership while becoming less favourable toward an outside group; this is a possible influence, not a prediction about any member.
StereotypingA generalized group belief is applied to an individual and overlooks personal differences.Teaching example: a hiring panel assumes each applicant from a professional category has the same traits instead of checking individual evidence.

Halo is impression spillover; contrast is a reference-point effect; stereotyping applies a group expectation. Social identity concerns how group membership informs a person's self-definition and intergroup attitudes. These concepts can overlap in practice, but they are not interchangeable explanations.

Problem recognition is perceptual

Decision-making is a response to a problem: a discrepancy between the current state and a desired state that calls for considering alternative courses of action. Whether a discrepancy counts as a problem is itself a perceptual issue. One manager may see a division's 2% quarterly sales decline as urgent; another manager with the same decline may consider it acceptable.

The observed change alone does not settle what response is warranted.

The slides present an organizational schema with data or transaction processing at the base, then operational, tactical, and strategic decision levels. A second figure associates decision structure with management groups and information needs:

Three approaches to individual decisions

Rational decision-making: an ideal sequence

The rational decision-making model assumes the decision-maker has complete information, can identify all relevant options without bias, and chooses the option with the highest utility. Its six steps give a useful order for analysis:

  1. Identify the problem.
  2. Clarify the decision criteria.
  3. Identify alternatives.
  4. Calculate risks and benefits.
  5. Evaluate alternatives.
  6. Select the best option.

Teaching example: choose a delivery supplier

A team needs a supplier whose delivery arrives before a planned launch. It cares about delivery reliability, timing, and total cost. Three suppliers are available. For this teaching example, assume the team can obtain complete, comparable information and evaluate every relevant option fairly; those assumptions are part of the rational model, not facts guaranteed in real decisions.

1. Identify the problem

The current supplier cannot reliably deliver before launch, while the desired state is dependable delivery in time. State the gap before choosing an alternative.

2. Clarify the decision criteria

Use the team's stated criteria: reliability, delivery timing, and total cost. Keep them visible so the most striking feature of one offer does not silently replace them.

3. Identify alternatives

List all relevant available suppliers, including the current arrangement if it remains a genuine option. The ideal model assumes the decision-maker can identify all relevant choices.

4. Calculate risks and benefits

For each supplier, gather comparable evidence about delivery reliability, timing, cost, and relevant risks. The example supplies no numbers, so none should be invented.

5. Evaluate alternatives

Compare every option against the criteria using the same evidence standard. Consider the trade-offs together rather than letting one attractive feature determine the judgment.

6. Select the best option and check

Select the alternative with the highest utility under the criteria and available evidence. Check that the selected option meets the stated launch need and that relevant alternatives were considered. If the information is incomplete, the ideal model's assumptions are not met.

In practice, the lecture says most decisions do not follow this complete optimization sequence. People often look for an acceptable or reasonable solution, and important decisions frequently depend on judgment rather than a defined prescriptive model.

Bounded rationality: a satisfactory choice under limits

Bounded rationality recognizes limits in knowledge, cognitive capacity, time, and resources. People simplify a complicated problem to a level they can manage, then satisfice: they search for something satisfactory and sufficient rather than proving that they found the best possible option. Satisficing is an adaptation to limits, not necessarily carelessness; it also means the chosen option has not been shown to be optimal.

Intuition: fast pattern recognition from experience

The lecture describes intuition as nonconscious decision-making formed through concentrated experience. It works outside conscious thought, links disparate pieces of information holistically, is fast, and is affectively charged. It is not necessarily wrong, does not always conflict with analysis, and is not presented as superstition or a magical sense. It is complex and based on years of experience and learning.

The lecture's practical limit is clear: use an experienced hunch to suggest a hypothesis, not as proof. Test it with objective data and careful analysis. Intuition and analysis can complement each other.

Decision biases: mechanism, cue, and a check

Each bias has a different signature. The checks below are supplemental practice suggestions to help apply the lecture's distinctions. The slides do not establish each check as a tested remedy. The clearest source-based guidance is to test hunches with objective evidence and analysis; the lecture also says a felt need for accuracy is associated with less confirmation bias.

Distinguish the mechanism before choosing a check

OverconfidenceConfidence about one's own or others' ability exceeds what is warranted, often without awareness.A person is sure the plan will work and does not prepare for problems. The lecture reports that people whose intellectual and interpersonal abilities are weakest are most likely to overestimate their own performance and ability, and that entrepreneur optimism is negatively related to new-venture performance; these are slide-reported claims, not rules about an individual.State what evidence supports confidence, identify uncertainties, and prepare a contingency plan. Do not infer incompetence from confidence alone.

The lecture notes that anchors are widely used where persuasion matters and that negotiations involve anchoring.Make an independent estimate before seeing the first proposal, then compare it with later evidence. ConfirmationPeople seek or accept support for past choices or views and discount contradictory information.Teaching example: a decision-maker with strong opinions and confidence in their information chooses sources that echo their view.Ask what evidence would change the conclusion and actively seek it. Overconfidence is not simply being confident; the cue is confidence that outruns evidence, often with limited planning for problems. Anchoring concerns the weight of early information. Confirmation concerns how supporting and conflicting evidence is searched for or evaluated. Availability concerns ease of recall. Escalation concerns continuing despite clear contrary evidence and may be reinforced by responsibility.

Loss aversion concerns unequal emotional weight for equivalent losses and gains. Hindsight appears after the outcome is known.

Common mistakes

  • Treating perception as objective reality, or assuming observed conditions alone explain behaviour.
  • Mixing up the situation, target, and perceiver as if they were stages or interchangeable factors.
  • Confusing selection (what receives attention) with organization (how selected material is grouped, completed, or simplified).
  • Treating closure or a hunch as proof rather than as an interpretation that needs checking.
  • Mixing up distinctiveness (other situations), consensus (other people), and consistency (other times), or treating one cue as decisive.
  • Calling halo, contrast, social identity, stereotyping, and selective perception the same shortcut.
  • Assuming a 2% sales decline automatically is or is not a problem without considering a desired state.
  • Describing the rational model as what people usually do; it depends on demanding assumptions.
  • Calling satisficing careless, or claiming a satisfactory choice has been proven optimal.
  • Treating intuition as either magic or necessarily wrong; it can suggest a hypothesis that evidence should test.
  • Confusing confirmation (evidence sought or discounted) with availability (evidence easy to recall).
  • Calling every persistent project effort escalation, every confident person incompetent, or the slide's “twice” loss comparison a universal rule.
  • Claiming an outcome was predictable after it is known without checking what was recorded beforehand.

Practice questions

1. Two employees observe the same busy customer-service desk. One notices the queue, another notices a colleague struggling with the software. Name three broad influences that could help explain their different perceptions.

Check your answer

The situation (such as time pressure), target features (what stands out in the scene), and perceiver characteristics (such as interests, expectations, or experience) can each influence perception. They are separate inputs, so more detail is needed to identify which mattered most.

2. A supervisor observes an employee arrive late once, but the employee is usually punctual, is punctual for other kinds of commitments, and many people were delayed by the same road accident. Apply distinctiveness, consensus, and consistency.

Check your answer

Distinctiveness is high because this is unusual for the employee across situations. Consensus is high because others faced the same delay. Consistency is low because it is not a repeated pattern. Together these cues point toward an external, temporary explanation, but the supervisor should still check what happened.

3. An interviewer rates a candidate immediately after a very weak candidate and gives extra credit for being personable. Which shortcuts may be operating?

Check your answer

The judgment against the previous applicant suggests contrast effect. Letting likeability shape judgments of separate qualifications suggests halo effect. Check each candidate against the same criteria and evidence.

4. Why can two managers disagree about whether the same 2% sales decline is a problem?

Check your answer

They may compare the observed result with different desired states or reference points. Recognizing a discrepancy that requires action is perceptual; the percentage alone does not settle the decision.

5. A team chooses the first supplier that meets its time and cost limits because there is not time to examine every option. Which decision approach is this, and what should the team avoid claiming?

Check your answer

This illustrates bounded rationality and satisficing: the team selects a satisfactory option within practical limits. It should not claim that the option is proven to be the best possible choice because it did not evaluate every alternative.

6. A manager searches mainly for reports supporting a system they already selected. After a vivid one-off failure, they also conclude the failure is now likely everywhere. Distinguish the biases.

Check your answer

The support-seeking and discounting of contradictory reports is confirmation bias. Treating an easily recalled vivid event as evidence of broad likelihood is availability bias. One concerns information search and evaluation; the other concerns ease of recall.

7. A project lead continues a project despite clear evidence it is failing and says after cancellation that the outcome was always obvious. Identify the two possible biases and an evidence check.

Check your answer

Continuing despite clear contrary evidence can be escalation of commitment, especially if the lead feels responsible. Claiming the failure was predictable only after it happened suggests hindsight bias. Review earlier forecasts, decision records, and the evidence available at each point; continuation alone does not prove escalation if new benefits justified it.

8. What role should intuition play in an important decision?

Check your answer

An experience-based hunch can suggest a hypothesis. It is not proof: test it with objective data and analysis. Intuition can complement rational analysis rather than replacing or always contradicting it.
