Emotions and Moods

Explain how emotions and moods shape workplace judgment, emotional labour, and responses to daily work events.

Big picture

Work is emotional as well as cognitive. People notice events through their current feelings, express emotions under workplace expectations, and carry the effects of daily events into satisfaction and performance. Understanding the distinctions among emotion, feeling, mood, and affect helps explain these reactions without treating emotion as the opposite of rational thought. The lecture then connects workplace events to emotional labour, affective events theory, emotional intelligence, and ways individuals and organizations can manage emotional demands.

Emotion, feeling, mood, and affect

An emotion is an intense response directed at someone or something. The lecture describes it biologically as chemicals released after a person interprets a specific trigger. It presents a quarter-second to identify a trigger, another quarter-second to produce chemicals, and about six seconds for those chemicals to last. Treat these timings as the lecturer’s account, not as universal clocks for every emotion. The lecture also describes the chemicals as moving through the body and forming feedback with the brain.

A feeling is the experience of sensing and thinking about an emotion as it is integrated. Feelings are more cognitively saturated, may draw on a mix of emotions, and last longer than the initial emotion. A mood is generally less intense, can lack a clear trigger, and may last hours or days. The lecture groups mood influences into the environment, physiology, and mental state. Affect is the broad term for the range of feelings that includes emotions and moods.

Slide 13 describes a sequence in which emotions come first, feelings follow as the emotion is processed, and moods develop from combinations of feelings. The slide 14 figure separately places emotions and moods under affect and shows a two-way relationship between emotion and mood. Together, these ideas help describe common patterns without implying that every mood comes from one discrete event.

Teaching example: separate an event reaction from the mood that follows

A colleague criticizes your report in a meeting. The criticism may trigger brief anger directed at that colleague or the feedback. As you think about the comment, you may feel embarrassed or disappointed as well as angry. If several difficult interactions continue through the afternoon, you may notice a broader low mood without identifying one single cause. Affect is the umbrella for the emotional and mood experience. These labels describe different aspects of the same day, not four interchangeable words.

Positive and negative affect are separate mood dimensions

The lecture describes positive affect and negative affect as two dimensions of mood. High positive affect includes excitement, enthusiasm, and elation; low positive affect includes boredom, depression, and fatigue. High negative affect includes nervousness, stress, and anxiety; low negative affect includes contentedness, calmness, and serenity. Each dimension has its own high and low end. A person can therefore be low in negative affect without being highly excited, or high in positive affect while also feeling nervous about a demanding event.

The slides also raise cross-cultural questions and show body maps for 13 emotions. Their caption says the mapped patterns were nearly identical across cultures and languages. Because the slide gives no full study citation, retain this as a claim made by the visual rather than a universal conclusion about how every person experiences emotion.

The lecture first describes positive emotions such as joy and gratitude as favourable evaluations, and negative emotions such as anger and guilt as unfavourable evaluations. It then connects the broad categories with positive and negative mood states. The separate affect dimensions below clarify that a person may vary in either dimension.

Teaching example: locate two feelings on separate affect dimensions

A presenter feels enthusiastic about sharing a new proposal and nervous about questions from senior managers. Enthusiasm suggests high positive affect, while nervousness suggests high negative affect. The two can coexist because the dimensions are separate. Calling one feeling “positive” does not erase the other.

Emotions contribute to rational thought and ethical judgment

The lecture challenges the idea that emotion automatically makes a person irrational. It reports that brain-injury studies suggest the capacity to experience emotion supports rationality because emotion provides context for understanding the world. It also describes a study in which people in a negative mood recognized truthful information better than people in a happy mood. This is an example reported by the lecture, not a rule that negative moods always improve judgment.

The practical point is to notice how emotions and moods may be affecting a decision instead of automatically dismissing emotional responses as invalid. Emotions also inform ethical judgment. The lecture names sympathy for another person’s suffering, guilt about one’s own immoral behaviour, anger at injustice, contempt for unethical conduct, and disgust at violations of moral norms. It says moral judgments are often feeling based, while fairness assessments retain a cognitive component. People may experience their moral boundaries as logical even when feelings contribute to them.

Teaching example: use an emotion as information during an ethical decision

A supervisor learns that a high-performing employee took credit for a teammate’s work. Anger at the unfairness may signal that an ethical boundary matters here. The supervisor can acknowledge that reaction, check what happened, hear from the people involved, and apply the same standards used in comparable cases. The feeling is relevant context, but it does not replace evidence or a fair process.

What influences emotions and moods?

The lecture separates influences into who a person is and the circumstances at the time. Personality contributes to tendencies to experience some moods or emotions more often. Affect intensity describes how strongly a person experiences both positive and negative emotions. These differences can shape reactions, but they do not let a manager predict an individual’s state from a category or demographic label.

The lecture’s glass-half-full/glass-half-empty illustration connects personality to a more optimistic or pessimistic way of interpreting circumstances. It is an illustration of a tendency in perspective, not a rule for classifying a person from one comment. Affectively intense people may experience both positive and negative emotions more deeply, so a happy period can feel especially happy and a sad period especially sad.

The slides report findings about age and gender. One study covering ages 18 to 94 found that negative emotions appeared less with age, highly positive moods lasted longer among older participants, and bad moods faded faster. The gender slide reports greater emotional expressiveness and intensity among women, longer retention, and more frequent displays of positive and negative emotions except anger. These are findings as presented by the lecture. They should not be used as rules for judging an individual employee.

Circumstances include time of day, day of week, weather, work stress, social activity, sleep, exercise, and blood sugar. The lecture reports higher positive affect in evenings and lower levels early in the morning, with negative affect highest overnight and lowest later in the morning. It reports that U.S. adults’ positive affect tends to peak Friday through Sunday and dip on Monday, with similar patterns in several other cultures. It says weather has little effect for most people. Stressful work events such as a hostile email, impending deadline, lost sale, or reprimand can lower mood, and accumulated stress can worsen it. Physical, informal, and shared-meal activities are described as more strongly associated with increases in positive mood than formal meetings or sedentary activities. Poor sleep can increase fatigue, irritability, and reduced alertness, and one study linked poor sleep to lower job satisfaction. The lecture also names disease, injury, and depression among health risks associated with increased fatigue. Exercise is associated with improved positive mood, though the lecture qualifies the overall effect as not strong and strongest among people who are depressed. The slides associate low blood sugar with irritability and combative behaviour, and link excessively high blood sugar, especially in untreated diabetes, with depression.

Teaching example: distinguish a person factor from a situational factor

Two analysts receive the same last-minute deadline change. One may experience a stronger reaction because of affect intensity, a person-level tendency. Both may also be tired after poor sleep, a situational or physiological condition at the time. The deadline is a work stressor that can affect mood, and repeated stressors may accumulate. The example does not establish a diagnosis or predict how either analyst will respond.

Emotional labour: what is felt and what is displayed?

Emotional labour is the expression of organizationally desired emotions during interpersonal transactions at work. The lecture calls it an important part of effective job performance. Felt emotions are what an employee actually experiences. Displayed emotions are the expressions the organization expects and considers appropriate for a job. Display rules are learned, and the required display may differ from what the person feels.

Surface acting changes the outward display while hiding or suppressing the inner feeling. Deep acting tries to change the felt emotion to align it with the display rule. Emotional dissonance is the disparity that occurs when someone projects one emotion while feeling another. Dissonance names the mismatch; surface and deep acting name different ways of responding to display expectations.

Teaching example: customer service and the two acting strategies

A customer insults a service worker. The worker feels anger, while the job’s display rule calls for a courteous response. If the worker hides the anger and smiles without changing the feeling, that is surface acting. If the worker reframes the situation and tries to understand why the customer is upset, the worker is attempting deep acting by changing the felt emotion. Projecting courtesy while still feeling anger creates emotional dissonance. The example shows possible responses, not a requirement that workers accept mistreatment.

Affective Events Theory links the workday to outcomes

Affective Events Theory (AET) explains how employees react emotionally to events at work and how those reactions influence job satisfaction and job performance. The lecture’s figure starts with the work environment, including job characteristics, demands, and emotional-labour requirements. These conditions shape daily hassles and uplifts. Events lead to positive or negative emotional reactions, which connect to job satisfaction and performance. Personality and mood shape the event-to-reaction relationship. The next slide notes that people low in emotional stability tend to react more strongly to negative events and that the same event can prompt different reactions depending on mood. The lecture also names organizational citizenship behaviour, organizational commitment, effort, intention to quit, and workplace deviance among the outcomes emotions may influence.

Teaching example: trace a missed deadline through AET

A job with frequent urgent requests creates a work environment in which missed deadlines and late changes can occur. A manager’s late request becomes a work event. One employee may feel frustration, while another may feel relief if the change removes a difficult task. Their current mood and personal tendencies can shape those reactions. The reactions may affect satisfaction, effort, performance, or willingness to help coworkers. AET explains the relationships to consider; it does not claim that every missed deadline produces the same response.

Emotional intelligence and related models

The lecture introduces Howard Gardner’s multiple-intelligences theory as a challenge to judging intellect through a single IQ test alone. The slide lists linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, intrapersonal, interpersonal, and naturalist intelligences. This is a distinct framework and is not another name for emotional intelligence.

The slides credit Peter Salovey and John Mayer with coining emotional intelligence (EI) in 1990. They describe EI as an ability, skill, or competency rather than a personality trait. In the workplace, EI means being able to recognize, understand, and manage one’s own emotions and the emotions of others. The lecture connects it with teamwork, communication, leadership, organizational culture, performance, conflict resolution, satisfaction, and productivity. These are the connections stated in the slides, not guaranteed effects for every person or organization.

One slide gives three abilities: perceive emotions in oneself and others, understand what the emotions mean, and regulate one’s emotions. Its cascading figure also pairs conscientiousness with perceiving emotions, cognitive ability with understanding their meaning, and emotional stability with regulating emotions. Preserve that as the lecture’s depicted mapping rather than a universal causal rule.

The same slide presents Goleman’s model in a separate two-by-two arrangement. Recognition versus regulation crosses with self versus social focus. The resulting domains are self-awareness, social awareness, self-management, and relationship management. The matrix gives examples such as emotional self-awareness and accurate self-assessment; empathy and organizational awareness; self-control, adaptability, and initiative; and influence, conflict management, building bonds, and teamwork.

Teaching example: perceive, interpret, and regulate with uncertainty

A usually talkative teammate becomes quiet after a project meeting. First, notice the change without deciding what it means. The teammate might be tired, concerned about a decision, focused on another task, or simply quieter that day. Ask a respectful open question and listen before settling on an interpretation. Then choose a response, such as clarifying the task or offering time to talk. The sequence uses perception, understanding, and regulation while recognizing that an observer cannot know another person’s emotion from silence alone.

Personal and organizational ways to manage emotions

The lecture lists personal strategies that can help people notice, process, or respond to emotions. These include mindfulness meditation, cognitive reappraisal, journaling, breathing techniques, physical activity, social support, setting boundaries, self-compassion, problem solving, and seeking professional help when emotion management remains difficult. The slides present them as strategies, not guaranteed outcomes or clinical treatments.

Organizations can support emotional intelligence through training and workshops, coaching and mentoring, feedback, open communication, mindfulness and stress-management practices, team building, leadership development, diversity and inclusion initiatives, conflict-resolution training, and regular assessments. These are recommendations from the lecture. Their value depends on how they are implemented and on the workplace conditions employees face.

The lecture closes by describing emotionally and socially intelligent people as better able to understand and express themselves, relate to others, and cope with daily demands. It highlights managing emotions alongside optimism, positivity, and self-motivation as part of that coping.

Teaching example: choose a response at the individual and team levels

An employee feels increasingly tense because urgent requests keep arriving without clear priorities. The employee might write down the main trigger, use a breathing pause before replying, and discuss workload boundaries with a manager. At the team level, the manager could invite open discussion, provide coaching or feedback, and clarify priorities. The personal practices may help the employee reflect, while organizational changes address part of the work condition that contributes to the strain.

Common mistakes

  • Treating emotion, feeling, mood, and affect as synonyms, or treating affect as a later time stage rather than an umbrella term.
  • Putting positive and negative affect on one good-to-bad line. The lecture presents them as separate dimensions.
  • Assuming any emotional response makes judgment irrational, or that a negative mood always improves judgment.
  • Turning slide-level age, gender, weekly-pattern, or blood-sugar findings into predictions about an individual.
  • Confusing felt emotion with displayed emotion, or surface acting with deep acting.
  • Treating emotional dissonance as one of the acting strategies rather than the mismatch between felt and projected emotion.
  • Flattening AET into “events cause performance” and omitting emotional reactions or the role of personality and mood.
  • Equating Gardner’s multiple intelligences with EI, or merging the EI cascade with Goleman’s four-domain matrix.
  • Assuming a personal or organizational strategy will work the same way for everyone or under every working condition.

Exam focus

  • Distinguish emotion, feeling, mood, and affect by target, trigger, intensity, duration, and scope. Attribute the lecture’s precise timing claims rather than treating them as universal constants.
  • Explain positive and negative affect as separate dimensions and give examples from their high and low ends.
  • Explain how emotion can contribute to rational thought and ethical judgment while still requiring evidence and fair reasoning.
  • Group emotion and mood influences into person factors and circumstances. State reported age and gender patterns cautiously.
  • Define emotional labour, felt emotion, displayed emotion, display rules, surface acting, deep acting, and emotional dissonance.
  • Trace the AET relationship from work environment through daily events and emotional reactions to satisfaction, performance, and other outcomes. Explain how personality and mood shape reactions.
  • Define EI through perceiving, understanding, and regulating emotions. Distinguish the cascading figure, Goleman’s matrix, and Gardner’s multiple-intelligences list.
  • Name personal and organizational regulation strategies and explain that the lecture presents them as options rather than guaranteed results.

Practice questions

1. A brief anger response follows a customer complaint, then a more general low mood lasts into the evening. Identify the concepts and explain how affect relates to them.

Check your answer

The brief anger directed at the complaint is an emotion. The broader, longer-lasting state is a mood. Affect is the umbrella term that includes both. A feeling may describe the person’s more considered, cognitively integrated experience of the initial emotion.

2. Can a person show high positive affect and high negative affect at the same time? Explain.

Check your answer

Yes. Positive and negative affect are separate mood dimensions. Someone can feel enthusiastic about a presentation and nervous about the questions that may follow.

3. A worker smiles at a customer while feeling angry, without trying to change the anger. Identify the display rule, acting strategy, and possible mismatch.

Check your answer

The courteous smile follows a display rule. Hiding anger while changing only the outward display is surface acting. The difference between projected courtesy and felt anger is emotional dissonance.

4. In AET, what mediates the connection between a work event and work outcomes, and what shapes the employee’s reaction?

Check your answer

The employee’s positive or negative emotional reaction connects the event to outcomes such as satisfaction and performance. Personality and mood shape the event-to-reaction relationship.

5. A teammate is unusually quiet. How can someone use the EI abilities without assuming they know the teammate’s emotion?

Check your answer

Notice the change, consider several possible explanations, ask a respectful question, listen, and regulate one’s own response before acting. Silence alone does not establish what the teammate feels.

6. What distinguishes Gardner’s multiple-intelligences framework from EI, and how do the two EI figures differ?

Check your answer

Gardner’s framework lists different forms of intelligence and is not a synonym for EI. The lecture’s EI cascade presents perceiving, understanding, and regulating emotions, with a separate depicted factor mapping. Goleman’s matrix organizes four domains across self/social focus and recognition/regulation.

7. Name one personal strategy and one organizational strategy from the lecture. Why should neither be presented as a guaranteed fix?

Check your answer

Personal examples include journaling or cognitive reappraisal. Organizational examples include coaching, open communication, or conflict-resolution training. The slides recommend these approaches but do not establish that they produce the same result for every person or workplace.
