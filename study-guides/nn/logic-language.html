Fuzzy propositions, linguistic variables and implications

How graded propositions, implication relations, linguistic variables and hedges connect fuzzy words to rule reasoning.

Big picture

Classical logic forces a proposition to be true or false. Fuzzy logic lets a linguistic statement such as “the temperature is hot” have a grade between zero and one. That grade becomes the input to fuzzy connectives and to a rule relation. A rule’s implication formula then determines how the antecedent and consequent grades are connected; composing an observed input with that relation produces an inferred fuzzy output. Linguistic variables and hedges provide the words that make those rules readable.

For the short binary-logic prerequisite, see classical logic and crisp rule relations. This page focuses on fuzzy semantics, implication operators, the source calculations, and linguistic terms.

T(P~)=μA~(x),0≤μA~(x)≤1

From a crisp boundary to graded truth

On the source universe X=[0,50] °C, “hot” is crisp at 30 °C: below the threshold its characteristic grade is 0; from 30 °C upward it is 1. The fuzzy version lets membership rise linearly between 20 °C and 30 °C, avoiding a sudden change at one boundary. A membership grade is a degree of compatibility with the word “hot,” not a probability that a temperature is hot.

χHot(x)={0,x<30∘C1,x≥30∘C
μHot(x)={0,x≤20∘Cx−2010,20∘C<x<30∘C1,x≥30∘C
Compare the source threshold at 29.9 °C and a teaching check at 25 °C

At 29.9 °C, χHot(29.9)=0 because 29.9<30; μHot(29.9)=(29.9−20)/10=0.99. At 30 °C both are 1. Teaching check: at 25 °C, χHot(25)=0 while μHot(25)=(25−20)/10=0.5. Each value follows directly from the stated piecewise definition.

Fuzzy propositions and connectives

A fuzzy proposition is a statement whose boundary is not sharp; if P̃ is assigned to fuzzy set Ã, its truth grade at x is Ã’s membership grade. An atomic fuzzy proposition has the form “x is A,” where x is a linguistic variable and A is one of its terms. A compound fuzzy proposition combines atomic propositions with AND, OR, or NOT.

The lecture’s standard choices are NOT = complement, OR = maximum, and AND = minimum. They are useful defaults, not the only possible fuzzy operators: more general complements c, S-norms s, and T-norms t can be selected. State the choices before evaluating a compound proposition.

T(¬P~)=1−a,T(P~∨Q~)=max(a,b),T(P~∧Q~)=min(a,b)

The kuppi note contrasts two situations. When fuzzy sets share one universe, union or intersection is computed pointwise and produces another fuzzy set on that universe. If the propositions describe different variables, such as temperature on U and speed on V, their combined rule is a fuzzy relation on U×V. For an OR-style relation its grade may be written s(μA(x), μB(y)); for an AND-style relation use the chosen T-norm.

Implication is a selected relation operator

In binary material implication, P⇒Q is false only when P is true and Q is false; equivalently it is ¬P∨Q. The kuppi truth-table check compares this form with (P∧Q)∨¬P, which yields the same implication column. These two classical forms motivate different fuzzy extensions. The converse implication rule is not the one implicit in ordinary composition; choose and name the desired relation operator.

A global material-implication interpretation can assign a high relation grade when a is low: a weak antecedent does not violate a conditional, just as a false antecedent makes a classical implication true. Mamdani minimum and product are local rule contributions: the lesson views the rule as active only where the antecedent applies, using the local convention P→Q ≡ P∧Q. The relation value is therefore bounded by the antecedent. This engineering convention is not classical material implication. A fuzzy ELSE rule uses both branches:

μR(x,y)=max(min(a,b),min(1−a,c))

Here the first branch contributes when the antecedent grade is a, while the ELSE branch contributes when its complement 1−a is high. For the complete rule firing, aggregation, and defuzzification pipelines, continue to Mamdani, Sugeno, Tsukamoto, and ANFIS.

Relational composition: infer a fuzzy output

An input fuzzy set A′ on X and a rule relation R on X×Y combine to produce an output fuzzy set B′ on Y. This is the compositional rule of inference. The lesson gives three alternatives; the operator changes how each input grade contributes to an output grade.

B′=A′∘R
μB′(y)=maxx∈Xmin(μA′(x),μR(x,y))(max–min)
μB′(y)=maxx∈X[μA′(x)μR(x,y)](max–product)
μB′(y)=min(1,∑x∈XμA′(x)μR(x,y))(bounded sum–product)

One input, one rule relation, three composition operators

Teaching example — added input: A′ on U={1,2,3,4} has grades (0, 0.8, 0.4, 0.2). Rule relation: use the source large→small Mamdani-minimum relation QMM on rows x=1…4 and columns y=1…3: [[0,0,0],[0.1,0.1,0.1],[0.5,0.5,0.1],[1,0.5,0.1]]. Outputs are reported in y=1,2,3 order.

Max–min: clip each relation entry, then take the maximum

Rule: μB′(y)=maxxmin(μA′(x),μR(x,y)). μB′(1)=max(min(0,0),min(0.8,0.1),min(0.4,0.5),min(0.2,1))=max(0,0.1,0.4,0.2)=0.4; μB′(2)=max(min(0,0),min(0.8,0.1),min(0.4,0.5),min(0.2,0.5))=max(0,0.1,0.4,0.2)=0.4; μB′(3)=max(min(0,0),min(0.8,0.1),min(0.4,0.1),min(0.2,0.1))=max(0,0.1,0.1,0.1)=0.1. Output: (0.4,0.4,0.1). The maximum selects the strongest clipped contribution for each output value.

Max–product: multiply each pair, then take the maximum

Rule: μB′(y)=maxx[μA′(x)μR(x,y)]. μB′(1)=max(0·0,0.8·0.1,0.4·0.5,0.2·1)=max(0,0.08,0.20,0.20)=0.20; μB′(2)=max(0,0.08,0.20,0.10)=0.20; μB′(3)=max(0,0.08,0.04,0.02)=0.08. Output: (0.20,0.20,0.08). Multiplication keeps each antecedent grade proportional to its relation grade.

Bounded sum–product: add all products and cap at one

Rule: μB′(y)=min(1,∑xμA′(x)μR(x,y)). μB′(1)=min(1,0+0.08+0.20+0.20)=0.48; μB′(2)=min(1,0+0.08+0.20+0.10)=0.38; μB′(3)=min(1,0+0.08+0.04+0.02)=0.14. Output: (0.48,0.38,0.14). The cap guarantees every output grade remains in [0,1].

Check axes, ranges, and why the outputs differ

The input fuzzy set is on U={1,2,3,4} and all three inferred outputs are on V={1,2,3}. Each result lies in [0,1]. The same relation and input produce three different output sets because the aggregation rule differs: max–min clips, max–product scales, and bounded sum–product accumulates contributions. These are relation-composition calculations, not a complete controller pipeline.

Composition is generally many-to-one: minimum and maximum discard information, so composing the original antecedent with a fuzzy relation need not recover the exact consequent.

For max–min and max–product composition on compatible finite universes, composition is associative, is monotone, distributes over pointwise max-union, and reverses order when taking inverse relations: (R∘S)⁻¹=S⁻¹∘R⁻¹. Here inverse relation means transpose, rather than a matrix inverse.

Bounded sum–product is monotone and has that transpose identity, but is not generally associative and does not generally distribute over max-union. Its intermediate cap at 1 can discard a different amount depending on grouping. Keep the chosen operators attached to any composition property. For foundational relation matrices and their composition procedures, see relations and matrix composition; this page uses a relation specifically to compare implication meanings.

The following associativity and max-union identities use max–min or max–product composition.

(R∘S)∘T=R∘(S∘T),R∘(S∪T)=(R∘S)∪(R∘T),S⊆T⇒R∘S⊆R∘T
(R∘S)−1=S−1∘R−1

Composing that intermediate matrix with C leaves just one nonzero output cell:

(A∘B)∘C=[0000.625]

Now group B with C first. Its first row and first column are zero. The remaining cell reaches the cap before A is applied:

(B∘C)22=min(1,0.5·0.5+1·1)=min(1,1.25)=1
B∘C=[0001]

A∘(B∘C)=[0000.5]

All other final cells are zero in both groupings. Since 0.625 differs from 0.5, bounded sum–product is not associative. In the second grouping, the intermediate cap discards 0.25 before multiplication by the 0.5 entry of A. This counterexample concerns associativity; it does not change the max–min and max–product identities above.

Linguistic variables: words with quantitative meaning

A linguistic variable takes words or phrases as values. Its four-tuple is (X,T,U,M): X is the variable name; T is its set of linguistic terms; U is the quantitative universe; and M maps every term in T to a fuzzy set on U. For Age, the lecture uses X=Age, T={young, old}, and U=[0,100] years. M assigns the two source membership functions below.

μyoung(y)={1,y≤25[1+(y−255)2]−1,y>25y∈[0,100]

A primary term such as small is a fuzzy set. A hedge modifies its membership grade, leaving the universe elements unchanged. Concentration moves partial grades downward; dilation moves them upward. The lecture adds plus, minus, and intensification to the primary very and more-or-less operations.

Precedence for compound linguistic expressions

Use parentheses first. Otherwise hedges and NOT bind before AND; AND binds before OR. Nested hedges evaluate from the inside out (right to left in the written modifier sequence). The lesson’s expression “plus very minus small” therefore means plus(very(minus(small))).

Common mistakes

  • Treating a membership grade as a probability. It describes compatibility with a fuzzy term in the chosen model.
  • Applying pointwise max/min to propositions on different universes. A rule between different variables is a relation on their Cartesian product.
  • Treating fuzzy implication as one universal formula. Name the global or local operator and state its formula.
  • Using a global implication as though it were a local rule contribution. Global relations can be high when the antecedent grade is low; Mamdani min/product are bounded by it.
  • Reading inclusive OR as XOR, or material implication as a∨b. Binary implication uses ¬a∨b.
  • Swapping relation axes. The first index is the antecedent x; the second is consequent y.
  • Applying nested hedges left-to-right, or interpreting “very very” as μ² rather than μ⁴.
  • Assigning an old-age membership outside the interval supplied for its formula.

Exam focus

  • State the selected fuzzy NOT, OR, and AND operators: 1−a, max(a,b), and min(a,b).
  • Recall the four-tuple (X,T,U,M), with M mapping each linguistic term to a fuzzy set on U.
  • Distinguish global QD, QL, QZ, QG from local QMM and QMP by formula and behavior at a=0.
  • Preserve the pointwise ordering QZ≤QD≤QL; Gödel is not included in that lemma.
  • For composition, keep x on the input axis and y on the output axis; distinguish max–min, max–product, and capped sum–product.
  • Apply every hedge to grades, preserve the source exponent, and evaluate nested modifiers inside out before AND and OR.
  • Use the age function only on its stated domain, especially μold for 50≤y≤100.

Lesson equation (5.6) writes max(a,b). It omits the antecedent complement 1−a. For Dienes–Rescher, material implication is max(1−a,b); the source’s later Dienes–Rescher and Zadeh formulas explicitly include 1−a.
