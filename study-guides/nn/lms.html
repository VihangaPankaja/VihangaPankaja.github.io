A sample gives an estimate of the gradient

  • Model: append 1 to the input so weights and bias fit into one dot product.
  • Loss here: e² has derivative −2ez, so the update adds 2αez.
  • Geometry: R determines curvature. A singular R prevents an ordinary inverse; it does not remove the least-squares minimum.

Expand the loss into a quadratic

Expand the square into three data statistics: c is the target second moment, h the target/input cross-correlation, and R the input correlation matrix.

c=E[t2],𝐡=E[t𝐳],𝐑=E[𝐳𝐳T]
F(𝐱)=E[(t−𝐱T𝐳)2]=c−2𝐱T𝐡+𝐱T𝐑𝐱

R averages products of input coordinates; h averages each input coordinate times its target. Include the appended bias coordinate in both.

From squared error to the Widrow–Hoff update

Givens: linear output a=xTz; target t; error e=t−a; objective F(x)=E[e2] with no one-half. The adjustable vector includes the bias, and the augmented input ends in 1.

1. Start with the expected squared error

For an augmented parameter vector x and input z, the linear output is a=xTz. The target residual is e=t−a. The handout minimizes the unhalved objective F(x)=E[e2].

Why this matters: differentiating the unhalved square leaves a factor of 2 in the update.

2. Expand the square before taking expectation

Rule: (t−xTz)2=t2−2txTz+(xTz)2.

Substitute into the objective: F(x)=E[t2−2txTz+xTzzTx].

Since the parameter vector is fixed while taking expectation, it can be factored outside: F(x)=E[t2]−2xTE[tz]+xTE[zzT]x.

3. Name the data statistics

Set c=E[t2], h=E[tz], and R=E[zzT]. The cross-correlation vector h connects each augmented input coordinate to its target. The correlation matrix R records pairwise products of augmented input coordinates.

Result: F(x)=c−2xTh+xTRx.

4. Read the gradient and curvature

Match the quadratic convention F(x)=½xTAx+dTx+c with A=2R and d=−2h.

Because R is symmetric, ∇F(x)=2Rx−2h and the Hessian is ∇2F(x)=2R.

Check: vTRv=E[(vTz)2]≥0 for every vector v, so R is positive semidefinite and the objective has no negative-curvature direction.

5. Find the stationary parameters when the inverse exists

Set the gradient to zero: 2Rx−2h=0. If R is invertible, x*=R⁻¹h. Positive definiteness gives a unique minimizer.

If R is singular, do not write R⁻¹h. For a genuine squared-error objective, a minimizer still exists because h lies in the range of R; multiple parameter vectors can give the same minimum.

Why the range claim holds: if v is in the nullspace of R, then E[(vTz)2]=0, so vTz=0 almost surely and vTh=E[t(vTz)]=0.

Thus h is orthogonal to the nullspace, which for symmetric R is its range.

6. Replace the expected gradient with one sample

At iteration k, use the instantaneous squared error e2(k) as a gradient estimate.

Since ∂e/∂wj=−pj and ∂e/∂b=−1, the gradient coordinates are −2e(k)pj(k) and −2e(k).

Together, ∇e2(k)=−2e(k)z(k).

Steepest descent subtracts this gradient, giving x(k+1)=x(k)+2αke(k)z(k). The positive sign follows from the target-minus-output error definition.

Quadratic geometry and the stationary solution

A symmetric quadratic has gradient Ax+d and Hessian A. Here A=2R and d=−2h, so the curvature is twice the input correlation matrix.

For any direction v, vTRv=E[(vTz)2]≥0; the surface is convex, though it may be flat along some directions.

F(𝐱)=12𝐱T𝐀𝐱+𝐝T𝐱+c,𝐀=2𝐑,𝐝=−2𝐡
∇F(𝐱)=2𝐑𝐱−2𝐡,∇2F(𝐱)=2𝐑

Positive-definite R gives a unique minimum. Singular R has no ordinary inverse, but a finite least-squares problem still has a minimum; some parameter directions are unconstrained.

𝐑𝐱=𝐡,𝐱*=𝐑−1𝐡only when 𝐑 is invertible

From steepest descent to the LMS sample update

Exact steepest descent uses the expected gradient. LMS substitutes the current sample gradient. For e=t−a and loss e², the gradient is −2ez. Subtracting this gradient adds 2αez:

∇e2(k)=−2e(k)𝐳(k),𝐱(k+1)=𝐱(k)+2αke(k)𝐳(k)
𝐰(k+1)=𝐰(k)+2αke(k)𝐩(k),b(k+1)=b(k)+2αke(k)

The plus sign comes from e=target−output. The factor 2 comes from the unhalved square.

Factor two and learning-rate bounds

Equivalent step sizes satisfy η=2α. The bounds concern the stated quadratic/mean dynamics, not a guaranteed error drop after every sample.
Keep togetherLesson 3Widrow–Hoff reading
Loss½e²e²
Weight correctionηep2αep
Bias correctionηe2αe
Rate bound using λmax(R)0 < η < 2/λmax0 < α < 1/λmax

These conventions point in the same direction but use different step scales. Keep each loss, update and rate bound together.

Lesson 3: E=12e2,Δ𝐰=ηe𝐩;Widrow–Hoff: F=e2,Δ𝐰=2αe𝐩

For the exact expected quadratic objective, deterministic steepest descent with this parameterization converges for a fixed positive step below the reciprocal of the largest eigenvalue of R.

Equivalently, the general Hessian bound is α<2/λmax(A) and A=2R.

This is a bound for the expected quadratic gradient dynamics; stochastic per-pattern updates do not guarantee monotone error after every sample, and practical behavior also depends on the input sequence and assumptions.

0<α<2λmax(𝐀)=1λmax(𝐑)

Lesson 3 presents the equivalent no-factor-two update bound 0<η<2/λmax(R) and the factor-two version 0<α<1/λmax(R). These are paired expressions, not interchangeable constants.

Its separate input-count rule is an engineering heuristic and belongs with the ADALINE learning-rate example.

Singularity and rate check: row 4 equals row 1, so R has no ordinary inverse. Each augmented vector has squared norm 4 and their dot product is 2.

The two nonzero eigenvalues match those of the small sample Gram matrix ½[[4,2],[2,4]], giving 3 and 1; the remaining two eigenvalues of this 4×4 R are zero.

Thus λmax(R)=3. The factor-two LMS condition gives 0<α<1/3, which contains the added teaching value α=0.1.

This checks the expected quadratic bound, not an error decrease after every online sample.

Several output neurons

With multiple outputs, each neuron has its own residual and bias. All share the same input vector. With one neuron per row of W, an outer product updates the rows together.

Quadratic definitions and theorem checks

The remaining theorem checks explain uniqueness and flat directions. Use them after understanding the sample update above.

How eigenvalues classify a symmetric quadratic

A stationary point of a symmetric quadratic solves Ax=−d. When the Hessian is singular, this requires the linear term to lie in its range. If that condition fails, there is no stationary point, even when the Hessian has both positive and negative eigenvalues.

Hessian eigenvalues Shape at a stationary point If the zero-curvature case has no stationary point
All positive One strict (strong) minimum —
All negative One strict (strong) maximum —
Positive and negative, no zero eigenvalue One saddle point —
Positive, negative and zero eigenvalues Saddle stationary points if the stationary equation is consistent; they may be non-unique No stationary point when the linear term is outside the Hessian's range
All nonnegative, at least one zero Weak minimum No stationary point is possible
All nonpositive, at least one zero Weak maximum No stationary point is possible

Teaching check: F(x)=(x12−x22)/2+x3 has Hessian eigenvalues 1, −1 and 0.

Its gradient is ∇F=(x1,−x2,1)T, whose third component can never be zero. It therefore has no stationary point.

Removing the linear x3 term instead gives a family of saddle stationary points along the third coordinate.

For this ADALINE objective, the Hessian is 2R and is positive semidefinite. It cannot have a negative-curvature saddle. Positive definiteness gives a unique minimum; singularity allows flat directions.

Theorem 3.4.1: entries of a positive-definite matrix

For a symmetric positive-definite matrix A=[aij]:

(i) 𝐀−1 exists;(ii) aii>0 (i=1,…,n);(iii) maxi≠j|aij|≤maxi|aii|;(iv) aij2<aiiajj (i≠j).

All four statements are valid. In particular, (iv) follows from positivity of each 2×2 principal minor. It also implies (iii), since |aij|<aiiajj≤maxrarr. The source states a weak inequality in (iii), which remains true.

Leading principal submatrices and definiteness tests

The leading principal submatrix Ak consists of the first k rows and first k columns. The source uses the following criteria for symmetric matrices:

𝐀≻0⟺det(𝐀k)>0 for every k=1,…,n⟺all eigenvalues of 𝐀 are positive
𝐀⪰0⟺all eigenvalues of 𝐀 are nonnegative

The last equivalence is the positive-semidefinite criterion; the handout's phrase “semi positive definite” is standardized here to the usual “positive semidefinite.”

When a singular quadratic still has a minimum

A general semidefinite quadratic may lack a stationary point. Least squares has extra structure: Rx=h is consistent. Singular R removes the ordinary inverse and may remove uniqueness, but a minimum still exists.

Common mistakes

  • Do not threshold a before calculating the LMS residual. A class label may be produced afterward, but training uses e=t−a.
  • Do not reverse the residual sign without reversing the update direction.
  • Do not pair Lesson 3's half-squared loss update with Widrow–Hoff's factor-two rate bound.
  • Do not omit the appended constant when forming the augmented correlation matrix; that would leave bias out of the optimization.
  • Do not use R⁻¹h when R is singular.
  • Do not treat the expected quadratic rate bound as a guarantee that every stochastic sample update lowers the dataset error.

Practice questions

Why is the bias input appended as 1?

Check your answer

It makes the bias another weight in the dot product: [w;b]T[p;1]=wTp+b.

For the unhalved loss, why does the weight update contain 2?

Check your answer

Differentiating (t−wTp−b)2 gives the weight gradient −2ep. Gradient descent subtracts that gradient, producing +2αep.

What does singular R imply for the ordinary inverse solution?

Check your answer

R⁻¹h cannot be used. In an actual squared-error problem, solve Rx=h; solutions exist, but may not be unique because some input directions are redundant.

Does the rate bound promise that full-set MSE falls after every online presentation?

Check your answer

No. It describes the expected quadratic gradient dynamics under the stated assumptions. A stochastic sample update can temporarily increase error on the full dataset.
