Relations and matrix composition

A relation matrix gives grades to ordered pairs. Composition compares routes through a shared middle universe.

Find the strongest route through the middle

  • Axes first: R has rows X and columns Y; S has rows Y and columns Z. The result has rows X and columns Z.
  • Max–min: take the weakest link on each route, then choose the strongest route.
  • Max–product: multiply the two links on each route, then choose the largest product. This is not ordinary matrix multiplication.

Read the matrix axes first. Then follow routes through Y. Later sections check relation properties and show two ways to assign strengths from data.

Ordered products and crisp relation matrices

An ordered pair keeps its positions: (x,y) and (y,x) are generally different. X×Y contains every possible pair. A relation selects pairs from it; matrix rows follow X and columns follow Y.

X×Y={(x,y):x∈X, y∈Y},R⊆X×Y

For the lecture’s A = {0,1} and B = {a,b,c}, A × B lists (0,a), (0,b), (0,c), (1,a), (1,b), and (1,c). Reversing the universes gives B × A = {(a,0),(a,1),(b,0),(b,1),(c,0),(c,1)}. These are different ordered pairs. Also A² = A × A = {(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)}.

When X = {1,2,3} and Y = {a,b,c}, the unconstrained product has all nine pairs, represented by a 3 × 3 matrix of ones. By contrast, X = {1,2}, Y = {a,b}, and R = {(1,a),(2,b)} give the constrained matrix below.

The universal relation on A = {0,1,2} contains all nine pairs; the identity relation contains only the three diagonal pairs.

The continuous crisp example uses R = {(x,y) : y ≥ 2x}. Its characteristic function is 1 on or above the line y = 2x and 0 below it.

The source does not specify bounds for X or Y; this compact graph shows a viewing window only, while the relation itself extends across the continuous universes. The solid boundary is included because equality satisfies y ≥ 2x.

χR(x,y)={1,y≥2x0,y<2x

There are |X||Y| possible pairs and 2|X||Y| crisp relations. A constrained relation can select fewer pairs. Fuzzy matrices have infinitely many possible grade assignments because each entry varies in [0,1].

Operations on relations

Relation operations act on matching cells: max for union, min for intersection, 1−grade for complement and ≤ for containment. The null matrix is all zeros; the complete relation is all ones.

μR∪S(x,y)=max{μR(x,y),μS(x,y)}μR∩S(x,y)=min{μR(x,y),μS(x,y)}μR―(x,y)=1−μR(x,y)R⊆S⟺μR(x,y)≤μS(x,y)for every (x,y)

These pointwise definitions cover crisp relations when grades are restricted to 0 and 1, and fuzzy relations when grades may be anywhere in [0,1]. With max/min and the standard complement, commutativity, associativity, distributivity, involution, idempotency and De Morgan’s laws hold. Excluded middle and excluded contradiction hold for crisp relations but fail in general for fuzzy relations.

Composition through an intermediate universe

For T=R∘S, take one row of R and one column of S. Each shared y gives a two-link route. Combine the two links, then choose the maximum route value.

T=R∘S,μT(x,z)=maxy∈Ymin{μR(x,y),μS(y,z)}
μT(x,z)=maxy∈Y{μR(x,y)·μS(y,z)}(max–product)

For crisp entries, min behaves like AND and max like OR. For fuzzy max–product, multiply the links. These are different compositions, and composition is not commutative. Follow the displayed axes rather than relying on another book’s symbol order.

Fuzzy relations and fuzzy Cartesian products

A fuzzy relation maps each ordered pair in X × Y to a membership grade in [0,1]. A grade is a modeled strength of relation, not a probability. If the two universes are finite, display those grades in a matrix with the same row/column convention as for crisp relations.

μR:X×Y→[0,1]

Given fuzzy sets A on X and B on Y, their fuzzy Cartesian product defines a relation by taking the smaller membership of the two elements. This is the lecture’s min-based product.

Fuzzy union, intersection, complement and containment are the same pointwise max, min, one-minus, and ≤ operations already shown above. De Morgan’s principles continue to hold, while the excluded-middle identities generally do not.

μA×B(x,y)=min{μA(x),μB(y)}

Reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and tolerance

For a relation on one universe, test three matrix properties:

reflexive: μR(xi,xi)=1symmetric: μR(xi,xj)=μR(xj,xi)max–min transitive: μR(xi,xk)≥min{μR(xi,xj),μR(xj,xk)}

A crisp equivalence relation has all three properties. A tolerance (or proximity) relation needs only reflexivity and symmetry. Therefore the five-element handwritten example is a tolerance relation but not an equivalence relation:

The diagonal is 1 and the matrix mirrors across it. But x₁→x₂ and x₂→x₅ exist while x₁→x₅ does not. That missing shortcut breaks transitivity.

The three-element crisp example has the same pattern: 1→2→3 exists but 1→3 is absent. Max–min self-composition fills it.

Other composition operators listed in the lecture

The source lists five other composition rules without worked numerical examples. They remain reference definitions below. In max-average, average each input pair before taking max; in sum-product, add products before the specified range-limiting f.

min–max: μB(y)=minx∈Xmax{μA(x),μR(x,y)}max–max: μB(y)=maxx∈Xmax{μA(x),μR(x,y)}min–min: μB(y)=minx∈Xmin{μA(x),μR(x,y)}max-average: μB(y)=12maxx∈X[μA(x)+μR(x,y)]sum-product: μB(y)=f(∑x∈XμA(x)·μR(x,y))

These are the source’s forms and operator assumptions: max/min range over x, the average is one half of the maximum pairwise sum, and the final f(.) limits the sum-product result to [0,1] (the source mentions a sigmoid or step function). They are not interchangeable with the two principal matrix compositions above.

Common mistakes

  • Do not swap X × Y for Y × X without also changing ordered-pair meaning and matrix axes.
  • Do not treat every possible product pair as belonging to a constrained relation. nXnY counts product pairs; a particular R may be smaller.
  • Do not confuse the universal relation (all pairs) with the identity relation (diagonal pairs only).
  • Do not use ordinary arithmetic matrix multiplication for max–min or max–product composition. First combine each shared-y path, then maximize.
  • Do not reverse the lecture’s composition convention: T = R ◦ S uses R : X → Y followed by S : Y → Z and yields X rows by Z columns.
  • Reflexivity plus symmetry makes a tolerance relation, not necessarily an equivalence relation; transitivity is also required.
  • Fuzzy transitivity requires a direct grade at least as large as the minimum of the two path grades. It is not equality with that minimum.
  • The lecturer’s “max–min method” similarity ratio is a sum-of-minima divided by a sum-of-maxima; it is distinct from max–min composition.
  • Do not silently replace the source’s absolute cosine numerator or rewrite its max-average and sum-product definitions.

Exam focus

  • State the row/column universes and relation dimensions before multiplying relations.
  • For each output pair, list all intermediate-y routes and apply min then max, or product then max.
  • Check crisp/fuzzy relation operations pointwise; fuzzy grades remain in [0,1].
  • Test reflexivity on the diagonal, symmetry across it, and transitivity on two-step paths.
  • Remember: tolerance = reflexive + symmetric; equivalence = reflexive + symmetric + transitive.
  • A tolerance closure adds missing path relations; it generally differs from a nontransitive starting relation and stabilizes when self-composition no longer changes it.
  • Keep the full vector dot products, squared norms, and absolute value visible when calculating cosine amplitude.

Practice questions

For X = {a,b} and Y = {0,1}, does (a,0) have the same ordered-pair meaning as (0,a)?

Check your answer

No. The first pair belongs to X × Y; the second belongs to Y × X. Ordered-pair position matters.

For R = [[0.7,0.5]] and S = [[0.6],[0.7]], calculate the one-cell max–min and max–product compositions.

Check your answer

Max–min: max(min(0.7,0.6), min(0.5,0.7)) = max(0.6,0.5) = 0.6. Max–product: max(0.7×0.6, 0.5×0.7) = max(0.42,0.35) = 0.42.

A relation is reflexive and symmetric but fails transitivity. What kind of relation is it?

Check your answer

It is a tolerance (proximity) relation, not an equivalence relation.

In the fuzzy tolerance example, why does R²(1,3) become 0.4?

Check your answer

The route through 2 has strength min(R(1,2), R(2,3)) = min(0.8,0.4) = 0.4. The other route minima are 0, so their maximum is 0.4.

What does the product nX nY count, and what does 2^(nX nY) count?

Check your answer

nX nY counts all possible ordered pairs in X × Y. 2^(nX nY) counts all crisp relations, since each pair may be present or absent.
